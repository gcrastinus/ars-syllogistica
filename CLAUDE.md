# Wording rules for this course (from Timothy's own edits)

These rules come from the changes Timothy made by hand to the course text and from the corrections he has given. Follow them for any new or revised student-facing text. Change wording only. Do not change doctrine, citations, Latin or Greek, or which answer is correct.

Rules

1. Use the plain, common term, not a coined label or a literal translation. Write "the arrangement of words", not "the joining"; "art", not "road"; "self-expression", not "wanting a voice"; "course" or "set of exercises", not "app".
2. Say the literal thing. Cut metaphors, figures and jokes ("a fog", "a sticker on a box you have not opened", "fails on Tuesday", "a detour") and state what is meant.
3. Cut aphorisms, punchlines and setup lines ("Now the payoff.", "This is the heart of it.", "here is the point", "Heat is not a claim."). If the line has content, fold it into a full sentence.
4. Write full sentences and make the logical link explicit with so, but, because, therefore, that is. Turn fragments and "Label: fragment" answers into clauses.
5. Prefer accuracy to a neat claim. Add the qualification that makes the sentence true ("usually begins with a capital letter"; "fine when album is a substantive adjective").
6. Name things exactly and gloss a technical word in plain words where it first appears ("the base (the main line)"; "the classical trivium (which is the coordinated studies of grammar, logic, and rhetoric)").
7. Use "we", or let the tradition make the claim. Do not coach the student with "you". Soften imperatives ("Ideally, you would have a teacher", not "You should have a teacher").
8. Put questions in plain words ("Why does grammar come before logic?", not "Why master grammar before logic?").
9. Cut meta-talk about the page, the app or the scoring ("This page is an invitation and a map.", "— not scored").
10. Prefer the precise term to casual idiom ("depends on", not "leans on"; "subordinates", not "ties into"; "pronounced", not "sounded").
11. Give concrete examples, in full sentences where they help ("paint the wall" and "buy the paint"; "the man to whom I threw the ball"). Keep only the historical points that help the student.
12. Quote exactly as written. Never modernize or correct the spelling inside a quotation.
13. Use American spelling: license, meter, center, color.
14. Use few dashes. Use a comma, parentheses or a semicolon instead.

Examples (OLD → NEW)

1. The Joining → The Arrangement of Words
2. What a sentence is, before the joining is judged → What a sentence is, before we judge the arrangement of words
3. The departure is a fault in common speech and a licence in a poem → The departure is a fault in common speech, but it is permissible in a poem
4. Incongruous: the parts do not fit; grammar has failed → Incongruous: the parts do not fit with one another, so the construction fails
5. Mute: needs a vowel beside → A mute: it cannot be pronounced without a vowel
6. Why can a Sumerian word-sign mean, when a Latin letter does not? → Why does a Sumerian word-sign have a meaning, when a Latin letter does not?
7. Why does the indirect object leave the base line? → Why is the indirect object drawn under the base (the main line)?
8. Before logic, grammar. → Before logic we must study grammar.
9. Words have jobs. → The functions that words have.
10. A noun holds its thing still; a verb has it happening. → A noun refers to a thing, a stable entity under consideration; a verb is a doing, or a being done, or a becoming, or a being in a certain way.
11. Words that lean on other words. … An adjective … leans on a noun and tells what sort → Some words depend on other words. … An adjective … depends on a noun and tells what sort of thing the noun refers to
12. A noun … names … something you cannot touch — courage, Tuesday, justice. → A noun … names … something you cannot sense, like an idea or a property.
13. A proper noun names one particular thing and takes a capital letter → A proper noun names one particular thing and usually begins with a capital letter
14. Put one of each together and you have said something. This is the hinge of the whole art. … and — here is the point — it can be true or false. → Combine one noun and one verb and you have said something. This is the principle of the whole art. … and only a complete thought can be true or false.
15. Now the payoff. → (deleted)
16. The cost of treating words as secondary is not that science fails on Tuesday. → The cost of treating words as secondary is not that science fails to produce data or theories.
17. Grammar is the art of putting words together rightly. → Grammar is the art of speaking and writing correctly.
18. English and Latin run through every part of this course. → English and Latin are the primary languages used in every part of the course.
19. Those applications are not a substitute for reading; they are where the reading is tried. → Those applications do not replace study of the classic texts.
20. Human teaching and learning are fundamentally linguistic; and that is where we must begin. → …; and so that is where the tradition begins.
21. You should have a teacher to lead you through these studies → Ideally, you would have a teacher to lead you through these studies
22. You have been misunderstood. → We have all been misunderstood.
23. Data do not walk into a community; sentences do. → (deleted)
24. If you cannot say what the sentence was doing, the label is a sticker on a box you have not opened. → If you cannot say what the sentence was doing, the label of the fallacy is just a name for something we do not really understand.
25. What is true in wanting a voice / Why training the tongue is not later → What is true in starting from self-expression / Why training our speech is earlier than finding a voice

Search your new text for these before you push

- Coined labels or literal translations used as terms: "the joining", "road(s)", "joiners", "pointers", "a voice" (for self-expression), "the tongue" (for speech). Use the ordinary term.
- "app", "application", "walk-through" in student-facing text. Use "course" or "set of exercises".
- Metaphors and figures standing in for a claim: fog, sticker, machine, hinge, payoff, seed, detour, heat, "lean on", "hands you", "offstage", "tried", "buys you".
- Setup and reveal phrases: "here is the point", "here is the thing", "this is the heart of it", "now the payoff", "what this buys you", "worth carrying away", "worth naming now", "notice that", "hold this", "remember this".
- A colon used as a reveal or punchline, and "Label: fragment" answers with no verb. A colon that introduces a list, a gloss, or the direct answer to a stated question is fine.
- Fragments and short staccato sentences used for effect ("Before logic, grammar." "He has to." "It is not the default.").
- Aphoristic closers that restate a paragraph in a neat line.
- Meta-commentary about the page or the scoring: "this page…", "not scored", "a later page will…", "it will not open by…".
- Coaching "you" and imperatives: "you will meet", "look at", "hear it", "take…", "do not…". Use "we", the tradition, or a statement.
- Claims made neat by leaving out the qualification (check "always", "never", "only", "is incongruous", "is not").
- Heavy dashes.
- British spellings: licence, metre, centre, colour, -ise/-isation, programme.
- Any quotation that differs from the source text, even by one letter.
