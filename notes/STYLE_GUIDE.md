## Two registers

| | **Expository (default for all app text)** | **Elevated (rare)** |
|---|---|---|
| Where | articles, logic overview, nominalism, history of theology | *Creation and Cosmos*, *From Light into Light* |
| Sentences | long, connected, explanatory | strings of rhetorical questions; short emphatic lines ("And it is.") |
| Stance | measured; concedes, then argues | hortatory ("What we need are…", "our task is…") |
| Use in apps | **always** | at most a closing invitation; probably **none in Trivium** |

## Core traits of the default voice

1. **Full, connected sentences.** His expository sentences average about 25–30 words (*Logic* 29, *Nominalism* 26). He joins clauses with semicolons and *but / so / therefore*. Short sentences carry a definition or an answer ("Logic is the art of reasoning well."). He doesn't use them for punch. (The current Trivium pages average 18 words, and 1 sentence in 5 is 8 words or fewer.)
2. **Question, then answer.** He states the question outright and answers it: *"So, what are words? … Words, therefore, are signs of things. But what are signs?"* and *"The nominalist answer is this: …"*
3. **Explicit logical signposts.** He opens sentences with *So,* / *Now,* / *But*, and uses *therefore, however, thus, that is, for example, more precisely, in general, in so far as, properly speaking*, and enumerations (*First … Second …*). He shows the reasoning instead of leaving it implied.
4. **"We" for shared inquiry.** He writes with *we* and *our*, and uses *I* sparingly for his own judgments or plan ("Let me start by…", "I argue"). He almost never addresses *you*; it appears only in passing examples ("the elm tree behind your house"). He doesn't coach the reader.
5. **Names things exactly.** He names the subject outright (his edit put "the classical trivium (which is the coordinated studies of grammar, logic, and rhetoric)" in place of a vaguer phrase). He defines terms before using them and glosses Latin or Greek in parentheses ("(Latin: *res*)"). He states distinctions in pairs ("primarily an art … but also a science"), and refers to *St. Thomas / Aquinas* and *the classical tradition*. A lesser-known person gets an appositive at first mention ("Quintilian, the Roman teacher of rhetoric of the first century A.D."); never "Dr."
6. **Measured concession instead of hype.** He grants what is true before arguing ("(which is true) … (which is also true)") and admits difficulty ("This is a counterintuitive claim in our time; but…"). He uses *seems* honestly. The tradition is the subject of the claim, not a "we must" ("that is where the tradition begins," not "where we must begin"). He qualifies historical claims by period, keeping the qualifier outside the quotation marks: Aristotle says delivery "has not yet been treated of by anyone" in his time.
7. **Punctuation.** He uses many semicolons and parentheses (for glosses, asides, concessions), colons to introduce an answer or a list, and *that is* / *e.g.* / *i.e.*. He uses **very few dashes** (about 0.6 per 1,000 words, against 3.3 in the current app text).
8. **Plain, concrete examples, introduced as such.** Examples include slime molds, *cat* and *billee*, an association of lawyers, and a ball on a plane. He introduces them with *for example*, *to take the example of*, *compare X to Y*, or *consider*. In teaching the arts, he prefers general, classical, or dignified examples to modern domestic vignettes (see the Ars Rhetorica edits below).
9. **What he cuts.** He cuts aphorisms and pithy closing lines ("they are where the reading is tried"), filler figures of speech ("a detour"), and vague institutional labels ("modern schooling" became "contemporary studies"). He also softens imperatives ("You should have a teacher" became "Ideally, you would have a teacher"), and pares wordy phrasing to the plain form ("until he knows what he needs to say", "takes the most time", "treated here only briefly and in certain respects", "passions of the audience").

## What his edits show

- **6 Oct (welcome page):** He prefers precise naming over a general gesture ("understand the foundations of the classical trivium"). He uses an academic register ("contemporary studies", "mathematical representations"). The tradition carries the claim ("the tradition begins"). He cut the epigram ("they are where the reading is tried" became "do not replace study of the classic texts"), cut the figure "a detour", and made the scope explicit ("at the end *of this introduction*").
- **24 Sep (welcome page):** He turned a tacked-on "ideally" into a full statement ("That is the ideal.") and softened a "should".
- **4 Oct (six pages):** These are mainly doctrinal precision, and it isn't certain they were all in his own hand. Examples: "to a mind" became "to a knowing power"; "a concept" became "a concept, the formal sign proper"; and "taken as what is known" and "second intentions" were added. The lesson: when accuracy and smoothness conflict, he keeps the technical term and adds the qualification.

- **19–20 Oct 2026 (Ars Rhetorica tutorial cards).** Line-by-line edits to the opening tutorial cards. Each is generalized below as a rule; points already covered above are cross-referenced rather than repeated.
  1. **Open with the reason, not an everyday hook.** A card that answers a question begins with the argued answer. *Before:* "We all persuade and are persuaded every day. A parent persuades a child that it is time for bed…" *After:* "Since we can do the same thing well or badly, and since we can teach both the reason why one person is able to persuade another and also why someone else may try to persuade but fail, persuasion is not only a natural gift but also an art." (Extends traits 2 and 9.)
  2. **General, classical, or dignified examples instead of modern domestic vignettes.** *Before:* "A doctor persuades us about our health, and an engineer persuades us that a bridge will or will not hold"; "Someone asked to give a toast at a wedding…"; memory by errands through a house. *After:* "Some professions speak chiefly about the things within their own expertise… But others, such as a lawyer before a jury, a legislator, a bishop, or a member of a town council, must consider and debate many subjects"; "A speaker always has several tasks"; "a great hall with rooms and busts at the entrance of each room," or steps up to a temple. (Qualifies trait 8.)
  3. **State the difficulty plainly instead of setting a scene.** *Before:* "A good speech read badly, in a flat voice and with the eyes on the page, can fail where a weaker speech said well succeeds." *After:* "It is not always easy to memorize or prepare a speech and to know how best to deliver it, what pace to speak at, what pauses to make, and so on. This is why memory and delivery are true parts of the art…"
  4. **"Audience," not "hearers."** *Before:* "rhetoric addresses a whole body of hearers". *After:* "rhetoric addresses a whole audience". (Quotations keep their own wording.)
  5. **Name what the tradition does, in its own terms.** *Before:* "teaches a trained memory of this kind". *After:* "renders memory itself as an art". (Extends trait 5.)
  6. **State the course's purpose and place it in the tradition.** Where a card explains why the course is as it is, say what the goal is and is not, on named authority. *After:* "Our goal, following St. Augustine and Cicero, is not mastery of the rules of rhetoric or the history of rhetoric…; our goal is learning the art itself, primarily through the study and imitation of great writers." Acknowledge the tradition's depth when giving only its core: "the doctrine of classical rhetoric, which over the centuries of antiquity became rich and sophisticated."
  7. **Instructional text: mostly plain commands, with "we" only sometimes.** *Illustration (not from the diffs):* "Name the part of the art at work in each passage," not "Let us now name…" every time; use "we" only occasionally. (Exception to the coaching-imperatives tic below.)
  8. **Folded into existing rules:** the appositive at first mention ("Quintilian, the Roman teacher of rhetoric of the first century A.D.") under trait 5; the historical qualifier outside the quotation ("has not yet been treated of by anyone" in his time) under trait 6; pared phrasing ("what he needs to say", "takes the most time", "in certain respects", "passions of the audience") under trait 9; the argument's sequence, not the interface ("the next card shows why" became "we next consider why") under *Student-facing wording*; bold only the term ("**Invention** or discovery (*inventio*)") under *Formatting*.
  9. **Standing rules confirmed:** American spelling; words mentioned as words in double quotes; never "Dr."

## AI-style tics to remove (from the *AI-style language cleanup* workflow, extended)

- **Coaching imperatives and second person** ("Look at…", "Start with…", "here is the sentence to hold on to"). Recast them as statements or as *we* / *let us* / *consider*. (Exception: exercise instructions are mostly plain commands, with *we* only sometimes; see the Ars Rhetorica edits below.)
- **Aphoristic closers and fragments** ("Three parts, and all three matter." / "Nobody is shut in anywhere." / "The sounds are local. What the sounds are for is not.").
- **Staccato runs of short sentences**, and **dramatic reveals** ("Something stands between… and it is doing work.").
- **Filler, hype, stock transitions:** *delve*, *it's important to note*, *crucially*, *not just X but Y*, reflexive lists of three.
- **Heavy dashes.** Use commas, parentheses, or a semicolon.
- **Casual idiom in place of the technical term** ("glued to a thing", "the causing runs", "lying about"). Use the proper term, with a short gloss if needed.
- **Keep exactly:** his terminology, Latin, citations, quotations, links, and meaning. Add no content.
- **Student-facing wording:** "sets of exercises," never "apps." Card text refers to the argument's sequence, not the interface ("we next consider why," not "the next card shows why"). (The Trivium welcome page still says "The three practice apps" and "live applications"; flag this for his decision.)
- **Report format:** OLD → NEW pairs unless he asks for the edits to be applied.

## Formatting

- **Lists set off from the lead-in (Oct 19, 2026).** A list set out on separate lines after a colon is visually set off from its lead-in paragraph: the lead-in ends at the colon and closes its paragraph, and the list begins a new block (in HTML, a new `<p>` with items separated by `<br>`). Never run the list on directly from the colon inside the same paragraph.

- **Bold only the term (Oct 19, 2026).** When a term is defined, bold the term itself and leave glosses and the Latin outside the bold: "**Invention** or discovery (*inventio*)", not "**Invention or discovery**".

## Revision checklist

1. Read the whole page first.
2. Turn *you* into *we* (keep an occasional incidental *your* in an example).
3. Merge fragments and short runs into connected sentences, making the logical link explicit (*so, therefore, but, that is*).
4. State any question the paragraph answers, then answer it.
5. Cut the epigram at the end of each paragraph, or fold its point into the preceding sentence.
6. Replace dashes and idioms. Keep or restore the technical term.
7. Check that every claim, example, heading, citation, and quotation is unchanged in substance.
