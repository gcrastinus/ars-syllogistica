## The fifteen lead-ins, in full

### I. The Predicables (3 cards)

**Card 1.**

**The five predicables.** Porphyry, a third-century Neoplatonist, set out in his introduction to Aristotle’s *Categories* the five ways in which a universal term can be said of a subject: genus, species, difference, property, and accident. Each question asks which of these a predicate is in relation to its subject. The same predicate can be a different predicable of a different subject, so the question always concerns this predicate said of this subject.

The **genus** is said of many things that differ in species, and it answers the question “What is it?” with the wider nature, as “animal” is said of man and of the horse. The **species** is said of many things that differ only in number, that is, of individuals, and it answers the same question completely: Socrates is a man, and Fido is a dog. The **difference** (differentia) divides the genus and makes the species; it answers the question “What sort of thing is it, in its very nature?” (*quale quid*), as “rational” divides animal and makes man.

The **property** (proprium) is no part of what the thing is, but it follows from what the thing is, and it belongs to every member of the species, to that species alone, and always; so the power to laugh is a property of man. The **accident** is what a subject may have or lack while it remains the same thing, as Socrates may be pale or tanned. An accident is either separable, as sitting is, or inseparable, as the blackness of a raven is; but even an inseparable accident is no part of what the subject is, since being a raven does not consist in being black.

*Sources line:* Timothy Kearns, “Logic (General and Historical),” *Encyclopedia of Catholic Theology* (ect.org, 2025); John A. Oesterle, *Logic: The Art of Defining and Reasoning*, 2nd ed. (Prentice-Hall, 1963).

*Check questions (one drawn at random; a wrong answer moves to another):*

1. In “Socrates is a man,” which predicable is “man”? Options: **Species** (correct); Genus; Difference; Accident. Explanation: “Man” is said of Socrates as his species, the kind said of individuals that differ only in number. Wrong-answer note: The genus would be a wider kind, such as “animal”; the difference would be “rational”; and an accident can come and go while Socrates remains.
2. Which question does the difference answer? Options: **“What sort of thing is it, in its very nature?”** (correct); “What is it?” as to the wider kind; “How much of it is there?”; “What merely happens to be true of it?”. Explanation: The genus answers “What is it?” with the wider nature; the difference tells what sort of thing it is in its nature, as “rational” tells what sort of animal a man is. Wrong-answer note: The wider kind is the genus, quantity is a category and not a predicable, and what merely happens to be true is an accident.
3. Every raven is black. Which predicable is “black” of the raven? Options: **An inseparable accident** (correct); A property; The difference; The genus. Explanation: Blackness never leaves the raven, but being a raven does not consist in being black, and much that is black is not a raven. So it is an accident, though an inseparable one. Wrong-answer note: A property belongs to its species alone, and the difference and the genus are part of what the thing is; blackness is neither.

**Card 2.**

**Two questions, and a test.** Two questions sort four of the predicables: is the predicate part of what the subject is, and is it said of that subject alone? If it is part of what the subject is and is said of more than the subject, it is the genus, as “figure” is said of the triangle. If it is part of what the subject is and is said of it alone, it is the difference (“three-sided”). If it is not part of what the subject is, yet is said of it alone, of all of it, and always, it is the property (“having its angles equal to two right angles”). If it is not part of what the subject is and is not said of it alone, it is an accident (“drawn in chalk”).

A property converts with its species; that is, subject and predicate can exchange places and both statements remain true. Every man is able to laugh, and whatever is able to laugh is a man. A genus does not convert: every man is an animal, but not every animal is a man. Nor does an accident, even one that never leaves its subject: every raven is black, but much that is black is not a raven. Porphyry gives several senses of “property,” and the exercise uses the strict one, in which a property belongs to the whole species, to it alone, and always.

A property is often a power, so a small change of wording changes the predicable. A man is always able to laugh, though he is not always laughing; so “Man is able to laugh” gives the property, and “This man is laughing” gives an accident. In the same way, being able to learn grammar is a property of man, but knowing grammar is an accident of this boy, since knowledge is gained and can be lost.

*Check questions (one drawn at random; a wrong answer moves to another):*

1. “A triangle is a figure.” How do the two questions sort the predicate? Options: **It is part of what a triangle is, and is said of more than triangles, so it is the genus** (correct); It is part of what a triangle is, and is said of triangles alone, so it is the difference; It is not part of what a triangle is, yet is said of triangles alone, so it is the property; It is not part of what a triangle is, and is not said of triangles alone, so it is an accident. Explanation: To be a triangle is to be a figure, but squares and circles are figures too; so “figure” is the genus. Wrong-answer note: “Three-sided” would be the difference, and “having its angles equal to two right angles” the property.
2. Which of these gives the property of man? Options: **“Man is able to laugh”** (correct); “This man is laughing”; “Man is an animal”; “Man is rational”. Explanation: The power to laugh belongs to every man, to man alone, and always, and it follows from what man is without being part of it. Wrong-answer note: The act of laughing comes and goes, so it is an accident; “animal” is the genus, and “rational” the difference.
3. Every man is an animal. Is every animal a man? Options: **No, so “animal” does not convert with “man,” and it is the genus** (correct); Yes, so “animal” is a property of man; No, so “animal” is an accident of man; Yes, so “animal” is the difference of man. Explanation: A genus is said of more than any one species under it, and so it does not convert with the species. Wrong-answer note: Not every animal is a man, so it does not convert; and “animal” is part of what man is, so it is not an accident.

**Card 3.**

**Genus and species are relative.** In the tree of Porphyry, which arranges the kinds from the highest genus down to the individual, a kind in the middle is a genus in relation to what is below it and a species in relation to what is above it: “animal” is a genus in relation to man, and a species in relation to living body. A remote genus is still a genus, so “An oak is a living thing” gives a genus of the oak (an oak is a tree, a tree a plant, and a plant a living thing). The highest genus in that tree is substance, which is never a species; each of the ten categories is a highest genus in its own line, and being itself is not a genus. The lowest kind, such as man, is always a species and never a genus. An individual such as Socrates is neither, because he is said of nothing else; things are only said of him.

The predicables are not the same as the categories. The categories sort what things are, as we first know them (first intentions); the predicables are relations that natures have only because they are known (second intentions), though these relations are grounded in what the things are. So in “Man is a species,” the term “man” does not stand for any man, since no man is a species; it stands for the nature as known. What a term stands for in a proposition is called its supposition, and in this sentence the supposition of “man” is simple.

*Check questions (one drawn at random; a wrong answer moves to another):*

1. Is “animal” a genus or a species? Options: **Both, since it is a genus in relation to man and a species in relation to living body** (correct); A genus only, never a species; A species only, never a genus; Neither, since it is an individual. Explanation: In the tree of Porphyry a kind in the middle is a genus to what lies below it and a species to what lies above it. Wrong-answer note: Only the highest genus is never a species, only the lowest kind is never a genus, and an individual is neither.
2. In “Man is a species,” what does “man” stand for? Options: **The nature as known, not any man** (correct); Each particular man; The word “man” itself; Nothing at all. Explanation: No man is a species, so the term stands for the nature as known; its supposition here is simple. Wrong-answer note: If it stood for particular men the sentence would be false; and it is not about the word, as “‘Man’ has three letters” would be.
3. How do the predicables differ from the categories? Options: **The predicables are relations natures have only as known (second intentions); the categories sort what things are (first intentions)** (correct); The categories are relations in the mind; the predicables are things outside it; They are two names for the same ten kinds; The predicables are mere names, with no basis in things. Explanation: Genus, species, and the rest are relations a nature has because it is known, though grounded in what things are; the categories sort the things themselves as we first know them. Wrong-answer note: The order is the reverse of the second option; there are five predicables and ten categories; and the predicables are grounded in things, not mere names.

### II. The Categories (3 cards)

**Card 1.**

**Substance and the nine accidents.** Aristotle sets out ten categories, the highest kinds under which anything said of a thing falls: substance, quantity, quality, relation, action, passion, when, where, posture, and having. Each question asks under which category a predicate falls. **Substance** is what exists in its own right and not in another. The other nine are accidents, which exist only in a substance or affect it, and so every other category depends on substance.

Aristotle distinguishes two senses of substance (*Categories* 5). Primary substance is the individual, such as this man or this horse, and it is neither said of a subject nor present in one. Secondary substance is the species or genus to which the individual belongs, such as man or animal, and it is said of a subject but is present in none, since “man” is said of Socrates. The mark most proper to substance is that one and the same substance can receive contraries through its own change: the same man is pale at one time and dark at another, while one and the same color cannot be both white and black. Substance itself has no contrary, and it does not admit of more and less.

*Sources line:* Timothy Kearns, “Logic (General and Historical),” *Encyclopedia of Catholic Theology* (ect.org, 2025); John A. Oesterle, *Logic: The Art of Defining and Reasoning*, 2nd ed. (Prentice-Hall, 1963); St. Thomas Aquinas, commentary on the *Metaphysics*, bk. V, lect. 9; Aristotle, *Categories* 5.

*Check questions (one drawn at random; a wrong answer moves to another):*

Explanation: Substance alone exists in its own right; the other nine are accidents, which exist only in a substance or affect it. Wrong-answer note: Quality, quantity, and relation are themselves accidents of a substance.
2. How does secondary substance (man, animal) differ from primary substance (this man)? Options: **It is said of a subject, though it is present in none** (correct); It is present in a subject, as an accident is; It is neither said of a subject nor present in one; It is an accident of the individual. Explanation: “Man” is said of Socrates, but it is not present in him as whiteness is (*Categories* 5). Wrong-answer note: Being neither said of a subject nor present in one is the mark of primary substance; and secondary substance is not an accident.
3. What is the mark most proper to substance? Options: **One and the same substance can receive contraries** (correct); It has a contrary, as hot has cold; It admits of more and less, as white does; It is always present in a subject. Explanation: The same man is pale at one time and dark at another, but one and the same color cannot be both white and black. Wrong-answer note: Substance has no contrary and admits of no degrees, and it is never present in a subject.

**Card 2.**

**How the nine follow from substance.** St. Thomas explains how the nine accidents follow from the different ways they relate to substance (commentary on the *Metaphysics*, bk. V, lect. 9). Some accidents inhere in the substance. What inheres in it absolutely follows either from its matter, and this is **quantity** (“two feet long,” “the number ten”), or from its form, and this is **quality** (“white,” “hot,” “just”). Quantity is either discrete, as a number is, or continuous, as a line or a surface is. What inheres in the substance relatively, as a bearing toward something else, is **relation** (“double,” “half,” “a master”).

Other accidents affect the substance. What affects it by being in it is **action**, what the substance does to another (“cutting,” “burning”), and **passion**, what is done to it (“being cut,” “being burnt”). What affects it from outside either measures it or does not. It measures it in time, and this is **when** (“yesterday,” “now”), or in position; and position has two aspects, **where**, the place the substance is in (“in the marketplace”), and **posture**, the arrangement of its parts in that place (“he sits,” “he lies”). What affects it from outside without measuring it, as something it has on it, is **having**, also called habit (“he is shod,” “he is armed”).

Explanation: It is something the man has on him, which affects him from outside without measuring him. Wrong-answer note: Posture is the arrangement of his parts, where is his place, and action is something he does to another.

**Card 3.**

**Distinctions that are easily missed.** Knowledge and virtue, taken as lasting states of the soul, are habits in the sense of a lasting quality, the first species of quality. This is not the category of having, which concerns what a thing has on it, such as shoes or armor. (Knowledge spoken of as knowledge of the knowable is also relative.) Posture differs from where, because where is the place itself, while posture is the arrangement of the parts in that place; and the posture taken up (“he sits”) is not an action going on. Relation is the category whose whole being is a bearing toward another, so that double cannot be understood without half, nor a master without a servant.

Finally, the categories differ from the predicables. The ten categories sort what things are, as we first know them (first intentions). The five predicables are relations that natures have only because they are known (second intentions), though these relations are grounded in what the things are.

### III. Division (2 cards)

**Card 1.**

**What division is, and its kinds.** John of St. Thomas, a seventeenth-century Dominican commentator on St. Thomas, defines division as “a statement that distributes a thing into its members or a term into its meanings” (*Summulae*, bk. II, ch. 4). Each question gives divisions of a whole into its members and asks which keeps or breaks a rule, or which kind of division one of them is. Division leads to definition, since a genus is divided by differences until a single species is reached.

Because there are different kinds of whole, there are different kinds of division, and the exercise names four. A **logical division** divides a genus into its species, which are marked off from one another by opposed differences, as animals are divided into the rational and the irrational, or musical instruments into strings, winds, and percussion. An **integral division** divides a whole into the parts that make it up, and none of these parts is called by the name of the whole; so a book is divided into its chapters, and no chapter is a book. A **division by powers** divides a soul, or one of its powers, according to what it is able to do, as the soul is divided into its power to know and its power to desire, or prudence into taking counsel, judging, and commanding. An **accidental division** sorts a subject by features it can gain or lose without becoming a different thing, as apples are divided into the red and the green.

Logical division is not the same as **essential division**, which divides an essence into its parts, as man is divided into body and soul, or into animal and rational. A division by powers is not an integral division either: a paw is a bodily part, not a power of the soul.

Wade (Marquette University Press, 1955); John A. Oesterle, *Logic: The Art of Defining and Reasoning*, 2nd ed. (Prentice-Hall, 1963).

Explanation: Body and soul are the parts that make up man’s essence; neither is a species of man. Wrong-answer note: A logical division would give kinds of which the whole is said, and body is not a kind of man.

Options: **It divides a whole into the parts that make it up, and no part is called a house** (correct); It divides a genus into its species; It sorts houses by features they can gain or lose; It divides the house according to what it can do. Explanation: The parts together make up the house, and none of them is itself a house. Wrong-answer note: A wall is not a kind of house, a feature gained or lost, or a power.

**Card 2.**

**The rules of division.** Whatever its kind, a sound division keeps four rules. First, the members taken together must cover the whole, leaving nothing out; otherwise the division is incomplete, as when animals are divided into mammals and birds, and the fish are left out. Second, the members must not overlap, so that nothing falls under two of them at once. Third, every member must belong to the whole being divided, since a member that lies outside the whole does not divide it; so a tree is not divided into root, trunk, and the soil, because the soil is no part of the tree. Fourth, the division must be made on a single basis: to divide shoes into leather, canvas, and left-footed is to sort by material and by foot at once, and so the members cut across one another.

A division can break more than one rule at once, and then each true diagnosis is correct. So in judging a division we ask four questions in turn: does anything belonging to the whole fall under none of the members; does anything fall under two of them; is any member outside the whole; and are the members sorted on one basis?

Wrong-answer note: The fault is the mixed basis, which makes a left-footed leather shoe fall under two members.

Wrong-answer note: The even and the odd neither overlap nor leave any number out; the fault is the member that does not belong.

### IV. Definition (2 cards)

**Card 1.**

**The four kinds of definition.** A definition, as John of St. Thomas, a seventeenth-century Dominican commentator on St. Thomas, puts it, is “a statement that sets forth the essence of a thing or the meaning of a term” (*Summulae*, bk. II, ch. 3). Each question gives definitions and asks which keeps or breaks a rule, or which kind of definition one of them is. The tradition distinguishes four kinds.

The **nominal** definition gives what the name means, as “philosopher” means a lover of wisdom. Aristotle puts it first, because we cannot ask what a thing is until we know what its name refers to. The **essential** definition says what the thing is, by its nearest genus and the difference that marks it off from everything else in that genus: man is a rational animal. The **descriptive** definition picks the thing out by a property or by a telltale feature, without saying what it is: man is the animal that can laugh, and the bee is the insect that makes honey. The **causal** definition identifies the thing through its outside causes, that is, by what made it or by what it is for: a clock is an instrument made to measure time, and a scar is the mark left by a healed wound.

A definition by matter and form is not a causal definition, although matter and form are causes, because they are inside the thing rather than outside it. John of St. Thomas counts it as a kind of essential definition, which he calls the physical definition.

Imelda Choquette (Sheed & Ward, 1937).

Explanation: It defines through the end for which the saw is made, which is an outside cause. Wrong-answer note: It does not explain a word, give the nearest genus and specific difference, or point the thing out by a telltale feature.
3. “The magnet is the stone that draws iron.” What kind of definition is this? Options: **Descriptive** (correct); Essential; Causal; Nominal. Explanation: An effect peculiar to the magnet points it out without saying what it is. Wrong-answer note: It does not say what the magnet is, name what made it or what it is for, or explain the word.

**Card 2.**

**The rules of definition.** Whatever its kind, a good definition keeps these rules. It must fit exactly what it defines, being neither too broad nor too narrow; that is, it must be convertible with the thing defined, true of exactly the things the term is true of. So “A triangle is a figure” is too broad, and “A triangle is a figure with three equal sides” is too narrow. It must be clearer than the thing defined, and so it may not use a metaphor: “A triangle is geometry’s arrowhead” pleases but explains nothing. It must not contain the term being defined, since nothing is made clearer by itself, and so “A triangle is a triangular figure” is circular.

A definition should also say what a thing is rather than what it is not, wherever a positive account is available; “A line is length without breadth” is allowed, because no positive account is at hand. And it should be brief, giving the nearest genus and the difference and nothing more, so that “A triangle is a three-sided figure drawn with a ruler” and “Man is a rational animal able to laugh” both carry wasted words. “A triangle is a three-sided figure” keeps every rule.

Wrong-answer note: A definition is a phrase, not a single word, and the fault is circularity, not brevity.

### VI. Immediate Inference (2 cards)

**Card 1.**

**The square of opposition.** An immediate inference draws one proposition directly from another, with no middle term and no second premise. Some questions suppose that one proposition is true or false and ask whether a second, with the same terms, is true, false, or undetermined; the square of opposition answers them. **Contradictories** (A and O, E and I) differ in both quantity and quality, and they always take opposite truth values: if “All S are P” is true, “Some S are not P” is false, and if it is false, “Some S are not P” is true. **Contraries** (A and E) cannot both be true, but they can both be false; so if one is true the other is false, but if one is false the other is undetermined. **Subcontraries** (I and O) cannot both be false, but they can both be true; so if one is false the other is true, but if one is true the other is undetermined.

In **subalternation** (A and I, E and O), truth descends from the universal to the particular, and falsity ascends from the particular to the universal. If “All S are P” is true, “Some S are P” is true, because an affirmative carries existential import and so supplies the existence that “some” requires; if “Some S are P” is false, “All S are P” is false. But a false universal leaves its particular undetermined, and a true particular leaves its universal undetermined. Wherever the square forbids nothing, the answer is “undetermined.” The Square of Opposition, in the Study section, sets out these relations for any pair of terms.

*Sources line:* Timothy Kearns, “Logic (General and Historical),” *Encyclopedia of Catholic Theology* (ect.org, 2025); Jacques Maritain, *Formal Logic*, trans. Imelda Choquette (Sheed & Ward, 1937).

*Check questions (one drawn at random; a wrong answer moves to another):*

**Card 2.**

**Converse, obverse, contrapositive, contradictory.** Other questions give one proposition and ask for one of these, typed in standard form, or “none” when the operation cannot be performed. The **contradictory** changes both quantity and quality: the contradictory of A is O, of E is I, of I is E, and of O is A. **Conversion** exchanges subject and predicate. E and I convert simply, so “No S are P” gives “No P are S,” and “Some S are P” gives “Some P are S.” A converts only by limitation (per accidens), to I: “All S are P” gives only “Some P are S,” because the predicate of an affirmative is not distributed. O has no converse.

**Obversion** changes the quality and replaces the predicate by its complement, written with “non-”: “All S are P” gives “No S are non-P,” “No S are P” gives “All S are non-P,” “Some S are P” gives “Some S are not non-P,” and “Some S are not P” gives “Some S are non-P.” The complement must be the contradictory term (“non-just”), not the contrary (“unjust”), since a man may be neither just nor unjust. **Contraposition** obverts, then converts, then obverts again, so that both terms are replaced by their complements and exchange places. “All S are P” gives “All non-P are non-S,” and “Some S are not P” gives “Some non-P are not non-S.” E contraposes only by limitation, to “Some non-P are not non-S,” and I has no contrapositive, because its obverse is an O, and an O does not convert.

Explanation: A converts only by limitation, since its predicate is not distributed. Wrong-answer note: “All P are S” is the illicit conversion; and only O lacks a converse.
2. What is the obverse of “No S are P”? Options: **“All S are non-P”** (correct); “No non-S are P”; “All S are P”; “Some S are non-P”. Explanation: Obversion changes the quality (E becomes A) and replaces the predicate by its complement. Wrong-answer note: Obversion keeps the subject and the quantity, and it must both change the quality and negate the predicate.
3. What is the contrapositive of “Some S are P”? Options: “Some non-P are non-S”; “Some P are S”; “No non-P are non-S”; **None, since an I proposition has no contrapositive** (correct). Explanation: Its obverse is “Some S are not non-P,” an O, and an O does not convert, so the contrapositive cannot be formed. Wrong-answer note: “Some P are S” is the converse; and no proposition with the terms exchanged and complemented follows from an I.

### VII. The Modal Propositions (2 cards)

**Card 1.**

**Mode and dictum.** A modal proposition says not only that the predicate belongs to the subject but how it belongs. John of St. Thomas, a seventeenth-century Dominican commentator on St. Thomas, counts four modes: necessary, impossible, possible, and contingent, and in this treatise he takes “contingent” in the same sense as “possible” (*Summulae*, bk. II, ch. 21). A modal proposition has two parts, the **dictum**, which is what is said (“that Peter is running”), and the **mode**, which is said of the dictum (“it is possible”). Necessary and impossible are universal in force, like “every” and “no”; possible and possible not are particular, like “some” and “some not.” So every modal expression comes to one of four corners: *necessary* (it cannot be false), *impossible* (it cannot be true, which is the same as “necessary not”), *possible* (it can be true), and *possible not* (it can be false).

Two modal expressions are **equipollent** (equal in force) when they come to the same corner. A “not” placed before the mode moves the expression to the contradictory corner: “not possible” is “impossible,” and “not necessary” is only “possible not.” A “not” placed after the mode denies only the dictum: “possible that he is not” is “possible not,” and “necessary that he is not” is “impossible.” So “It is not possible that Peter is not running” comes to “It is necessary that Peter is running.” The commonest error is to read “not necessary” as “impossible,” but denying the stronger mode grants only the weaker denial. The scholastics set out these equipollences under the words Amabimus, Edentuli, Iliace, Purpurea.

*Sources line:* Timothy Kearns, “Logic (General and Historical),” *Encyclopedia of Catholic Theology* (ect.org, 2025); John of St. Thomas, *Outlines of Formal Logic*, trans. Francis C. Wade (Marquette University Press, 1955).

Explanation: A “not” before the mode moves the expression to the contradictory corner, and the contradictory of possible is impossible. Wrong-answer note: “Possible not” would need the “not” after the mode; and denying possibility cannot make a thing necessary or possible.

Explanation: Denying the stronger mode grants only the weaker denial; what is not necessary may still be possible. Wrong-answer note: Reading “not necessary” as “impossible” is the commonest modal error, and a denial cannot come to “necessary.”
3. In “It is possible that Peter is running,” what is the dictum? Options: **“that Peter is running”** (correct); “possible”; “Peter”; “It is possible”. Explanation: The dictum is what is said, and the mode is said of it. Wrong-answer note: “Possible” and “It is possible” give the mode; “Peter” is only the subject of the dictum.

**Card 2.**

**The modal square.** The four corners stand on a square, as A, E, I, and O do. Necessary and impossible are **contraries**: they cannot both be true, but both are false of whatever is contingent. Possible and possible not are **subcontraries**: they cannot both be false, but both are true of whatever is contingent. Necessary and possible not are **contradictories**, and so are impossible and possible; they always take opposite truth values. From the necessary to the possible the inference holds (*ab necesse ad posse valet consequentia*), and likewise from the impossible to the possible not; so truth descends from the stronger mode to the weaker. From the possible to the necessary it does not hold (*a posse ad necesse non valet consequentia*), and only falsity ascends from the weaker mode to the stronger.

**The composite and the divided sense.** Other questions ask whether a modal sentence is true in the composite sense, in the divided sense, in both, or in neither. In the composite sense (*in sensu composito*) the mode governs the whole dictum, its parts taken together at once: “It is possible that the seated man walks” asks whether sitting and walking can be joined at once, and so it is false. In the divided sense (*in sensu diviso*) the mode is said of the subject alone: “The seated man can walk” says that the man who is seated has the power to walk, and this is true, since sitting does not take the power away. In the same way “The one who is seated is necessarily seated” is true in the composite sense and false in the divided sense. Some sentences are true in both senses, as “A man is necessarily an animal,” where the predicate belongs by essence. Some are true in neither, as “The blind man can see,” since blindness is a privation that removes the power itself, and “A man can be a stone,” since the essence of man excludes it.

Explanation: Necessary and impossible are contraries; both are false of whatever is contingent, so the falsity of one settles nothing. Wrong-answer note: The contradictory of “necessary” is “possible not,” not “impossible.”
2. If “It is necessary that s is P” is true, what of “It is possible that s is P”? Options: **True** (correct); False; Undetermined. Explanation: From the necessary to the possible the inference holds, so truth descends. Wrong-answer note: What must be so can be so; only the step from possible to necessary fails.
3. “The man who is seated can walk.” In which sense is it true? Options: **In the divided sense only** (correct); In the composite sense only; In both senses; In neither sense. Explanation: Sitting and walking cannot be joined at once, so it is false in the composite sense; but the seated man keeps the power to walk, so it is true in the divided sense. Wrong-answer note: The composite reading joins sitting and walking at once, which is impossible; and the power to walk is not taken away by sitting.

### XII. The Conjunctive and Disjunctive Syllogism (2 cards)

**Card 1.**

**Arguments from a compound premise.** The conjunctive and the disjunctive syllogism are built not on terms but on whole propositions joined by a connective. The major premise is compound; the minor premise posits one member (asserts it) or removes it (denies it); and the conclusion posits or removes the other member. Each question asks whether the conclusion follows.

The **conjunctive** premise says “not both”: it denies that two propositions are true together, as in “No one can serve both God and Mammon.” Positing one member removes the other: “Judas serves Mammon; therefore he does not serve God.” This is the positing-removing mood (*ponendo tollens*), and it is the only lawful mood of the conjunctive. Removing one member posits nothing, because both members may be false together; so from “He does not serve Mammon” it does not follow that he serves God. To argue so is the fallacy of the removing-positing mood (*tollendo ponens*).

The **disjunctive** premise says “either … or,” and it asserts that at least one member is true. So removing one member posits the other: “The number is either odd or even; it is not odd; therefore it is even.” This removing-positing mood is the proper mood of the disjunctive, and it holds for every disjunction.

*Sources line:* Timothy Kearns, “Logic (General and Historical),” *Encyclopedia of Catholic Theology* (ect.org, 2025); Jacques Maritain, *Formal Logic*, trans. Imelda Choquette (Sheed & Ward, 1937); John of St. Thomas, *Outlines of Formal Logic*, trans. Francis C. Wade (Marquette University Press, 1955).

*Check questions (one drawn at random; a wrong answer moves to another):*

Explanation: This is the positing-removing mood, the one lawful mood of the conjunctive. Wrong-answer note: The conjunctive does have a lawful mood, and here the minor posits rather than removes.
2. “Socrates is not both a B and a C. Socrates is not a B. So Socrates is a C.” Is it valid? Explanation: “Not both” asserts neither member, so removing one posits nothing. Wrong-answer note: Reading “not both” as “one or the other” is the usual error; the conjunctive does have a lawful mood, but it posits in order to remove.
3. “The number is either odd or even. It is not odd. So it is even.” Is it valid? Options: **Valid, since removing one member of a disjunction posits the other** (correct); Invalid, since the disjunction may be broad; Invalid, since the minor premise must posit a member; Valid only if the disjunction is strict. Explanation: Every disjunction asserts that at least one member is true, so if one is removed the other stands. Wrong-answer note: The strict or broad kind matters only when a member is posited; removing-positing holds for both.

**Card 2.**

**Strict and broad disjunction.** Whether positing one member of a disjunction removes the other depends on the kind of disjunction, and the premise marks the kind. A **strict** disjunction, marked “but not both,” says that exactly one member is true; so positing one removes the other, and both moods are lawful. A **broad** disjunction, marked “or perhaps both,” says only that at least one member is true; so from “He is either clever or lucky, or perhaps both” and “He is clever,” nothing follows about his luck. The marker must therefore be read before the conclusion is judged.

The members may concern one subject, as in “Socrates is not both a B and a C,” and then positing and removing are said of that subject. Or each member may be a whole categorical proposition, such as “All B are C.” To remove such a member is to assert its contradictory: the contradictory of A is O, of O is A, of E is I, and of I is E. So “Some B are not C” removes “All B are C,” and “No D are F” removes “Some D are F.” Whatever the members are, the mood alone decides whether the conclusion follows.

*Check questions (one drawn at random; a wrong answer moves to another):*

1. “Socrates is either a B or a C, or perhaps both. Socrates is a B. So Socrates is not a C.” Is it valid? Options: **Invalid, since a broad disjunction allows both members together** (correct); Valid, since positing one member removes the other; Valid, since the members are opposed; Invalid, since nothing ever follows from a disjunction. Explanation: “Or perhaps both” marks a broad disjunction, so positing one member removes nothing. Wrong-answer note: Only a strict disjunction (“but not both”) lets the positing of one member remove the other; and removing one member of any disjunction does posit the other.
2. “Socrates is either a B or a C, but not both. Socrates is a B. So Socrates is not a C.” Is it valid? Options: **Valid, since a strict disjunction allows only one member to be true** (correct); Invalid, since positing one member never removes the other; Invalid, since the minor premise must remove a member; Valid, since every disjunction is strict. Explanation: “But not both” marks a strict disjunction, so both moods are lawful. Wrong-answer note: A broad disjunction would not allow this conclusion, so not every disjunction is strict.
3. To remove the member “All B are C” is to assert which proposition? Options: **“Some B are not C”** (correct); “No B are C”; “Some B are C”; “All C are B”. Explanation: To remove a proposition is to assert its contradictory, and the contradictory of A is O. Wrong-answer note: “No B are C” is the contrary, which says more than the denial; “Some B are C” is the subaltern, and “All C are B” the converse.

### XIII. The Hypothetical Syllogism (2 cards)

**Card 1.**

**The conditional and its two lawful moods.** A conditional proposition asserts a connection, not a fact: “If it has rained, the ground is wet.” The clause after “if” is the **antecedent**, and the clause it supports is the **consequent**. A conditional is true when the consequent really follows from the antecedent, even if neither is true in fact. Each question gives a conditional premise, a second premise, and a conclusion, and asks whether the conclusion follows.

Two moods are lawful. In the positing mood (*modus ponens*, also called *ponendo ponens*), the second premise posits the antecedent, and the consequent follows: “It has rained; therefore the ground is wet.” In the removing mood (*modus tollens*, also called *tollendo tollens*), the second premise removes the consequent, and the antecedent is removed with it: “The ground is not wet; therefore it has not rained.” The second mood holds because the antecedent cannot be true without the consequent.

*Sources line:* Timothy Kearns, “Logic (General and Historical),” *Encyclopedia of Catholic Theology* (ect.org, 2025); Jacques Maritain, *Formal Logic*, trans. Imelda Choquette (Sheed & Ward, 1937); John of St. Thomas, *Outlines of Formal Logic*, trans. Francis C. Wade (Marquette University Press, 1955).

*Check questions (one drawn at random; a wrong answer moves to another):*

1. “If Socrates is a B, then Socrates is a C. Socrates is a B. So Socrates is a C.” Is it valid? Options: **Valid, by the positing mood (modus ponens)** (correct); Invalid, by affirming the consequent; Invalid, by denying the antecedent; Valid, by the removing mood (modus tollens). Explanation: The second premise posits the antecedent, so the consequent follows. Wrong-answer note: Nothing is removed here, and it is the antecedent, not the consequent, that is posited.
2. “If Socrates is a B, then Socrates is a C. Socrates is not a C. So Socrates is not a B.” Is it valid? Options: **Valid, by the removing mood (modus tollens)** (correct); Invalid, by denying the antecedent; Invalid, by affirming the consequent; Valid, by the positing mood (modus ponens). Explanation: The antecedent cannot be true without the consequent, so removing the consequent removes the antecedent. Wrong-answer note: The second premise removes the consequent, not the antecedent, so this is no fallacy.
3. In “If it has rained, the ground is wet,” which clause is the antecedent? Options: **“it has rained”** (correct); “the ground is wet”; The whole conditional; Neither, since a conditional asserts no fact. Explanation: The antecedent is the clause after “if”; the consequent is the clause it supports. Wrong-answer note: “The ground is wet” is the consequent; a conditional asserts a connection, but its parts still have names.

**Card 2.**

**The two fallacies.** Two arguments imitate these moods and fail. **Affirming the consequent** reasons, “If it rained, the ground is wet; the ground is wet; so it rained.” It fails because the consequent may be true on other grounds, as a burst pipe would also wet the ground. **Denying the antecedent** reasons, “If it rained, the ground is wet; it did not rain; so the ground is not wet,” and it fails for the same reason, since the consequent was never made to depend on the antecedent alone. A conditional asserts the connection in one direction only, from antecedent to consequent; it does not assert the reverse, even when the reverse happens to be true.

The antecedent or the consequent may itself be negative, as in “If Socrates is not a B, then Socrates is a C.” To posit a negative antecedent is to assert it as it stands (“Socrates is not a B”), and to remove it is to assert the affirmative (“Socrates is a B”). The components may also be whole categorical propositions, and then to remove one is to assert its contradictory, as “Some B are not C” removes “All B are C.” In every case the mood decides whether the conclusion follows, whatever the components are.

*Check questions (one drawn at random; a wrong answer moves to another):*

1. “If it rained, the ground is wet. The ground is wet. So it rained.” Is it valid? Options: **Invalid, by affirming the consequent** (correct); Valid, by the positing mood; Invalid, by denying the antecedent; Valid, by the removing mood. Explanation: The ground may be wet on other grounds, so positing the consequent does not posit the antecedent. Wrong-answer note: The positing mood posits the antecedent, not the consequent; and nothing is denied here.
2. “If it rained, the ground is wet. It did not rain. So the ground is not wet.” Is it valid? Options: **Invalid, by denying the antecedent** (correct); Valid, by the removing mood; Invalid, by affirming the consequent; Valid, by the positing mood. Explanation: The consequent was never made to depend on the antecedent alone, so removing the antecedent does not remove it. Wrong-answer note: The removing mood removes the consequent, not the antecedent.
3. “If Socrates is not a B, then Socrates is a C. Socrates is not a C.” What follows? Options: **Socrates is a B** (correct); Socrates is not a B; Socrates is a C; Nothing follows. Explanation: Removing the consequent removes the antecedent, and to remove “Socrates is not a B” is to assert “Socrates is a B.” Wrong-answer note: The antecedent is negative, so removing it gives the affirmative; and this is the removing mood, so something does follow.

### XV. Arguments in Ordinary Speech (2 cards)

**Card 1.**

**Finding the argument in the prose.** Each question states an argument in ordinary English, which may be categorical, conditional, conjunctive, disjunctive, or modal, and asks whether it is a valid syllogism. The first step is to find the conclusion. Words such as “therefore,” “so,” “hence,” and “it follows that” mark the conclusion; words such as “since,” “because,” “for,” and “after all” mark a premise. The conclusion often comes first, as in “No oaks are shrubs, since every oak is a tree and no tree is a shrub.”

The next step is to restate each premise in standard form. “Every S is a P,” “Whatever is an S is a P,” “If something is an S, then it is a P,” and “S are always P” are A. “S are never P,” “Not a single S is a P,” and “There is no S that is a P” are E. “There are S that are P,” “Certain S are P,” and “A few S are P” are I, but “Few S are P” chiefly denies and is read as “Some S are not P.” “Not all S are P” and “Not every S is a P” are O, since they deny the universal without asserting “none.” The English “All S are not P” may mean “No S are P” or only “Some S are not P” (“All that glitters is not gold” means the second), so it should be read twice. “Only P are S” means “All S are P,” so the term after “only” becomes the predicate, and “none but” and “no one except” work in the same way.

When no sign of quantity appears, the sense decides: “Dogs are mammals” means all dogs, but “Dogs were barking in the alley” means some. A verb other than the copula is restated with it, so “All dogs bark” becomes “All dogs are things that bark.” A singular subject, as in “Socrates is a philosopher,” is taken as universal, since it speaks of the whole of its subject. A term stated in the singular or in the plural, or in other words with the same meaning, is still one term.

*Sources line:* Timothy Kearns, “Logic (General and Historical),” *Encyclopedia of Catholic Theology* (ect.org, 2025); Jacques Maritain, *Formal Logic*, trans. Imelda Choquette (Sheed & Ward, 1937).

*Check questions (one drawn at random; a wrong answer moves to another):*

1. Which form is “Not every sailor is a pirate”? Options: **O** (correct); A; E; I. Explanation: It denies the universal without saying that no sailor is a pirate, so it says that some sailors are not pirates. Wrong-answer note: It does not say that every sailor is a pirate (A), that none is (E), or that some are (I).
2. What does “Only citizens are voters” state? Options: **“All voters are citizens”** (correct); “All citizens are voters”; “Some citizens are voters”; “No voters are citizens”. Explanation: The term after “only” becomes the predicate, so whatever votes is a citizen. Wrong-answer note: It does not say that every citizen votes; that would be the converse.
3. “No oaks are shrubs, since every oak is a tree and no tree is a shrub.” Which is the conclusion? Options: **“No oaks are shrubs”** (correct); “Every oak is a tree”; “No tree is a shrub”; The argument has no conclusion. Explanation: “Since” marks what follows it as premises, so the conclusion is the clause that comes first. Wrong-answer note: The two clauses after “since” are the premises.

**Card 2.**

**Judging what has been found.** Once the argument is in standard form, it is judged by the rules already learned. For a categorical syllogism, the middle term must be distributed at least once; no term may be distributed in the conclusion unless it is distributed in its premise; nothing follows from two negative premises or from two particular premises; and a negative premise requires a negative conclusion, as a negative conclusion requires a negative premise. For a compound premise, the connective decides which moods are lawful. “If … then” concludes by positing the antecedent or by removing the consequent, and affirming the consequent is the common error. “Not both” concludes only by positing one member in order to remove the other. “Either … or” concludes by removing one member in order to posit the other, and by positing one in order to remove the other only when it is strict (“but not both”).

Some arguments carry a mode, such as “necessarily” or “contingently,” and then the conclusion may not claim a stronger mode than follows from the premises. When both premises have the same mode, the conclusion has that mode. In the first figure a necessary major premise with a plain minor premise gives a necessary conclusion. When a premise is contingent (in the first figure, the major premise; in the third figure, either premise), the conclusion is only contingent. In the second figure two contingent premises give no conclusion at all. An argument whose conclusion claims a stronger mode than these rules allow is invalid as it is stated.

*Check questions (one drawn at random; a wrong answer moves to another):*

1. “If Plato is a philosopher, he loves wisdom. Plato loves wisdom. So he is a philosopher.” Is it valid? Options: **Invalid, since it affirms the consequent** (correct); Valid, since it posits the antecedent; Valid, since it removes the consequent; Invalid, since a conditional never yields a conclusion. Explanation: The second premise posits the consequent, and the consequent may be true on other grounds. Wrong-answer note: The antecedent is not posited and the consequent is not removed; and a conditional does yield conclusions by its two lawful moods.
2. “Necessarily, every man is an animal. Every runner is a man. So, necessarily, every runner is an animal.” Is it valid as stated? Options: **Valid, since in the first figure a necessary major premise with a plain minor premise gives a necessary conclusion** (correct); Invalid, since the conclusion must follow the weaker premise in mode; Invalid, since the minor premise is plain; Valid, since both premises are necessary. Explanation: Aristotle holds this for the first figure (*Prior Analytics* I.9), and the course follows him. Wrong-answer note: The rule of the weaker premise in mode is Theophrastus’s, which the course does not follow here; and the minor premise is plain, not necessary.
3. “Contingently, no traveler is a merchant. Contingently, every pilgrim is a merchant. So, contingently, no pilgrim is a traveler.” Is it valid? Options: **Invalid, since in the second figure two contingent premises give no conclusion** (correct); Valid, since its plain form (Cesare) is valid; Valid, since the conclusion claims only contingency; Invalid, since the middle term is undistributed. Explanation: “Merchant” is the predicate of both premises, so the syllogism is in the second figure, where two contingent premises give nothing (*Prior Analytics* I.17). Wrong-answer note: The plain form is valid and the middle term is distributed in the first premise, but the modes decide that nothing follows.

### XVI. The Modal Syllogism (2 cards)

**Card 1.**

**The question of mode.** A modal syllogism is one whose premises say how the predicate belongs to the subject. Three modes are considered here: the **necessary** (it cannot be otherwise), the **assertoric** or plain (it simply is so), and the **contingent** (it can be, and can also not be). Each question gives a syllogism with its premises in these modes and asks in what mode the conclusion follows: necessarily, assertorically, contingently, or not at all.

The first test is the form. If the syllogism is invalid by the general rules, nothing follows in any mode. In quality and quantity the conclusion follows the weaker premise, as in every categorical syllogism, so a negative premise requires a negative conclusion, and a particular premise a particular one.

In mode the course follows Aristotle. When both premises are necessary, the conclusion is necessary, and when both are plain, it is plain. In the first figure a necessary major premise with a plain minor premise gives a necessary conclusion, because what belongs necessarily to everything under the middle term belongs necessarily to whatever falls under it: “Every man is necessarily an animal; every runner is a man; therefore every runner is necessarily an animal” (*Prior Analytics* I.9). But a plain major premise with a necessary minor premise gives only a plain conclusion, because the major premise says only that the predicate belongs, not that it must.

*Sources line:* Timothy Kearns, “Logic (General and Historical),” *Encyclopedia of Catholic Theology* (ect.org, 2025); Jacques Maritain, *Formal Logic*, trans. Imelda Choquette (Sheed & Ward, 1937); Aristotle, *Prior Analytics* I.9, 14–17, 20–21.

*Check questions (one drawn at random; a wrong answer moves to another):*

1. “Necessarily, all M are P. All S are M. So all S are P.” In what mode does the conclusion follow? Options: **Necessarily** (correct); Assertorically; Contingently; Nothing follows. Explanation: In the first figure a necessary major premise with a plain minor premise gives a necessary conclusion. Wrong-answer note: “Assertorically” is the answer by Theophrastus’s rule of the weaker premise, which the course does not follow here; and the form is Barbara, so something follows.
2. “All M are P. Necessarily, all S are M. So all S are P.” In what mode does the conclusion follow? Options: **Assertorically** (correct); Necessarily; Contingently; Nothing follows. Explanation: In the first figure a plain major premise with a necessary minor premise gives only a plain conclusion, because the major premise says only that the predicate belongs. Wrong-answer note: The necessity of the minor premise does not carry over to the conclusion, and the form is valid.
3. “Necessarily, all P are M. Necessarily, all S are M. So all S are P.” In what mode does the conclusion follow? Options: **Nothing follows** (correct); Necessarily; Assertorically; Contingently. Explanation: The middle term is the predicate of two affirmatives and is never distributed, so the form is invalid, and no mode can rescue it. Wrong-answer note: Necessary premises cannot make an invalid form valid.

**Card 2.**

**Contingent premises.** When a premise is contingent, Aristotle’s rules differ from figure to figure, and the exercise uses only the cases in which his rule is simple. In the first figure, a contingent major premise with a contingent or plain minor premise gives a contingent conclusion (*Prior Analytics* I.14–15). In the third figure, two contingent premises, or one contingent and one plain, give a contingent conclusion (I.20–21). In the second figure, two contingent premises give no conclusion at all, whatever their quantity or quality (I.17). Aristotle’s other mixtures differ from mood to mood, and the exercise does not ask about them.

**A note on another view.** Theophrastus, Aristotle’s successor at the Lyceum, held that the conclusion always follows the weaker premise, in mode as in quality and quantity; by his rule the syllogism of the runner concludes only that every runner is an animal, as a plain assertion. The medieval commentators, the logic handed down under St. Thomas’s name, and St. Thomas himself when he cites the rule all follow Aristotle on this point, and so does this course.

Explanation: The syllogism is in the second figure, where two contingent premises give no conclusion (*Prior Analytics* I.17). Wrong-answer note: Its plain form (Cesare) is valid, but in this figure the modes leave nothing to conclude.
2. “Contingently, all M are P. All S are M. So all S are P.” In what mode does the conclusion follow? Options: **Contingently** (correct); Assertorically; Necessarily; Nothing follows. Explanation: In the first figure a contingent major premise with a plain minor premise gives a contingent conclusion (*Prior Analytics* I.15). Wrong-answer note: The plain minor premise does not raise the conclusion above contingency, and the form is valid.
3. Whose rule is it that the conclusion always follows the weaker premise in mode? Options: **Theophrastus, Aristotle’s successor at the Lyceum** (correct); Aristotle; St. Thomas; John of St. Thomas. Explanation: Theophrastus held the rule; Aristotle held otherwise for the first figure, and the course follows Aristotle. Wrong-answer note: Aristotle and St. Thomas, when he cites the rule, grant a necessary conclusion from a necessary major premise and a plain minor premise in the first figure.

### XVII. The Chain of Syllogisms (1 card)

**Card 1.**

**A chain of syllogisms.** Reasoning rarely stops at one syllogism. A chain of syllogisms, which the tradition calls a polysyllogism, links several syllogisms so that the conclusion of one serves as a premise of the next: “All B are C; all A are B; therefore all A are C. All C are D; therefore all A are D.” In each link after the first, one new premise is stated, and the conclusion just drawn serves as the other premise. (When the intermediate conclusions are left unstated, the chain is called a sorites; the chains here state every conclusion.) Each question gives a chain in schematic letters and asks whether the whole chain is valid or whether some link fails.

The chain is judged link by link, and each link by the general rules of the syllogism. In each link the middle term is the term its two premises share and its conclusion omits. That middle term must be distributed at least once; no term may be distributed in the conclusion unless it is distributed in its premise; nothing follows from two negative premises or from two particular premises; and a negative premise requires a negative conclusion, as a negative conclusion requires a negative premise. The whole chain is valid only if every link is valid, so one invalid link makes the whole chain invalid, even when the last conclusion looks plausible.

*Sources line:* Timothy Kearns, “Logic (General and Historical),” *Encyclopedia of Catholic Theology* (ect.org, 2025); Jacques Maritain, *Formal Logic*, trans. Imelda Choquette (Sheed & Ward, 1937).

*Check questions (one drawn at random; a wrong answer moves to another):*

1. “All B are C; all A are B; therefore all A are C. All C are D; therefore all A are D.” Is the whole chain valid? Options: **Valid, since each link keeps every rule** (correct); Invalid, since the second link has no middle term; Invalid, since a chain may have only one link; Valid only if the premises are true. Explanation: Both links are in Barbara: C is the middle term of the second link, and it is distributed in “All C are D.” Wrong-answer note: The second link’s middle term is C, shared by “All C are D” and “all A are C”; and validity does not depend on the truth of the premises.
2. “All B are C; all A are B; therefore all A are C. Some C are D; therefore some A are D.” Is the whole chain valid? Options: **Invalid, since the middle term C is not distributed in the second link** (correct); Valid, since the first link is valid; Valid, since every link states a conclusion; Invalid, since the second link has two negative premises. Explanation: C is the subject of a particular and the predicate of an affirmative, so it is distributed in neither premise of the second link. Wrong-answer note: A valid first link does not save an invalid second one, and no premise here is negative.
3. If one link of a chain is invalid, what of the whole chain? Options: **The whole chain is invalid** (correct); The chain is valid if its last link is valid; The chain is valid if most of its links are valid; The chain is neither valid nor invalid. Explanation: The chain is valid only if every link is valid. Wrong-answer note: A conclusion that does not follow at one step cannot be made to follow by later steps.

### XVIII. The Matter and the Form (2 cards)

**Card 1.**

**Form and matter.** Every argument can be judged in two ways. Its **form** is the arrangement of its terms, and the argument is **valid** when it is impossible for the premises to be true and the conclusion false, whatever the terms stand for. Its **matter** is what the premises say, and the question there is whether they are in fact true. So the tradition distinguishes, within demonstrative science, **formal logic**, which studies the arrangement of the terms and whether the conclusion can be deduced from premises taken as given, and **material logic**, which studies how demonstration works, how different kinds of premises relate to the truths we can reach through them, and how each discipline reasons according to its own subject matter.

An argument that is valid and has true premises is **sound**, and it proves its conclusion. A valid argument with a false premise proves nothing, however good its form, and it is the commonest imitation of knowledge. True premises do not save a bad form either: if the form is invalid, the argument fails even when every premise and the conclusion are true. Each question therefore asks whether the form is valid and whether the premises are true, and the answer is “Valid & Sound,” “Valid, Unsound,” or “Invalid.” The form is judged first, by the general rules, and then each premise is tested.

*Sources line:* Timothy Kearns, “Logic (General and Historical),” *Encyclopedia of Catholic Theology* (ect.org, 2025); John A. Oesterle, *Logic: The Art of Defining and Reasoning*, 2nd ed. (Prentice-Hall, 1963).

*Check questions (one drawn at random; a wrong answer moves to another):*

1. “All fish are mammals; all whales are fish; so all whales are mammals.” How is it judged? Options: **Valid, Unsound** (correct); Valid & Sound; Invalid. Explanation: The form is Barbara, so it is valid; but “All fish are mammals” is false, so it proves nothing. Wrong-answer note: A true conclusion does not make the argument sound, and a false premise does not make the form invalid.
2. “All birds are animals; all robins are animals; so all robins are birds.” How is it judged? Options: **Invalid** (correct); Valid & Sound; Valid, Unsound. Explanation: “Animal” is the predicate of two affirmatives and is never distributed, so the form is invalid, although the premises and the conclusion are all true. Wrong-answer note: True premises cannot make an invalid form valid.
3. Does a valid argument with a false premise prove its conclusion? Options: **No, since a valid form proves nothing from false matter** (correct); Yes, since the form is valid; Yes, if the conclusion happens to be true; No, since a false premise makes the form invalid. Explanation: Validity concerns what would follow; proof needs true premises as well. Wrong-answer note: The form is still valid, but the argument is unsound, and a true conclusion reached this way is not proved by it.

**Card 2.**

**Soundness and demonstration.** A sound argument is not yet a demonstration. A demonstration in the strict sense is a valid syllogism whose premises are true, better known than the conclusion, and express the necessary causes of the conclusion, so that it shows not only that the conclusion is true but why it must be. This exercise asks only whether the argument is valid and its premises true; it does not ask whether the premises are first and causal.

The arguments are drawn from four matters: the everyday world of living things and things made, classical mathematics after Euclid, the doctrine of virtue after Aristotle and St. Thomas, and the soul and living things after Aristotle’s *De Anima*. Each premise is judged by ordinary knowledge of these things and by their classical definitions, which are given above the argument as “Key terms.” Some true premises sound doubtful at first. For Euclid an isosceles triangle has two sides alone equal, so no equilateral triangle is isosceles; the unit is not a number; and a point is not a magnitude. For St. Thomas a passion is not a habit, the theological virtues observe no mean, and prudence is intellectual in what it is but is counted with the moral virtues because it concerns conduct. And according to the *De Anima* the oyster is an animal without sight. So a surprising premise is tested against the definitions rather than against first impressions.

Explanation: An equilateral triangle has three equal sides, so it does not have two sides alone equal. Wrong-answer note: On the looser modern definition the statement would be false, but the exercise judges by the classical definition.

Options: **Premises better known than the conclusion that express its necessary causes** (correct); More than two premises; Premises granted by everyone; A surprising conclusion. Explanation: A demonstration shows why the conclusion must be so, through premises that give its cause. Wrong-answer note: Number of premises, general agreement, and surprise have nothing to do with demonstration.
3. A valid argument has true premises, but they do not give the cause of the conclusion. Is it a demonstration in the strict sense? Options: **No, though it is sound** (correct); Yes, since it is valid and its premises are true; No, since it is unsound; Yes, if its conclusion is necessary. Explanation: It is sound, and so it proves that its conclusion is true; but it does not show why it must be so. Wrong-answer note: Soundness is not yet demonstration, and the argument is not unsound.

### XIX. The Enthymeme (2 cards)

**Card 1.**

**The unspoken premise.** An enthymeme is a syllogism with a premise (or, at times, the conclusion) left unspoken, which the hearer is trusted to supply: “Socrates is mortal, for he is a man” leaves out “All men are mortal.” The enthymeme is far more frequent than the full syllogism, in scientific speech as in common speech. It is called first-order when the major premise is left unsaid, and second-order when the minor premise is. A premise is left unsaid sometimes for brevity, sometimes because it is too obvious to state, and sometimes because it is weak and would not be granted if it were stated; so the suppressed premise is where an argument hides its weakness, and it should be supplied and examined. Restoring a valid form does not make the argument sound, since the premise supplied may be false.

Some questions give the stated premise and the conclusion and ask for the tacit premise, typed in standard form, or “none.” First find the two terms of the conclusion: its predicate is the major term and its subject the minor term. Then find the middle term, the term of the stated premise that is not in the conclusion. Then join the middle term to the term of the conclusion that the stated premise leaves out, in a quantity and quality that make a valid syllogism by the general rules; any premise that does so is accepted. So for “All A are C, since all B are C,” the tacit premise is “All A are B.” Sometimes no premise can complete the argument, because the stated premise is particular or negative and the conclusion claims more than any added premise could support, as in “All A are C, for some B are C”; then the answer is “none.”

*Sources line:* Timothy Kearns, “Logic (General and Historical),” *Encyclopedia of Catholic Theology* (ect.org, 2025); Jacques Maritain, *Formal Logic*, trans. Imelda Choquette (Sheed & Ward, 1937).

*Check questions (one drawn at random; a wrong answer moves to another):*

Explanation: The middle term “man” must be joined to “mortal”; with this major premise the argument is valid. Wrong-answer note: “All mortals are men” leaves the middle term undistributed, “Some men are mortal” is too weak, and the last merely restates the conclusion.

Wrong-answer note: “All B are A” yields only “Some A are C,” and “Some A are B” yields only a particular conclusion.

Explanation: Whatever premise is added, the particular premise allows only a particular conclusion, and “All A are C” is universal. Wrong-answer note: With “All A are B” the middle term is undistributed, and with the others the conclusion still cannot be universal.

**Card 2.**

**The direction of the premise, and signs.** The tacit premise must run in the right direction. For “Socrates is mortal, for he is a man,” the premise needed is “All men are mortal,” not its converse “All mortals are men,” which would leave the middle term undistributed; and “Some men are mortal” is too weak to conclude anything about Socrates. Other questions give such an argument in ordinary words and ask what is being taken for granted.

Aristotle also uses the word “enthymeme” in another sense, for a rhetorical argument from likelihoods and signs, and many of these examples are arguments from signs. A necessary sign concludes of necessity, as in Aristotle’s example “She has given birth, for she has milk,” whose tacit premise is “Whoever has milk has given birth.” A refutable sign does not: “It must have rained, for the streets are wet” is valid only with “Whenever the streets are wet, it has rained,” and that premise is false, since a street-sweeper also wets the streets. The familiar premise “Whenever it rains, the streets are wet” is true, but with it the argument only affirms the consequent.

Wrong-answer note: The converse, true or not, would leave the argument affirming the consequent; the others conclude nothing about her.

### XX. Dialectic (3 cards)

**Card 1.**

**Reasoning from the probable.** Dialectic is the use of reason to discover what is probably true in cases where we have not yet found demonstrative proof. It reasons from what is probable, that is, from what seems so to everyone, or to most people, or to the wise, and among the wise to all of them or to the most eminent. The probable in this sense is not a guess about odds but what a reasonable person may be asked to grant. So dialectic reaches a reasoned opinion, well supported and still open to challenge, while demonstration reaches science. The tradition places dialectic between rhetoric, which inclines us where the evidence is nearly even, and demonstration, which proves through the cause.

Some questions ask what standing a premise has before it is offered. It may be **demonstrable**, a matter of science that can be proved, and so not the business of dialectic. It may be **granted by everyone**, probable in the fullest sense, so that it may simply be assumed. It may be **granted by the learned**, still probable, though whoever uses it should expect to defend it. Or it may be **one person’s opinion**, perhaps true, but not yet something an opponent can be asked to grant. A principle known through itself as soon as its terms are understood, such as “Every whole is greater than any one of its parts,” is granted by everyone; it is certain, but it is not proved.

*Sources line:* Timothy Kearns, “Logic (General and Historical),” *Encyclopedia of Catholic Theology* (ect.org, 2025); John A. Oesterle, *Logic: The Art of Defining and Reasoning*, 2nd ed. (Prentice-Hall, 1963).

Wrong-answer note: Knowledge of the cause belongs to demonstration, persuasion to rhetoric, and stories to poetics.

**Card 2.**

**Places and maxims.** Logic has two parts: the art of judging an argument already given, which the *Prior* and *Posterior Analytics* teach, and the art of finding an argument when one is needed, which Aristotle’s *Topics* teaches. The instrument of finding is the place (*locus*), also called the topic, a standing relation such as genus to species or whole to part, from which an argument may be drawn. Given something to be shown and something already granted, a place shows which relation will make the one a reason for the other. Each place carries its maxim (*maxima propositio*), the governing proposition on which an argument drawn from that place depends. When the maxim is true the argument holds; when it is false but looks true, the argument is a fallacy, so dialectic and the study of fallacies are one doctrine seen from opposite sides.

The exercise names twelve places. The place *from the definition* rests on the maxim that whatever fits the definition fits the thing defined, and whatever does not, does not. The place *from the wider kind* (genus) rests on the maxim that whatever belongs to the whole family belongs to each kind within it, and what the whole family lacks, each kind lacks. The place *from the narrower kind* (species) rests on the maxim that whatever the narrower kind is said of, the wider kind is said of too. The place *from the property* rests on the maxim that where the property is, the thing is, and where it is absent, the thing is absent. The place *from opposites* rests on the maxim that what holds of one opposite, the contrary holds of the other. The place *from the more and the less* rests on the maxim that if it holds where it is less to be expected, it holds all the more where it is more to be expected. The place *from likeness* rests on the maxim that what holds in one case holds in a case like it, so far as they are alike. The place *from the whole and its parts* rests on the maxim that what holds of the whole holds of the parts, when it holds of the whole by reason of the parts. The place *from the cause and the end* rests on the maxim that where the cause is, the effect follows, and a thing is judged by what it is for. The place *from words of the same root* (conjugates) rests on the maxim that what holds of the thing holds of what is named from it, so that if justice is good, acting justly is good. The place *from the name* rests on the maxim that what the name means tells something of what the thing is, though not always, and never on its own. The place *from authority* rests on the maxim that what those who know a subject agree on may be granted, until better reason appears. The tradition counts authority the weakest of the places, because it moves us by who says a thing rather than by what makes it so; but it is still a real reason.

Wrong-answer note: The maxim is neither an argument, nor a name, nor a conclusion, but the rule the argument depends on.

Wrong-answer note: The tradition counts authority among the places, and those who know a subject are often right.

**Card 3.**

**Answering an objection.** A disputation takes place between two parties, the one who puts the case and the one who answers (the opponent and the respondent, in Latin *opponens* and *respondens*). The schools answer an objection by distinguishing the term, by denying the premise, or by granting the premises and denying the consequence; and when the objection is simply sound, the respondent concedes it and states the thesis again more narrowly. So four answers are open. The respondent **distinguishes the term** (the *distinguo*) when a word in the objection is true in one sense and false in another, granting the one sense and denying the other. He **denies the premise** when the objection rests on something simply false. He **grants it all and denies that the conclusion follows** when every premise stands and the conclusion still does not follow, since the matter is sound but the form is not. And he **grants the whole objection** when it is right, and states the thesis again more narrowly.

A dialectical disputation also differs from a testing one. Dialectic argues toward the better view, while a testing disputation works from what seems true to the respondent, only in order to find out whether he really knows.

*Check questions (one drawn at random; a wrong answer moves to another):*

Explanation: “Good” as a knife and good in its use are two senses; the first is granted and the second denied. Wrong-answer note: The premise is true, and the trouble lies in the word “good,” not in the form or in the thesis.
2. Every premise of an objection is true, yet its conclusion does not follow. Which answer fits? Options: **Grant it all, and deny that the conclusion follows** (correct); Deny the premise; Distinguish the term; Grant the whole objection. Explanation: The matter is sound but the form is not, so the respondent concedes the premises and denies the consequence. Wrong-answer note: Nothing false is to be denied and no word is ambiguous; and since the conclusion does not follow, the thesis need not be given up.
3. How does a testing disputation differ from a dialectical one? Options: **It only finds out whether someone really knows, while dialectic argues toward the better view** (correct); They are the same thing; Testing reaches certainty and dialectic does not; Dialectic uses syllogisms and testing does not. Explanation: A testing disputation works from what seems true to the respondent, in order to see how much he knows. Wrong-answer note: They differ in aim, and neither one reaches the certainty of demonstration.

### XXI. The Fallacies (3 cards)

**Card 1.**

**What a fallacy is.** A fallacy is an argument that looks sound and is not. The tradition studies the typical errors in reasoning so that we can avoid them; Aristotle treats them in the *Sophistical Refutations*, a book aimed especially at refuting those who use errors in reasoning to manipulate others, as the sophists of antiquity did. The exercise follows *De fallaciis*, a short treatise attributed to St. Thomas, which explains each fallacy by two causes. The **cause of the appearance** is what makes the argument look good, and the **cause of the failure** is what actually breaks it; we are deceived by the two together, since something appears to be so and is not. So “I know the man who is coming; Coriscus is the man who is coming; so I know Coriscus” looks sound because the man and the feature really do go together here, and it fails because a man and a feature he happens to have are not the same. The answer to a fallacy removes its cause of failure: the ambiguous word is stated twice in different words, the dropped qualification is put back, or the hidden question is answered first.

Every argument depends on a rule, usually unspoken. “Socrates is a man, therefore an animal” depends on a true rule, that whatever the narrower kind is said of, the wider kind is said of too. “Socrates is an animal, therefore a man” depends on the same rule reversed, which is false. So both premises may be true and the argument still fail, because the rule it depends on is false but looks true. When we reason badly by ourselves, St. Thomas notes, it is always beside our intention, since nobody sets out to deceive himself; so this study is applied first to our own arguments.

*Sources line:* Timothy Kearns, “Logic (General and Historical),” *Encyclopedia of Catholic Theology* (ect.org, 2025); John of St. Thomas, *Outlines of Formal Logic*, trans. Francis C. Wade (Marquette University Press, 1955); St. Thomas Aquinas (attributed), *De fallaciis*; Aristotle, *Sophistical Refutations*.

**Card 2.**

**The thirteen fallacies.** The appearance arises in two ways: from the words, when one expression seems to name one thing although it means several, and from outside the words, when the language is in order but two things that agree in some way are taken to be simply one. Six fallacies are in the words, and their number follows from the kinds of ambiguity. Actual ambiguity gives **equivocation** in a word (“dog,” said of the animal and of the star) and **amphiboly** in a phrase (“the book of Aristotle,” the one he wrote or the one he owned). Potential ambiguity, meaning several things according to how the words are said, gives **accent** in a word, where a change of stress changes the claim, and in a phrase **composition**, which takes words together that are true only taken apart (“A seated man can walk”), and **division**, which takes words apart that are true only taken together (“Two and three are five”). Apparent ambiguity gives **figure of speech**, which treats two expressions of the same form as if they signified in the same way.

Seven fallacies are outside the words. **Accident** takes what is true of a passing feature for what is true of the thing that has it, as in the case of Coriscus; modern books have no exact name for it. **In a certain respect, taken flatly** takes what is true in some respect as true without qualification; modern books call it hasty generalization, and in its other direction, where a rule that holds for the most part is applied to a case it does not fit, they call it the fallacy of accident. **Missing the point** (ignorance of the refutation) proves something other than what was denied, since a real refutation must contradict the very thing said, in the same respect and at the same time; the straw man and the red herring are species of it. **Begging the question** assumes in the premises what was to be proved. **Treating what is not the cause as the cause** takes what merely goes with a thing for what produces it, which modern books call false cause. **The consequent** reads a one-way link as running both ways, as in “If it rained, the ground is wet; the ground is wet; so it rained”; modern books call this affirming the consequent, and in categorical form the undistributed middle, but Aristotle and the schools count the two as one fallacy. **Many questions asked as one** hides a second question inside the first, so that either answer grants it; modern books call it the loaded question.

**Card 3.**

**Disputations and the sophist’s aims.** *De fallaciis* begins from the disputation, an act of one person toward another to show something proposed, and it distinguishes four kinds. A **demonstrative** disputation, between a teacher and a learner, aims at certain knowledge from premises that are true and known in themselves. A **dialectical** disputation aims at a reasoned opinion from what is probable. A **testing** disputation aims at finding out whether someone really knows, by working from what seems true to him. A **sophistical** disputation aims at seeming wise and winning, from what looks true or probable but is not; for “sophistical” means apparent wisdom.

The sophist has five aims, five awkward positions into which he tries to drive the respondent, and each offends a different discipline, so that he may appear wise in all of them. He tries to make the respondent contradict himself (refutation, *redargutio*), which offends against metaphysics; grant something plainly false, which offends against natural science and mathematics; grant something absurd, against what nearly everyone believes, which offends against dialectic; say something ungrammatical (a solecism), which offends against grammar; or babble, repeating the same thing uselessly (*nugatio*), which offends against rhetoric. The false and the absurd differ, since some false things are widely believed and some things against common opinion are true.
