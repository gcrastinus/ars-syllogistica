## Slide 3
First and Second Intentions
First intention: Knowledge concerned with realities that are fully mind-independent
Examples from “natural” being: Tomato, Plant, Ivy, Human, Animal, Wolf
Moral being: Virtue, Justice, Temperance, Human Act, Merit, Demerit
Second intention: Knowledge concerned with realities inasmuch as they are known and, in particular, are subject to our natural processes of knowledge
Tomato is a species of plant
Merit is a property of human acts
Virtue is a genus in relation to justice and temperance
A way to remember this: You’ve never seen a genus walking down the street

## Slide 4
First and Second Intentions
However, it is important to realize that there are three genera of second intentions.
To understand this, we must see that the human intellect has three kinds of actions in our speculation
(“Simple apprehension”) Definition: Man = Rational Animal; Wolf = Wild Animal; Virtue = operative habitus
Propositions: [Every] Man is a rational animal; [Every] Man is a cultural being; [Every] man is intelligent (=has an intellect)
(“Argumentation”) Syllogisms
[Every] man is an intelligent being
Intelligent beings are cultural by their very nature
Therefore, [every] man is a cultural being
(For the philosophers out there, note carefully what I qualified: in our speculation.  There are other intellectual acts involved in practical intellection, especially command and artistic judgment.)

## Slide 5
Second Intentions
These “second intentions” are what are called “relations of reason” (relationes rationis)
They tell us something about how realities as known relate to each other
Historically, the term “second intention” came to be attached to these kinds of relations formed naturally by our reason as it works out definitions, propositions, and syllogisms
We could call them, broadly, “logical relations”
We won’t dispute terms for our purposes

## Slide 10
Terminist Vs. Predicate Logic
Broadly speaking, Aristotelian logic is terminist logic, though this did involve some Western-medieval development and changes
We cannot avoid this sort of logic, and it would be foolish to do so
We obviously:
Form terms and concepts – even if imperfectly
We form propositions – which are made up of terms
And we form syllogisms, which are made up of propositions and of terms
Let’s take a few examples…

## Slide 12
Some more examples… Syllogisms
Example 1
Grace is a participation in the uncreated life of God
Participation in the uncreated life of God is Thaboric in character
Therefore, grace is Thaboric in charcter
Example 2
Salvation in Christ is eschatological in character
The life of grace lived in the Church is the means for salvation in Christ
Therefore, the life of grace in the Church is a means for living an eschatological life
Example 3 (Developing 2 in slightly more formal articulation)
That which is eschatological is in some way participatively eternal
The life of grace lived in the Church is eschatological.
Therefore, the life of grace lived in the Church is in some way participatively eternal

## Slide 14
Terms are Important
In short: All knowledge is attained through some kind of statement of truth.  The creeds are not merely pure apophaticism, even though they ultimately demand mysticism.
We believe in transcendent truths about whom God is, even if we do so imperfectly
Moreover, theologians (even ones who claim to deny this) exercise reasoning – and the Fathers did too
Too many great saints suffered for the truths of faith for us to act as though the Christian message is merely a kind of inarticulable experience that does not, in fact, communicate a truth
The tools of terminist logic will help us to provide ourselves with guideposts for how to ensure that we do not misuse human reasoning
But this does not mean to deny that, in the end, we must pass beyond reason to that which reason presupposes: the experience of reality, above all the Divine Reality known through mystical experience

## Slide 15
Propositional Logic and Terms
As already noted, we will also make use of predicate logic.  For now, a few examples
We‘ll start with hypothetical syllogisms.  Notice, however, how many terms are involved in every single proposition here
Example 1:
If one denies the hypostatic union as something “too uncreated“, then one also denies theosis
But Arius denies the hypostatic union
Therefore, he also denies theosis
Example 2:
If the sacramental mysteries are not received by a person who is rightly disposed, that person will impede the grace received
A person who receives the Eucharist while freely denying truths of the faith is not rightly disposed
Therefore, such a person will impede the grace received from the Eucharist

## Slide 16
Propositional Logic and Terms
A disjunctive syllogism together with a conditional syllogism
Grace is either created or uncreated in its most important specificiative nature
But, if grace is specificatively created, then theosis is impossible
However, we know through the Word of God that we are called to the life of theosis
Therefore, grace is not specificatively created
And, thus, it must be specificatively uncreated
A conjunctive syllogism
You cannot be in a state of grace and not love God above all things: Note, the structure here is something like this: State of Grace AND Loving God above all things. Technically, we could rewrite this in a categorical form.
You are in a state of grace
Therefore, you love God above all things

## Slide 17
Formalization and Thinking
At times, we will formalize our logic, using symbols and shorthand.
But, you need to always ask yourself: what is going on here?  What am I thinking? 
It is a temptation to reduce reasoning to a kind of calculus, pushing tokens around.... Don‘t give into this.  Logical symbolization is a tool of thinking, not a replacement for it.  
True rationalism is to be found in a kind of thoughtless logic-chopping that doesn‘t stop to ask: why is this a good definition?  Why is this a valid propositional conversion?  Why is this a valid syllogism?

## Slide 19
Universals
The problem of universals haunts all of philosophy: what in reality provides the grounding for our thought?
There are several humans involved in this course.  But what about  “human nature“?  What does human refer too?
There will be various solutions:
Absolute / Extreme Realism
Moderate Realism
Nominalism
Ultimately, this question is one of metaphysics, not logic
But, because you nonetheless need to keep a kind of “ background metaphysics“ in mind, we should consider each case...

## Slide 20
Absolute Realism
Plato is the king of absolute realism: Ideas actually exist independent of our knowing of them
To know reality is to have a kind of remembrance of these ideas, to be in touch with the “really real”
There is an exact reality that corresponds to the strongest case of what we call “knowledge”
This sort of realism will variously be operative in the Fathers and in Scholastics
Sometimes, it is allied to related topics, such as “divine illumination”
But somewhat found in someone like Bl. Duns Scotus: Common natures exist positively before we know them (We’ll come back to this in a second)

## Slide 21
Nominalism
You‘ll hear this term bandied about a lot
It is tied up with a lot of different positions that took root in the West from the early 14th century onward
For our purposes, we‘re worried about this aspect: individual humans are maximally similar to each other, but they do not share one and the same nature, as some kind of positive reality
Think of this as a kind of Anti-Platonism.  The only thing that gives unity to our knowledge of reality is our activity of knowing it and naming it (hence, “nominalism”—nomen, name)
This tends to be the bête noire of a kind of “good vs. evil” narrative set forward by thinkers in the West
It’s not wrong to say that nominalism caused immense problems in philosophy and theology
But, that being said, often the greatest nominalists were trying to deal with real questions raised in Scholastic theology
But, this is not the class for this important topic of intellectual history

## Slide 22
Moderate Realism
Generally associated with Aristotelianism (and echoes of this can be found variously in different later Patristic and Greek scholastic thinkers)
Common natures exist as common only in potency before we know them.
But different schools of thought will articulate this differently.  Two very characteristic solutions:
Thomists: Individuals are only not-divided (ONE) as regards sameness of nature—this is how they are ONE; The intellect actualizes this commonality
Scotists: Individuals share in a kind of unity of nature,  though it is a kind of less than numerical unity.  This is the common nature.
Thomism leans in the direction of nominalism; Scotists in the direction of Platonism

## Slide 24
But what is a Universal Logically Speaking?
Therefore, when we speak about universals and particulars, you must keep remembering that we are above all concerned with a relationship of superiority and inferiority, as well as a claim about…
Extension: How broadly does the term in question extend over that which is “under it”
Comprehension: the meaning / “notes” involved in that concept
It is in this sense, and not in an absolutely metaphysically definitive sense, that we can say that universality and individuality / singularity are second intentions
(Note, however, that we will distinguish between particularity and singularity)

## Slide 31
Accident and Property
Accidents reveal something about what we are considering, but not something that uniquely belongs to it.  Thus:
Human: tall, blue-eyed, hairy
Heretic: vexatious, contemptuous, sinful (perhaps loosely proper)
Thomist: orthodox, profound, Western
Palamite: anti-naturalist in matters of grace (perhaps loosely proper); hesychastic (perhaps loosely proper)
Properties, however, reveal something essential, even if they are (definitionally) qualitative
Human: political, scientific, capable of carpentry
Church: One, Holy, Catholic, Apostolic
Christ: Dyothelite, hypostatically Holy, risen at the right hand of the Father
Almost all of the task of trying to define something will be taken up with attempting to provide definitions that are increasingly proper and, perhaps, essential

## Slide 33
Division: The Tool of Definition
All praise be to Plato who realized and worked through the notions of “division” and “collection” (cf., for example, Sophist, Phaedrus, Statesman)
It is an attempt to divide up genera and species and identify what falls “under” what.
Thus, some rules (modified slightly from Smith, 68-9):
What is divided must be of greater extension than what it is divided into
All of the members of a division must ultimately have the same extension as what is divided (don’t leave anything out)
There must be some sort of opposition that divides the members (so that they don’t overlap)
We could call these:
Rule of hierarchy
Rule of extension
Rule of comprehension

## Slide 34
Division: The Tool of Definition
It is really important to realize that we use this kind of reasoning all the time
Plato thought this was so important that, for him, dialectic was the most important sort of reasoning (and so too thought the neo-Platonists)—for dialectic helps us to reach understanding / insight
Aristotle deals with some related topics to this in Posterior analytics and, especially, Topics.  For him, though, syllogistic reasoning holds a special place.  (Nonetheless, we should be careful not to contrast him too much to the Platonists.)

## Slide 49
Supposition
Supposition is concerned with this question: For what does this term stand?
Thus, in addition to the meaning of a term, it can stand for different things
In modern logic, one will speak of “sense” and “reference”
However, much earlier logic was aware of the suppositio of a term
We are only going to introduce this, without examination.  Just exposing you to this
I will follow Maritain rather directly with examples (p. 64ff)

## Slide 50
Supposition
Material: Stands for the written sign.  “Lamb is a four letter word”; “Man is a one-syllable word”
Improper (metaphor): ”The Lamb was offered up for the sins of the world” or better “The red herring distracted me in the debate”
Simple: Reference to common term as common (“Lamb / man is a species of the genus animal”)
Singular: Also for the individual, but one: “The man ran away at once”
Common
Particular-Determinate: Only stands for some determinate few (“Men are liars” = “Some men are liars”)
Particular-Indeterminate: Stands for some things but indistinctly (“Some instrument is necessary”)
Particular-Collective: Stands for a group (“The apostles were twelve.”  Or “The men (as a group) pushed forward.”)
Universal / Distributed
Complete: Stands for all individual subject (”Man is mortal” = Every man is mortal)
Incomplete: Stands for all the kinds of subjects (“Every [kind of] animal was in the Ark.”)
Exceptive: Every man that is merely a man is born in sin, exception made for the Mother of God

## Slide 63
Forming of Statements
The second operation of the intellect involves the forming of complex wholes: statements, upon which we render judgments
A definition solely states the essence, by means of a definition
In a statement / judgment, we affirm that one thing IS or IS NOT another in some way.  It is a kind of duality held together by the verb to be
It is only here that we begin to formally speak about truth: judgments are true and false; concepts might be clear / obscure / vague / distinct / etc.; but only judgements are true and false
But, notice that in some way every statement must involve notions / concepts that it unites
And by such judgments, we slowly but surely begin the process of unpacking the richness of reality
(Moreover, some theologians will hold that faith, being made up of truths, is primarily an act of judgment

## Slide 71
Stepping Back for a Second
Before we keep getting lost in the details of operations, treating the vital activity of the mind as though it were nothing other than token calculation, let’s observe a few points
We opened by noting that logic is concerned with second intentions, a special kind of relationship formed by the mind in its various speculative activities
When defining, we had all the various “predicable” relationships, as well as others, and also properties thereof: species, genus, property, universal, supposition, etc.
By these, we noted how various notions (REALITIES AS KNOWN) are related to each other as we strive to define
Now, we are considering a new kind of ”interweaving” relationship: that of forming propositions.  
Thus, there will be various ways of relating subjects and predicate
And these relationships themselves will be able to be converted into each other
But we must always appreciate that each kind of statement is not merely some token in a calculus but, RATHER, a way that the mind expresses truth to itself
These too are second intentions, but second intentions formed by the intellect’s second operation

## Slide 75
Contradiction: The easiest opposition
The relationship of pure negation between two propositions
This exists between the “corners” of the square of opposition
RULE: Two contradictory propositions cannot be TRUE at the same time, nor can they be FALSE at the same time
A-O Contradiction
Every Arian denies theosis means It is false to say SOME ARIAN DOES NOT DENY THEOSIS
And, moreover, if O is true, then A must be False: Some Bananas are not soft means that it is FALSE to say that All bananas are soft
E-I Contradiction
If we can say that it is FALSE that No Arian denies theosis
Then, it is TRUE to say that SOME ARIAN DENIES THEOSIS

## Slide 76
Contrariety
Relationship between A and E
Cannot both be true.  BUT, they could both be false.  (See the arrows of Kreeft.)
You can pass from a true to a false.
Examples
All sacraments are eschatological => it is FALSE to say No sacraments are eschatological
However, if someone proves to me that it is FALSE to say No Sacraments are Eschatological, I cannot prove that all of them ar
Likewise, even if it is false to say, All Eastern Churches are Byzantine I cannot thereby infer No Eastern Churches are Byzantine

## Slide 77
Sub-Contrariety
Relationship between I and O
Cannot both be false.  BUT, they could both be true.  (See the arrows of Kreeft.)
You can pass from a false to a true.
Examples
That it is TRUE that Some sacraments are unrepeateable DOES NOT BY ITSELF allow us to say it is FALSE that Some sacraments are not unrepeatable
But, when we say that it is FALSE to say Some divine persons are subordinate, we can then say immediately: Some divine persons are not subordinate
Notice, that this is a question of IMMEDIATE “inference” from a given truth, not a full exhaustion of the state of affairs.
(More on the question of so-called immediate inferences briefly next week.)

## Slide 78
Sub-alternation
Relationship from A to I and from E to O
From a true A or E we can pass to a true I or O
Thus…
All sacraments are eschatological => Some sacraments are eschatological
(But, obviously, merely because some are eschatological, we cannot then immediately hold that all are)
So too, No divine persons are subordinate => Some divine persons are not subordinate

## Slide 87
Conversion
Conversion involves the swapping of subject and predicate of a proposition
This is another tool for drawing examples of how we can play out what is implicit in a given statement (note: I am trying to avoid “immediate inference”, as many speak)
But, obviously, we can’t go swapping in all cases!
All Byzantine Churches are Eastern DOES NOT mean that All Eastern Churches are Byzantine!
Likewise: Some theologians are not Palamites DOES NOT MEAN Some Palamites are not theologians.  (Or more down to earth: Some trees are not oaks does not mean Some oaks are not trees.)
Etc.

## Slide 92
Contraposition
A more complex series of operations.  Basically strung together:
(1) Obversion
(2) Followed by a conversion			“Partial contraposition”
(3) Then followed by another obversion
For example:
Every Byzantine is an Eastern Catholics.			A
(1) No Byzantine is a Non-Eastern Catholic.		E
(2) No Non-Eastern Catholic is a Byzantine.		E
(3) Every Non-Eastern Catholic is a non-Byzantine.		A
As you see, this is really:
Every B is E => Every non-E is non-B.
Works for A, E [But note that the conversion changes the quantity], and O

## Slide 116
Rules for Syllogism
Only three terms
Three propositions (2 premises and a conclusion)
Middle term must be distributed at least once
If term is undistributed in a premise, it cannot be distributed in the conclusion 
Cannot have two negative premises
If one premise is negative, conclusion is negative; and vice versa
Two corollaries 
Can’t have two particular premises (though, there semi-exceptions here, for example, for expository syllogisms… Not our problem)
If there is a particular premise, conclusion must be particular
(Cf. Kreeft, 243ff)

## Slide 117
Rule 1: Three Terms
Obviously bad reasoning (many examples could be made):
Every Nestorian is a heretic.
Every Heretic is a sinner.
Every Nestorian is a beefcake.
But, it’s really more operative in the case of ambiguity.  Example from Kreeft:
Every power tends to corrupt.
Knowledge is power.
Therefore, knowledge tends to corrupt.
This is an AAA syllogism in the first-direct figure.  BUT, the sense of power (and of knowledge…) is ambiguous.  (In major: political power)

## Slide 118
Rule 2: Three Propositions
Sometimes, there is an implied premise in an argument that only is presented as premise and a conclusion.
This is called an enthymeme. (See Kreeft, 264–275)
It’s always best to try to concede what ever would be the strongest implied premise.
Example:
A true man (one having a human nature) must have a will human will.
Christ is a true man (one having a human nature) .
Therefore, Christ has a human will.
We’re not going to get into poly-syllogisms, sorites (a “heap” of propositions), which can in fact lead to paradoxes.  (See Kreeft, 275–279)
(Technically, though, there are certain specifically dialectical and rhetorical applications of Enthememes and what are called Epicheiremas, which are syllogisms that have enthymemes attached to one or both premises.  On Epicheiremas, see Kreeft, 279–282)

## Slide 139
Hypothetical Syllogisms
These sorts of “syllogisms” are not like categorical syllogisms, in that they do not necessarily involve a new light cast upon a conclusion through a middle term. 
Rather they tell us something about how propositions are related / connected
Maritain (244): “It is no longer a question of manifesting the union (or separation) of wo terms to each other by means of a third term or concept but of manifesting by means of one of the members of the Major the necessity of positing or destroying the other member.  Consequently, the conditional syllogism does indeed conclude from the whole to the part; but not, as in the categorical syllogism, from a more universal (from the point of view of logical relations that exist between the connected terms) to a less universal truth, but from a compound proposition to one of the members that it contains.  The mind, in the conditional syllogism does not infer one proposition from another; rather, it infers a proposition from an inference that has already been made and affirmed between two propositions.  The need is not to see by a new light and in virtue of an inference that the mind discovers while it brings it about; rather, it is to affirm or deny something by a renewed application of an already existing light and by the use of a previously made inference.”
Developed by Theophrastus (c. 371–287); then in stoicism, especially in Chrysippus (c. 279–206); and passed on to the medieval west by Boethius

Therefore, it is false to say that you have murdered someone
Or
If you are baptized, then you are saved in Christ (at least by seal)
You are not saved in Christ (at least by seal)
Therefore, it is false to say that you are baptized
BUT, You cannot Affirm the Consequent and then Affirm the Antecednet

## Slide 143
Disjunctive Syllogism (Inclusive)
Form: X or Y, NOT X Then Y
X v Y; ~X; Y
For inclusive forms, both can be true
Thus, for example
A good Catholic likes either Byzantine or Non-Byzantine Liturgies
A given good Catholic does not like Non-Byzantine Liturgies
Therefore, that person likes Byzantine liturgies
Notice, though, in this case, IF you affirm X, you cannot DENY Y
Both can be true
This is called “Affirming a disjunct”

## Slide 144
Disjunctive Syllogism (Exclusive)
Very often, though, we form exclusive disjunctives:
Overly simple example
The food is either hot or cool.
The food is hot
Therefore, it is not cool
Another
A philosopher is functioning like a pagan, according to completely ‘purified’ reason or is functioning like a Christian
 A philosopher is functioning like a Christian
Therefore, we cannot say that this philosopher is functioning like a pagan, according to completely ‘purified’ reason
Therefore, just always ask yourself: is this a case where both can be true?  If not, then you can also do this kind of affirming of a disjunct.

## Slide 145
Conjunctive Syllogism
Form:
X and Y; X; Y
Thus, for example
The sun is out and it is hot; both are true, on the supposition of the truth of the first claim in a given situation
More useful: Negative conjunctive
Form: Not (X and Y); X; Therefore, not Y
Example: You cannot serve God and Mammon; but you serve mammon; therefore you do not serve God
Notice how easily this is reduced a conditional:
If a man serves God, then he does not serve mammon… 
But, you cannot pass from saying X is not true to holding something one way or the other about Y
This is called “denying a conjunct”

## Slide 146
Negation of Disjunctive / Conjunctive
Sometimes referred to as “De Morgan’s Law”
NOT (A and B)  NOT A or NOT B
NOT (A or B)  NOT A and NOT B
Symbolized:
~(A ^ B)  ~A v ~B
~(A v B)  ~A ^ ~B

## Slide 154
Explicative Syllogism
Example
Man is mortal						Middle: man
But a rational animal is a man

## Slide 156
Explicative Syllogism
In explicative syllogisms, the conclusion is of itself (quoad se) identical to the major premise
But it is not identical as regards our knowledge (quoad nos) because it involves us re-expressing the terms in a more explicit manner
Therefore, there is formally illation / inference, but NOT objective inference
Here, cf. Schultes’s studies on implicit / explicit faith, as well as the history of dogmas
More advanced point (recalling the “modes of per se”)—the major and the conclusion must be in the same mode of per se.  Otherwise, there will be objective inference.  Example:
A rational animal is capable of scientific knowledge.	Fourth mode
But man is a rational animal
Therefore, man is capable of scientific knowledge.		Second mode per se

## Slide 178
Per Se Attribution – Mode 1
Mode 1 – Predicate is part definition or part of definition
Convertible = Definition / Difference: Per se primo
Notice that in this case, due to the matter, a conversion works:
Every man is a rational animal DOES convert into Every rational animal is a man.
Not convertible = Genus: Per se secundo
But, here we encounter the normal conversion rule for A statements (namely, that they convert into I statements)
Every man is an animal => Some animal is man

## Slide 179
Per Se Attribution – Mode 2
Mode 2 – Predicate is property of subject
Convertible = strict property, Per se primo
Notice that in this case, due to the matter, a conversion works:
Every man is capable of being an political being <=> Everything that is capable of exercising political rationality is a man
Not convertible = Less strict property (flowing from the genus of the subject) Per se secundo
But, here we encounter the normal conversion rule for A statements (namely, that they convert into I statements)
An isoceles triangle has three angles equal to two right angles. 
This converts A=>I
But, the sense of property here is broader than normal (belongs always to all, but not to only it)
I‘m hiding the important distinction between physical and metaphysical properties (cf. Woodbury, Logic, 124E)
Physical property: Real acident consequent upon an essence
Metaphysical property: only conceptually distinct from the essence, or the essence itself as foundation for a physical property —Cf. Antoine Goudin

No distinction of conversion here

Don‘t misuse “ad hominem“
Usually, this means a kind of appeal to someone‘s character to disprove the point unjustly or to evade argumentation

“ An ‘absolute’ demonstration is one which proceeds from premises whose truth we admit and assume in order to then draw an inference, absolutely speaking, as when we demonstrate the real existence of God on the basis of the contingent character of creatures, and other such demonstrations. However, a ‘relative’ (that is, ‘ad hominem’) demonstration is one which proceeds from principles which are admitted by the person we are arguing against and which we assume for the sake of refutation, setting aside the question of the truth of such principles, as when someone assumes principles admitted by materialists or by rationalists, in order to convince them that their doctrine is false.” (Tommaso Zigliara, Summa philosophica, 157)

Archimedes, in explaining the princilpes of the lever, was said to have declared to his friends: “Give me a place where I can stand—and I shall move the world.“  My felow inhabitants of this planet: Let us take our stand here in this assembly of nations.  And let us see if we, in our own time, can move the world to a just and lasting peace.“
This remark is an allusion to allusion made by Thomas Jefferson: The good opinion of mankind, like the lever of Archimedes, with the given fulcrum, moves the world.

## Slide 213
A Theological Debate over Illation…
In the background of our discussion of expository and explicative syllogisms is an entire debate concerning the kind of reasoning that is permitted for the defining of dogmas
This was hotly debated running up to Vatican II
The two major contenders were 
Francisco Marín-Sola: Followed by most people; has some questionable points in his explanations… 
Reginald Schultes: Followed only by a few, Reginald Garrigou-Lagrange, but also Michel Labourdette (likely, for that reason, Marie-Rosaire Gagnebet)
On many points they agreed, but Marín-Sola held that conclusions that are theologically deduced and even enunciate new truths (but not new realities) can be defined as dogmas
Schultes (following a number of Latin Baroque figures) combatted this particular point (though he was not a “fixist”)

## Slide 214
A Theological Debate over Illation…
Basically – the debate came down to this:
When theologians do at least one kind of reasoning, where they add something to the doctrine of faith that is not implicitly or explicitly revealed, what can be said about this truth and the Church’s authority to declare this truth?
And one cannot say that one does not use theological reasoning of this sort in the East.  The Fathers did—admittedly with less zeal than some later day scholastics—and even the theologians who claim that we don’t nonetheless avail themselves of modern scholarship, categories, and methods.
There had a been a distinction between “Divine Faith” and “Ecclesiastical Faith.”  This all got lost after the 1960s, though echoes of it still remain in the Doctrinal Commentary on the Professio Fidei (1998)

## Slide 215
A Theological Debate over Illation…
So then, in theology, one must make some very careful distinctions:

## Slide 216
Analogy
Used regarding our reasoning to God
But, most importantly, analogy indicates the way that our reasoning falls short of knowing God
Every analogy involves more difference than likeness
Famous passage from Lateran IV (Denzinger, no. 806 [432]): “When, then, he who is the Truth prays to the Father for his faithful ‘that they may be one in us as we also are one’, the word ‘one’ as applied to the disciples is to be taken in the sense of a union of charity in grace, but in the case of the Divine Persons in the sense of the unity of identity in nature.  In the same way, on another occasion the Truth says, ‘you must be perfect as your heavenly Father is perfect,” as though he were saying more explicitly, ‘You must be perfect’ in the perfection of grace, ‘as your heavenly Father is perfect’ in the perfection of nature, that is, each in his own way. Also: What kind of analogy does faith involve (“superanalogy”)
