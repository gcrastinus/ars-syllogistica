## The Predicables in Depth (`adv-pred`)

1. The mind first knows things: when we grasp what a tree is, the concept is not what we know but that by which we know the tree, and this is *direct* knowledge.
2. Afterward the mind can turn back on what it has grasped and consider the nature as it is in the mind, abstracted from this tree and that one and able to be said of many; this turning back is *reflex* knowledge, and it always comes second, since we know that we know only by first knowing something.
3. The predicables are found by reflex knowledge, because being a genus, being a species, and being said of many are relations that a nature has only as it is known, and the tradition calls them second intentions.
4. No genus exists outside the mind as one thing among others, since what exists outside the mind is this oak and that pine; so the logician studies the universal as it is in the mind, while the metaphysician asks what in things grounds it.
5. Porphyry, a third-century Neoplatonist who wrote the standard introduction to Aristotle’s *Categories*, set out one line of the category of substance from the top down, and this series, drawn on a page with the differences branching to either side, became known as the tree of Porphyry.
6. In the tree, substance is divided into body and spirit, body into living and non-living body, living body into animal and plant, and animal into rational and irrational animal; and rational animal gives man, under whom are Socrates, Plato, and every other man.
7. Each step of the tree is made by a difference, and each difference is one of a pair: corporeal or incorporeal, animate or inanimate, sentient or insentient, rational or irrational.
8. The genus at each step joined to its difference gives the species at the next, so that “animal” is “living body” with the difference “sentient,” and reading the tree upward gives the definition of man by its successive genera and differences.
9. In the tree some terms are only genera, some only species, and some both: the **supreme genus** (substance) has no genus above it, and so it is a genus and never a species; the **lowest species** (man) has no species below it, only individuals, and so it is a species and never a genus.
10. Every term between them is a **subaltern** genus, that is, a species of the term above it and a genus of the term below it, as animal is a species of living body and a genus of rational animal.
11. The individual stands below the lowest species and is neither genus nor species, since it is not said of many; so Socrates is a man, but nothing is a Socrates.
12. The supreme genus cannot be defined by a genus and difference, because it has no genus, and the individual cannot be defined at all, because a definition states what is common, and the individual is known by the senses and named, not defined.
13. Porphyry distinguishes four senses of “property”: in the first, a property belongs to one species only, but not to every member of it (being a physician belongs to man alone, but not to every man); in the second, it belongs to every member, but not to that species only (being two-footed belongs to every man and also to birds).
14. In the third sense, a property belongs to every member and only to that species, but not always (turning gray in old age); in the fourth and strict sense, it belongs to every member, only to that species, and always, as the power to laugh belongs to man.
15. Only a property in the fourth sense converts with its species (whatever can laugh is a man, and every man can laugh), and so the tradition calls it property in the proper sense; the exercise on the predicables uses this fourth sense, and the other three are properties only in a qualified way.
16. The word “accident” is used in logic in two related senses; among the categories, the nine accidents are the ways of being that exist *in* a substance and cannot exist on their own, such as quantity, quality, and relation, and this is **predicamental** accident, the accident of the categories.
17. Among the predicables, an accident is anything said of a subject that is no part of its essence and does not follow from it, and this is **predicable** accident.
18. The two do not always coincide: rationality is a quality, and so a predicamental accident if we place it among the categories, but it is no predicable accident of man, since it belongs to his definition.
19. The predicable accident can be said of an accident as well as of a substance (in “This large area is white,” white is a predicable accident of the quantity), and St. Thomas makes the same distinction when he notes that the soul’s powers are accidents in the first sense but not in the second.
20. A specific difference does two things at once: it **divides** the genus, separating the species that have it from those that lack it, as “rational” separates man from the brutes, and it **constitutes** the species, making it what it is, as “rational” joined to “animal” makes man.
21. Cajetan, the sixteenth-century Dominican commentator on Porphyry and on St. Thomas, notes that the difference divides the genus not by cutting its essence in pieces, since the whole nature of animal is found in man and in the horse, but by dividing the species that fall under it.
22. A property also marks off one species from another, but it does not constitute the species, because it flows from a species already constituted (the power to laugh follows from man’s being rational); so in seeking a definition we look for the difference, and we use the property as a sign that leads us to it.
23. Aristotle does not list five predicables; in the *Topics* he considers every predicate in relation to a subject and finds four, according as the predicate converts with its subject or not, and states the essence or not, and the central books of the *Topics* are arranged under these four heads.
24. If a predicate converts and states the essence, it is a **definition**; if it converts and does not state the essence, it is a **property**; if it does not convert and belongs to the essence, it is a **genus** (the difference is counted with the genus); and if it does not convert and is outside the essence, it is an **accident**.
25. Porphyry’s five are reached from a different question, namely how a universal is related to the things under it, and so the species and the difference appear in his list, while the definition does not.
26. The two lists do not contradict each other, because Aristotle’s is the list of the dialectician, who asks how a predicate stands to a subject in an argument, and Porphyry’s is the list of the logician who orders universals for definition and classification.
27. The question of universals asks what in reality grounds our general concepts, and three answers are usually distinguished; the first, **absolute realism**, associated with Plato, holds that the natures we grasp exist apart from things and from minds as universals.
28. **Nominalism** holds that only individuals exist and that general words and concepts are names for groups of individuals, with nothing common in the things themselves; **moderate realism**, the view of Aristotle and St. Thomas, holds that the nature exists in individuals, really but as individual, and becomes universal only in the mind, which abstracts it.
29. If nominalism is true, a universal proposition such as “Every man is mortal” is only a summary of the cases observed, and a definition states no essence; on the moderate realist view, a definition can state what a thing is and a demonstration can show why it must be so, and nominalism, which became dominant in the later Middle Ages, is one of the sources of the modern doubt about definition and demonstration.

## The Categories in Depth (`adv-cat`)

1. Before the categories, Aristotle distinguishes the ways a name can be said of several things: things are named **equivocally** when they share a name but the definition that goes with the name differs for each, as “animal” is said of a man and of a man painted in a picture.
2. Things are named **univocally** when they share both the name and its definition, as “animal” is said of a man and of an ox, and they are named **derivatively** (denominatively) when they take their name from something else, with a change of ending, as the grammarian is named from grammar.
3. These distinctions come first because the categories sort what is said univocally, so an equivocal name must be divided into its senses, and each sense placed in its own category, before anything can be classified.
4. Between the univocal and the equivocal stands the **analogous** name, which signifies different natures but is said first of one and of the others by reference to the first.
5. In analogy by **attribution**, the secondary things are named from their relation to the first, as “healthy” is said first of the animal, which has health, and then of food, which causes health, and of a complexion, which is a sign of it.
6. In analogy by **proportion**, the name is said of each thing in its own way, according to a likeness of relations, as “being” is said of substance and of accident, each according to its own manner of being, and “good” is said of a good knife and a good man, each good as it fulfills its own end.
7. When the likeness of relations is only figurative, as when a brave man is called a lion, the analogy is **metaphor**; and many of the most important words in philosophy, such as “being,” “good,” and “true,” are analogous, and so neither univocal nor merely equivocal.
8. Aristotle also distinguishes two ways in which one thing can stand to a subject: to be **said of** a subject is to be predicable of it, as a universal is said of what falls under it, and to be **present in** a subject is to exist in it, not as a part, but as something that cannot exist apart from it, as a color exists in a body.
9. The first is a distinction in the order of knowing, and the second is a distinction in the order of being.
10. Combining the two gives four classes: what is said of a subject and present in none is **universal substance** (“man”), and what is neither said of a subject nor present in one is **singular substance** (this man).
11. What is said of a subject and present in one is **universal accident** (“white”), and what is present in a subject but said of none is **singular accident** (the whiteness of this wall); only the universals are placed in the categories, as genera and species of their kinds.
12. The tradition sets five conditions for placing a nature in a category: it must be one simple nature, signified by a simple expression (“white horse” signifies two natures and belongs in no single category); and it must be a natural thing, not an artifact as such, since an artifact is a shape given by the mind of the maker to some material.
13. The nature must also be signified univocally; it must be universal, since a category is a highest predicate; and it must be finite, so that God, who is not limited to any kind of being, is in no category.
14. So a chair is a substance with respect to its wood, but the chair as a chair is outside the categories; Socrates is not in the category of substance as a predicate, but under it as the ultimate subject of predication; and an analogous word such as “being” is in no single category, since it is said of all of them, each in its own way.
15. The categories are the ten highest genera of what can be said of a thing: substance, quantity, quality, relation, action, passion, when, where, posture, and having (also called habit).
16. As modes of being the categories belong to metaphysics, but logic studies them as modes of predication, that is, as the highest kinds under which predicates are ordered; logic does not tell us what a man or a color is, but it supplies the order in which natures are classified, and knowing the supreme genus of a thing is the first step toward its definition.
17. Being itself is not an eleventh and highest genus above the ten, because a genus is divided by differences that lie outside it, as “rational” lies outside the nature of animal, but nothing lies outside being, since every difference is itself something that is.
18. So St. Thomas, following Aristotle’s *Metaphysics*, holds that being cannot be the genus of anything, and each category is a highest genus in its own line.
19. After the categories come the postpredicaments, the relations that appear once natures are placed in categories, and the first is opposition, of which there are four kinds, in order of decreasing strength.
20. **Contradictory** opposition sets a thing against its simple negation, as man against non-man, with nothing between them; **privative** opposition sets a form against its absence in a subject that ought naturally to have it, as hearing against deafness in an animal.
21. **Contrary** opposition sets two positive extremes of one genus against each other in the same subject, as odd and even in number, or white and black in color, and some contraries have intermediates while others do not.
22. **Relative** opposition, the weakest, sets two correlatives against each other, as whole and part, which exclude each other only in the same respect; only contradiction is found in every category, while privation and contrariety are found in several, and relative opposition chiefly in the category of relation.
23. Aristotle counts the senses of “prior”: a thing is prior in **time**, as the older is prior to the younger; it is prior when the **sequence of being cannot be reversed**, as one is prior to two, since if there are two, there is one, but not conversely; and it is prior in **order**, as the elements of geometry come before the theorems.
24. A thing is also prior in **excellence**, as the better is said to come first; and where two things imply each other, the one that is the **cause** is prior, as a man’s being is the cause of the truth of “There is a man,” and not the reverse.
25. Things are **simultaneous** in time when they come to be at once, and simultaneous by nature when each implies the other and neither causes the other (the double and the half), or when they are species divided from one genus by the same division (the winged, the land, and the water animals); but the genus is prior to its species, since if there is a water animal there is an animal, but not conversely.
26. Aristotle counts six kinds of motion: generation, destruction, increase, diminution, alteration, and change of place; they are distinct, since a thing can be altered in quality without growing, and a square can grow by an added figure without being altered.
27. Rest is the contrary of motion in general, and each kind of motion has its contrary: destruction of generation, diminution of increase, and motion downward of motion upward.
28. Aristotle also counts the senses of “to have”: we have a quality, a quantity, clothing, a part, a content, a possession, and a husband or a wife, the most remote sense; but only what is worn or carried on the body is the category of having, so “He has a cloak” falls under having, while “He has courage” falls under quality, though the same verb is used for both.

## Definition and Division in Depth (`adv-defdiv`)

1. Aristotle observes that every question we ask about things is one of four kinds: of a single term we ask *whether it is* and *what it is*, and of a subject and predicate together we ask *whether it is so* and *why it is so* (“Is the moon eclipsed?” and “Why is the moon eclipsed?”).
2. The first two questions concern the first act of the mind, and the second two concern judgment and reasoning.
3. A definition answers the question “What is it?”, but we cannot ask what a thing is until we know what its name means, and we do not seriously ask what it is until we know that it is; so the nominal definition comes first, the question of existence next, and the real definition after them.
4. The four questions also show why definition and demonstration belong together, because the answer to “What is it?” often turns out to be the answer to “Why is it so?”
5. Plato’s *Sophist* shows how division can lead to a definition: seeking to define the angler, the speakers divide art into making and acquiring, acquiring into exchange and taking by force, taking by force into open fighting and hunting, and so on, and at the end the definition of angling gathers up the differences found along the way.
6. Each division keeps one member and divides it again, and every member kept stays within the first genus, so the definition is reached by narrowing one genus step by step, and each step contributes one difference.
7. The genera all fall in one category (in the angler, art, a kind of quality), even when the differences that divide them, such as “living” and “lifeless,” are taken from elsewhere.
8. A division made to reach a definition must keep three requirements: it must state the essence, dividing by differences that belong to the genus as such and not by accidents; it must be **ordered**, so that no step is skipped and each member contains everything under the step before it; and it must be **complete**, continuing until it reaches a lowest species, which is divided only into individuals.
9. A division that skips a step leaves something out: if animal is divided at once into the feather-winged and the membrane-winged, the animals that do not fly fall under neither member, so the division should first separate the flying from the walking and the swimming, and only then divide the flying.
10. John of St. Thomas makes the same point, that substance is not well divided at once into man, plant, and angel, but in order, into corporeal and spiritual, and corporeal into living and non-living; and where the members are not immediate and equal, the division should have two members, or as few as possible.
11. Often no single known difference marks off a species, and then we gather several characteristics, each of which belongs to more than the species but none of which goes outside its genus, until together they belong to that species alone.
12. Aristotle’s example is the number three, which is odd, which it shares with other odd numbers; prime, which it shares with five and seven; and not the sum of other numbers, which it shares with two; together these belong to three alone, and they lead toward its definition.
13. The characteristics gathered this way are signs of the difference, not yet the difference itself, and the hope is to reach the single difference that explains why they occur together.
14. We use such signs instead of the property because a property is known to be proper only after the species is known, and so the search for a definition usually begins from what is outward and common, and moves inward toward what is essential.
15. A second method, definition by likeness, begins from individuals rather than from the genus: we take a group of things called by one name and find what they have in common, then another group called by the same name, and then ask whether the two common features share something further.
16. Aristotle’s example is magnanimity: Alcibiades, Achilles, and Ajax would not bear an insult, and Lysander and Socrates were indifferent to good and bad fortune; if something is common to both, such as thinking oneself worthy of great things, that is the definition, and if nothing is, there are two kinds of magnanimity.
17. This method proceeds from what is easier for us to grasp, the single cases, and it guards against equivocation, since it compares small groups before forming a general notion; its one rule is that the likeness must be taken in the same sense throughout, because things alike only by metaphor do not share an essence.
18. A strict definition gives a genus and a difference, and so some things cannot be defined in the strict sense: the highest genera, such as substance and quality, have no genus above them, and they are made known by examples and by their properties.
19. Individuals cannot be defined, because a definition states what is common, and the individual is reached by the senses and named; and being and the other notions said of every category cannot be defined by genus and difference either, since nothing lies outside them.
20. Accidents are defined, but in a special way: since an accident exists only in a subject, its subject enters its definition from outside its own genus (“snub” is defined as a nose that is concave), and so the tradition says that an accident has a definition by addition, and that the substance alone is defined in the full and proper sense.
21. Because to define a thing is to say what makes it what it is, a full definition can name its causes, and St. Thomas gathers the definition of virtue in this way: virtue is a good quality (its formal cause, with “quality” as its genus) of the mind (its material cause, the subject in which it exists).
22. Virtue is a good quality of the mind by which one lives rightly and which no one uses badly (its final cause, the good act), which God works in us without us (its efficient cause, said of infused virtue).
23. Not every definition names all four causes, and the best definition names the one that explains the rest; in natural things the form is usually that cause, and in things made for a purpose it is usually the end.
24. This is why the tradition sets the essential definition above the causal one but values both, because the causal definition by the outside causes (the maker and the end) often leads us to the essential one.
25. Aristotle shows that the same account can appear as a definition and as a demonstration: an eclipse of the moon is “a loss of light from the moon,” which states the effect, or “a loss of light from the moon caused by the earth’s coming between it and the sun,” which states the cause.
26. Put in order, the cause is the middle term of a demonstration whose conclusion is the effect: the moon is screened by the earth, and what is so screened loses its light, so the moon loses its light.
27. So there are three kinds of definition in relation to demonstration: the definition that states the cause, which serves as the middle term; the definition that states the effect, which is the conclusion; and the definition of the first principles, which is not demonstrated at all but understood; and the definition through the cause is the most perfect, since it says not only what the thing is but why it is so.

## Supposition (`adv-supp`)

1. In “Man is a species,” “Man has three letters,” and “Peter is a man,” the word “man” means the same thing each time, a rational animal, yet it stands for something different each time, since we cannot conclude that Peter is a species or that Peter has three letters.
2. The tradition therefore distinguishes a term’s **signification**, what it makes us understand, from its **supposition** (Latin *suppositio*), what it stands for in a proposition; a term has its signification by itself and always, but it has supposition only within a proposition.
3. John of St. Thomas defines supposition as the use of a term for that of which it is verified, according to what the copula requires, and it is chiefly the predicate that decides what the subject stands for: “species” makes “man” stand for the nature, “three letters” for the word, and “is Peter” for individual men.
4. A term has **material** supposition when it stands for itself, as a spoken or written word (in “‘Lamb’ is a four-letter word,” the term stands for the word “lamb”), and **formal** supposition when it stands for what it signifies; in writing, material supposition is usually marked with quotation marks.
5. Formal supposition is **proper** when the term stands for what it signifies in its proper sense, and **improper** when it stands for something by a figure of speech, as “Lamb” stands for Christ in “The Lamb was offered for the sins of the world.”
6. An argument that treats improper supposition as proper fails, as in “Every lamb has four legs; the Lamb was offered for the world; so something with four legs was offered for the world.”
7. Formal supposition is **simple** when a common term stands for the nature it signifies, and not for the individuals that have it, as in “Man is a species,” where the subject stands for the nature as known, since no individual man is a species.
8. Formal supposition is **personal** when the term stands for the individuals that have the nature, as in “Man is mortal,” which is true of each man; and “personal” here has nothing to do with persons, since a stone has personal supposition in “Some stone is heavy.”
9. The medieval logicians agreed on the name “simple supposition” but not on what it reaches: realists such as Walter Burley, an English logician of the fourteenth century, held that the term stands for the common nature found in each individual, and nominalists such as William of Ockham, the English Franciscan, held that it stands for the concept in the mind.
10. Either way, the inference from “Man is a species” to “Socrates is a species” fails, because the term “man” changes what it stands for.
11. Personal supposition is **singular** (or discrete) when the term stands for one individual, as “Socrates” or “this man,” and **common** when a common term stands for many.
12. Common supposition is **determinate** when we may descend to the individuals by “or” (“Some man is running” means that this man is running, or that one, or that other one), and **distributive** when we may descend by “and” (“Every man is mortal” means that this man is mortal, and that one, and that other one).
13. The descent shows what the proposition requires to be true: a determinate term needs only one of the individuals to make the proposition true, and a distributive term needs every one of them.
14. From the singulars we may also ascend, from “This man is running” to “Some man is running,” but not from “This man is mortal” to “Every man is mortal,” since one instance does not establish all of them.
15. Some common terms allow neither descent: the predicate of “Every man is an animal” allows only “Every man is this animal or that animal or that other one,” with the “or” inside the predicate, and this is **merely confused** supposition, the supposition of the predicate of a universal affirmative.
16. A term has **collective** supposition when it stands for many things taken together as one subject, as in “The apostles were twelve,” where we cannot descend to “Peter was twelve, and Paul was twelve”; so “All the soldiers are brave” is said of each soldier, while “All the soldiers surrounded the fort” is said of them together.
17. The modern word “distributed” names what the medieval logicians called common, confused, and distributive supposition: in A, “Every S is P,” the subject is distributive and the predicate merely confused, and in E, “No S is P,” both terms are distributive.
18. In I, “Some S is P,” both terms are determinate, and in O, “Some S is not P,” the subject is determinate and the predicate distributive; these are the rules of distribution that the exercises on the syllogism use.
19. The modes of supposition also explain conversion: E and I convert simply because their two terms have the same mode of supposition, so exchanging them changes nothing, but A and O do not, because their terms have different modes, and a change in a term’s supposition is enough to spoil an inference.
20. So the predicate of an affirmative is never taken universally, and the predicate of a negative is always taken universally.
21. Supposition also depends on the time and the manner of being signified by the copula: an affirmative proposition whose subject stands for nothing at the time signified is false (“Napoleon will be emperor,” said now), but a negative proposition can be true even when its subject stands for nothing (“Napoleon is not in Paris”).
22. This is the ground of the classical rule that affirmatives carry existential import and negatives do not.
23. Maritain distinguishes **natural** supposition, where the predicate belongs to the subject by its essence and the copula abstracts from time (“Every triangle has three sides” holds of triangles as possible), from **accidental** supposition, where the predicate belongs only by accident and the copula signifies actual existence (“Peter is running” is false if Peter does not now exist).
24. Words such as “can,” “will,” and “was” **enlarge** a term to possible, future, or past things (ampliation), and qualifiers such as “the poor” or “of this town” **restrict** it (restriction).
25. Since a valid inference needs each term to stand for the same things throughout, an argument fails when a term’s kind of supposition changes from one premise to another, as “The logical categories were set out by Aristotle; quantity is a logical category; so quantity was set out by Aristotle” fails, because quantity itself, as a way of being, was not invented by anyone.
26. The same rule covers a change in the manner of existence: from premises that speak of the centaur only as imagined we may conclude that some creature of fable is half man, half horse, but not that such a creature really exists; and many errors that look like failures of form are failures of supposition, which the disputations remedied by distinguishing what each term stands for.

## Opposition and Conversion in Depth (`adv-opp`)

1. It is natural to think that contraries (“Every man is just” and “No man is just”) are the most opposed of propositions, but the tradition holds that **contradiction** is the strongest opposition, because the one proposition simply denies what the other affirms, and the two agree in nothing.
2. Contraries still agree in something, since both take the subject universally, and so both can be false together.
3. Subalternation, between A and I or between E and O, is not opposition in the proper sense at all, because the subaltern says the same thing as its universal, only partially, as “Some man is mortal” repeats in part what “Every man is mortal” says.
4. Subalternation is placed on the square of opposition for the sake of symmetry, so that all the relations between propositions with the same terms can be seen together, but Aristotle’s own account of opposition does not include it.
5. The square assumes a universal and a particular subject, but a singular proposition has only one kind of opposition: “Peter is wise” and “Peter is not wise” are contradictories, and there is no singular that could be their contrary or their subaltern, so two singulars of opposite quality cannot both be true or both be false.
6. The same holds when a universal nature is taken as one thing, as in “Man is the noblest of bodily creatures” and its negation, and St. Thomas explains that here the predicate is said of the nature as it is one in the mind, though what is said belongs to the nature as it is in things; so such propositions behave like singulars and admit only contradiction.
7. The laws of opposition hold by form, whatever the terms: contraries cannot both be true, subcontraries cannot both be false, and contradictories divide truth and falsity between them.
8. The matter can add more: in **necessary matter**, where the predicate belongs to the subject by its essence, contraries cannot both be false and subcontraries cannot both be true (“Every man is mortal” is true, and “No man is mortal” and “Some man is not mortal” are false).
9. In **contingent matter**, where the predicate may or may not belong, contraries are usually both false and subcontraries both true (“Every man is just” and “No man is just” are false, while “Some man is just” and “Some man is not just” are true).
10. Only in necessary matter may we conclude from the truth of one subcontrary to the falsity of the other, and an argument that does so in contingent matter mistakes a feature of the matter for a law of the form.
11. Aristotle asks whether “There will be a sea battle tomorrow” and “There will not be a sea battle tomorrow” must be one true and the other false now, since if one were already true, the battle would be already fixed, and deliberation would be useless.
12. His answer, which St. Thomas develops in his commentary, is that such propositions about a contingent future are not yet determinately true or false, since their truth depends on things that do not yet exist even in their causes.
13. The law of contradiction is not abandoned, because the disjunction “Either there will be a sea battle tomorrow or there will not” is necessarily true now, and what is not yet determined is which part is true; so the two propositions exclude each other, but neither can yet be called the true one.
14. This applies only to the contingent future, since “The sun will rise tomorrow” concerns what follows from causes already at work, and “There was a battle yesterday” is already determinately true or false.
15. To convert a proposition is to exchange its terms so as to say the same truth, and the term that becomes the subject must keep the extension it had as the predicate, so the rules of conversion follow from the supposition of the terms.
16. In E both terms are distributive, and in I both are determinate, so E and I convert simply; in A the subject is distributive and the predicate merely confused, so A converts only by limitation (“Every man is mortal” gives “Some mortal is a man”).
17. O does not convert at all, because in “Some man is not just” the predicate “just” stands for just men, from whom some man is excluded, while in “Some just thing is not a man” the subject could stand for something other than a man, such as God, so the supposed converse does not say the same truth.
18. O converts only by contraposition: “Some man is not just” gives “Some non-just thing is not a non-man,” that is, some non-just thing is a man.
19. John Buridan, the fourteenth-century master at Paris, noticed that contraposition and obversion need a further condition when the affirmatives carry existential import: “Every man is a being” is true, but its contrapositive “Every non-being is a non-man” is false, because nothing is a non-being, and an affirmative whose subject stands for nothing is false.
20. So contraposition of A and of O holds only when the terms stand for things that actually exist.
21. Obversion moves safely from the affirmative side of the square to the negative (“Every lawyer is learned” gives “No lawyer is non-learned”), but it moves from the negative to the affirmative only when the subject exists, since “No chimera is a woman” is true, while “Every chimera is a non-woman” is false; this is the course’s doctrine of existential import applied to the operations on a single proposition.
22. Conversion, obversion, subalternation, and the passage from a proposition to the falsity of its contradictory are often called immediate inferences, but Maritain argues that none of them is an inference in the proper sense, because in each the mind says the same truth in a second form and does not advance from a known truth to one not yet known.
23. So “No man is an angel” and “No angel is a man” are two ways of stating one separation of the two terms.
24. An inference in the proper sense reaches a new truth, and for that the mind must relate one known truth to another, so every inference properly so called has at least two premises and three terms.
25. The immediate operations are still useful, since they let us restate a premise so that it fits an argument, and the logician can verify them by a syllogism, but the verification comes after the mind has already seen that the two forms say the same thing.
26. The **reciprocal** of a universal affirmative exchanges its terms and keeps the universal quantity (the reciprocal of “Every square is a rectangle” is “Every rectangle is a square”), and since the predicate of an affirmative is taken particularly, the reciprocal says a different truth and must be proved on its own; sometimes it is true, and sometimes it is false.
27. The **inverse** negates both the subject and the copula (the inverse of “Every man is an animal” is “Every non-man is not an animal”), and it says in negative form the same thing as the reciprocal; to pass from a universal affirmative to its reciprocal is a common error.
28. Modern predicate logic, developed by Gottlob Frege, the German mathematician and logician, writes “All men are mortal” as “For anything whatever, if it is a man, it is mortal,” and on this reading the universal is true when there are no men at all, since a conditional with a false antecedent is counted true.
29. So on the modern reading the A proposition carries no existential import, the step from “All S are P” to “Some S are P” fails, and the square loses its subalternation and contrariety.
30. The tradition answers in two ways: first, the formula represents the universal without saying what it means, since “All men are mortal” attributes mortality to man as such; second, as Gyula Klima and Timothy B. Noone point out, the medieval logicians did see the problem of empty terms, and placed existence in the affirmatives, and contemporary Aristotelians such as Klima and Terence Parsons have shown that the older square can be stated formally without contradiction.

## The Modal Proposition in Depth (`adv-modp`)

1. A mode is a determination added to something, and some modes affect only the subject (“The prudent man is wise”) and some only the predicate (“Peter runs fast”), but neither makes a proposition modal.
2. A proposition is modal only when the mode affects the copula itself, that is, the way in which the predicate belongs to the subject, as in “Peter is necessarily a man.”
3. There are four such modes: necessity (it cannot not be), impossibility (it cannot be), possibility (it can be), and contingency (it can be and can also not be).
4. Aristotle treats only two, the necessary and the contingent, because the impossible is the necessary with a negation (necessary that it not be), and the possible, in the sense he uses there, goes with the contingent; and a proposition with no mode of the copula, such as “Man is rational,” is called simply attributive (Latin *de inesse*, “of being in”).
5. A modal proposition has two qualities, that of the mode and that of the dictum, and it is affirmative when both are affirmative or both are negative, and negative when one is affirmative and the other negative.
6. So “It is impossible that it not be,” with a negative mode and a negative dictum, is affirmative, and it comes to “It is necessary that it be”; counting “impossible” as “not possible” makes this rule work.
7. The modes also have a kind of quantity: necessity and impossibility are like universals, since what must be or cannot be holds in every case, and possibility and contingency are like particulars, since what can be need hold only sometimes.
8. This is why the four modes stand at the four corners of a square: “necessary” with the universal affirmative, “impossible” with the universal negative, “possible” with the particular affirmative, and “possible not” with the particular negative.
9. When the dictum itself has a universal or particular subject, the modal square grows to eight corners (“It is necessary that every man be mortal,” “It is necessary that some man be mortal,” and so on), each standing in fixed relations to the others.
10. The relations still follow the pattern of the square: what is necessary of every subject is necessary of some, and what is possible of some is not thereby possible of every.
11. The ordinary wording can mislead, because “It is impossible that every man run” means only “It is not possible that every man run,” the contradictory of “It is possible that every man run”; to say that no man can run, we must say “It is impossible that any man run,” or simply “No man can run.”
12. Maritain gives a serious example: from the falsity of “Every error can be avoided without revelation,” it follows only that some error cannot be so avoided, not that none can.
13. To keep the combinations of mode and dictum in view, the scholastics marked each with a vowel: A when both mode and dictum are affirmative, E when the dictum is negative and the mode affirmative, I when the mode is negative and the dictum affirmative, and U when both are negative.
14. Each of the four words Purpurea, Iliace, Amabimus, and Edentuli lists, in order, the forms with the modes possible, contingent, impossible, and necessary that say the same thing, so Purpurea gathers “not possible that not,” “not contingent that not,” “impossible that not,” and “necessary that it be,” which are equipollent.
15. The words are an aid to memory, and what they record is the rule that a negation before the mode moves to the contradictory corner, and a negation after the mode denies only the dictum.
16. A modal can be read in two ways: read as said **of what is said** (*de dicto*), the mode governs the whole proposition, and read as said **of the thing** (*de re*), the mode is said of the thing the subject stands for.
17. So “It is necessary that whatever God knows is true” says that the proposition “What God knows is true” cannot be false, while “Whatever God knows is necessarily true” would say that each thing God knows is necessary in itself.
18. St. Thomas uses the distinction to answer an objection that God’s knowledge makes all things necessary: “Everything known by God is necessarily so” is true of what is said and false of the thing, because God knows contingent things as contingent, and what is necessary is the truth of the whole statement, not each thing known.
19. The distinction runs close to that of the composite and divided senses, and it decides many arguments that seem to make free acts necessary.
20. Aristotle distinguishes two senses of “possible”: in one sense the possible is whatever is not impossible, and in this sense the necessary is also possible, since what must be certainly can be; in the other, stricter sense the possible is what can be and can also not be, which is the **contingent**, and in this sense the necessary is not possible.
21. Much confusion comes from passing between the two senses, since from “It is necessary that fire is hot” it follows that it is possible in the first sense, but not that it is contingent; and the course takes “contingent” in the strict sense, what can be and can also not be.
22. Aristotle’s modes concern the way the predicate belongs to the subject, but Immanuel Kant, the eighteenth-century German philosopher, divided judgments by their modality as problematic, assertoric, and apodictic, meaning the possibility or necessity of the act of judging, not of the relation judged; and Maritain points out that Kant also counts the simple assertion as a mode, though in it nothing modifies the copula.
23. For the tradition, “Man is necessarily an animal” states something about man and animal, not about the certainty of the one who judges, since one can be certain of a contingent fact and uncertain of a necessary truth; and a further sign that the mode belongs to the proposition is that conversion keeps it.

## Compound and Exponible Propositions (`adv-comp`)

1. A simple, or categorical, proposition joins two terms by the copula “is” (“Man is mortal”), while a **compound**, or hypothetical, proposition joins two propositions by a connecting word other than the verb, such as “and,” “or,” or “if.”
2. A compound proposition states a new truth of its own, which depends on the truths of its parts but is not the same as any of them.
3. A compound is **openly** compound when its structure shows two propositions joined, and **hiddenly** compound (exponible) when it looks simple but contains a word, such as “only,” “except,” or “as such,” that makes it equivalent to several propositions, as “Only man laughs” says that man laughs and that nothing else does.
4. A **copulative** proposition joins its parts by “and,” and it is true only when every part is true, and false when any one part is false (“The moon moves and the earth stands still” is false because its second part is false).
5. So from a true copulative we may assert either part by itself, but from a true part we may not assert the copulative, and from a false copulative we may not deny any particular part, since we do not know which one is false.
6. Some copulatives are hidden in a joined subject or predicate (“Peter and Paul died at Rome”), but a joined subject is not always copulative, since “Peter and Paul are friends” says something of the two together, and so it is a simple proposition with one subject.
7. John of St. Thomas notes that the copulative follows its weaker part: if any part is false, or only probable, the whole is no stronger than that part.
8. A **disjunctive** proposition joins its parts by “or”; in the strict sense it says that the parts cannot be true together and cannot be false together (“A number is either odd or even”), and in the broad sense it says only that at least one part is true.
9. Either way, the disjunctive is true when one part is true, and so it follows its stronger part; an improper disjunctive merely offers alternatives (“You may come by train or on foot”), and denying one of these posits nothing.
10. The **conjunctive** proposition denies that two propositions are true together, without denying that both may be false (“No one can be both a judge and a party in the same case”).
11. Where there is no middle between the two parts, the conjunctive says the same thing as a disjunctive, as “No one can serve both God and Mammon” amounts to “One serves either God or Mammon,” since on the Gospel’s account there is no middle between the two.
12. A **conditional** proposition joins its parts by “if,” and it does not assert either part but asserts only that the second follows from the first.
13. So a conditional is true when the sequence holds, even if both parts are false (“If twenty were odd, it could not be divided by two”), and false when the sequence fails, even if both parts are true (“If England is an island, two and two make four”).
14. This is why a conditional can be asserted without asserting its condition, as St. Paul could write that if an angel from heaven preached another gospel, “let him be anathema,” without thinking that an angel would do so.
15. Maritain distinguishes conditionals in the strict sense, where “if” signifies a necessary sequence, from conditionals in a broad sense, where it signifies only a regular connection in fact, and a broad conditional is true only if its parts are in fact verified.
16. Other compounds can be resolved into categorical propositions: a **causal** proposition, such as “The soul is immortal because it is spiritual,” asserts that the soul is immortal, that it is spiritual, and that its spirituality is the cause of its immortality, so, unlike a conditional, it requires the truth of both its parts.
17. A **rational** proposition, such as “The soul is spiritual, therefore it is immortal,” is in fact a short argument, an enthymeme.
18. A **relative** proposition may have a causal sense (“The soul, which is spiritual, is immortal”) or may amount to a copulative (“The man whom I see is running”); an **adversative** proposition, joined by “but,” may deny a causal link or may simply join two facts (“He laughs, but I weep”).
19. To contradict a compound we must deny exactly what it asserts, and so the contradictory of a copulative is a disjunctive of the contradictories of its parts: the contradictory of “Every rich man is sad and honored” is “Some rich man is not sad or not honored.”
20. The contradictory of a broad disjunctive is a copulative of the contradictories, as the contradictory of “Either the bridge is closed or the road is flooded” is “The bridge is not closed and the road is not flooded”; and modern logic states these two rules as De Morgan’s laws, after Augustus De Morgan, the nineteenth-century English logician.
21. The contradictory of a conditional denies the sequence, not the parts: the contradictory of “If he is rich, he is happy” is “It is not the case that if he is rich he is happy,” that is, he could be rich and not happy, and not “If he is rich, he is not happy,” which is a contrary claim.
22. An **exclusive** proposition contains a word such as “only” or “alone,” and it resolves into two propositions, as “Only the human species laughs” means that the human species laughs, and that no other species laughs; it is true only if both are true.
23. An **exceptive** proposition contains “except” or “but,” and it resolves into three, as “Every metal except mercury is solid at room temperature” means that every metal other than mercury is so solid, that mercury is a metal, and that mercury is not so solid.
24. The resolution shows how such propositions can be false: “Only citizens may vote” is false if a non-citizen may vote, even if every citizen may, and an exceptive is false if the excepted thing does not really belong to the subject (“Every bird except the bat flies”).
25. A **reduplicative** proposition contains “as,” “insofar as,” or “as such”: sometimes the word names the very nature of the subject, so that the predicate belongs to the subject by its own nature (“The colored, as colored, is visible”), and sometimes it names a different feature of the subject, which is the reason or condition for the predicate (“Man, as rational, can laugh”), and this is the reduplicative in the strict sense.
26. Mistaking a reduplicative for a simple proposition causes many errors: “The wicked, as wicked, are to be hated” does not say that the wicked are to be hated simply, since as men they are to be loved, and “Man as man is not greedy” differs from “Man as man is non-greedy,” since man as man is neither greedy nor free of greed.

## Figures and Moods (`adv-fig`)

1. A syllogism has a double matter, its terms and its propositions, and so a double form: the **figure** is the arrangement of the terms, fixed by the place of the middle term, which is subject of the major and predicate of the minor in the first figure, predicate of both in the second, and subject of both in the third.
2. The **mood** is the arrangement of the propositions by quantity and quality, named by the letters A, E, I, and O for the major, the minor, and the conclusion in order.
3. Each premise can be A, E, I, or O, so there are sixteen pairs of premises in each figure, and sixty-four combinations in the three figures together with the indirect arrangement, and most of them break one of the general rules.
4. The scholastic account counts nineteen legitimate moods: four in the first figure, five indirect moods of the first figure, four in the second, and six in the third; the later textbook account counts the indirect moods as a fourth figure and adds the weakened moods, which conclude in particular what could be concluded universally.
5. In the first figure the major premise must be universal and the minor affirmative, because if the major were particular, the middle term would be distributed in neither premise, and if the minor were negative, the conclusion would be negative and would distribute the major term, which the affirmative major leaves undistributed.
6. So four moods remain in the first figure: AAA, EAE, AII, and EIO (Barbara, Celarent, Darii, Ferio).
7. These moods are called perfect because the principle of all syllogisms is seen in them directly, namely that what is said of every member of a class is said of whatever falls under it, and what is denied of every member is denied of whatever falls under it (the *dictum de omni et nullo*).
8. In the first figure the middle term is the subject of the major and the predicate of the minor, so the minor term is plainly placed under the middle, and the major term is said or denied of it through the middle.
9. In the second figure one premise must be negative and the major must be universal, because if both premises were affirmative, the middle term, which is the predicate of both, would be distributed in neither, and a particular major would leave the major term undistributed while the negative conclusion distributes it.
10. So four moods remain in the second figure: EAE, AEE, EIO, and AOO (Cesare, Camestres, Festino, Baroco), and every conclusion of the second figure is negative.
11. The principle of the second figure is that if an attribute is said or denied of every member of one class, whatever it cannot be said or denied of in the same way is not in that class (sometimes called the *dictum de diverso*), and so the second figure is suited to refutation and to exclusion.
12. In the third figure the minor premise must be affirmative and the conclusion particular, because a negative minor would make the conclusion negative and distribute the major term, which the affirmative major leaves undistributed, and a universal conclusion would distribute the minor term, which is the predicate of an affirmative minor and so undistributed.
13. So six moods remain in the third figure: AAI, EAO, IAI, AII, OAO, and EIO (Darapti, Felapton, Disamis, Datisi, Bocardo, Ferison).
14. The principle of the third figure is that two terms that share a common instance agree at least in part, and if one has a part the other lacks, they differ at least in part (sometimes called the *dictum de exemplo*); so the third figure is suited to the counterexample, which shows that a universal claim does not hold.
15. When the middle term is the predicate of the major and the subject of the minor, the syllogism concludes indirectly, and the term that is naturally the predicate becomes the grammatical subject of the conclusion, as in “Some mortal thing is Peter.”
16. The scholastics, following Aristotle, counted these as five indirect moods of the first figure (Baralipton, Celantes, Dabitis, Fapesmo, Frisesomorum), since they are the first figure with the conclusion turned around.
17. The later textbook account counts the same arrangement as a separate fourth figure, with the moods AAI, AEE, IAI, EAO, and EIO (Bramantip, Camenes, Dimaris, Fesapo, Fresison), and historians of logic report that this fourth figure was attributed to Galen, the second-century physician.
18. The Aristotelian and Thomist tradition, including St. Thomas and John of St. Thomas, treats it as the indirect first figure, and Maritain argues that a fourth figure attends to the words rather than to the thought, since in the mind the grammatical predicate of its conclusion is the real subject.
19. A singular minor premise counts as universal, since it speaks of the whole of its subject (“Socrates is a man” in Barbara), but a singular major does not, so “This man is a liar; Peter is a man; so Peter is a liar” does not conclude, since “man” in the minor is not the man spoken of in the major.
20. When the middle term itself is singular, the argument is an **expository syllogism**, which simply displays an individual in whom two terms meet (“Socrates is wise; Socrates is a man; so some man is wise”), and it is in the third figure, which suits it best.
21. Because the middle of an expository syllogism stands for one individual, it admits arrangements the ordinary syllogism forbids, such as two affirmatives in the second figure (“This apostle is Judas; this traitor is Judas; so this traitor is an apostle”).
22. It is often said that a syllogism moves from a more universal truth to a less universal one, and Maritain grants that this is true of the logical relations, since in the first figure the minor term is placed under the middle, but he denies that it is the essence of the syllogism.
23. The essence of the syllogism is the identification of two terms with one and the same third term, from which their identity with each other follows; the proof is that the conclusion can be as universal as the major, when its terms are convertible, and Aristotle and the scholastics took such syllogisms, where the middle term is the definition of the subject, as the model of demonstration.
24. Aristotle holds that the first figure is the most suited to science, because science seeks the reason why a property belongs to a subject, and only the first figure concludes universally and affirmatively, in Barbara, which is the form of “Every triangle has angles equal to two right angles.”
25. The second figure concludes only negatively, and the third only particularly, so neither can state that a property belongs to every member of a species; and the first figure is also the most direct, since the middle term stands between the extremes in extension, though the second keeps its use for showing what a thing is not, and the third for disproving a universal.
26. Pierre Conway, the twentieth-century Dominican teacher of Aristotelian logic, adds that Barbara and Celarent are the moods proper to demonstration, since through them a definition or a cause is shown to bring its property with it.

## Reduction (`adv-red`)

1. Aristotle calls the moods of the first figure **perfect**, because their force is evident from the premises alone: what is said of every member of a class is said of whatever falls under it.
2. He calls the moods of the other figures **imperfect**, because their force becomes evident through something not stated in the premises, such as the conversion of a premise or an argument from the impossible.
3. To **reduce** an imperfect mood is to show its validity by a perfect one, and every A conclusion is drawn most perfectly in AAA of the first figure (Barbara), every E conclusion in EAE (Celarent), every I in AII (Darii), and every O in EIO (Ferio).
4. Reduction is either **direct**, drawing the same conclusion in a first-figure mood, or **to the impossible**, showing that whoever grants the premises and denies the conclusion contradicts himself.
5. The Latin names of the moods record how each is reduced, and the first letter tells which perfect mood it goes to: a name beginning with B goes to Barbara, with C to Celarent, with D to Darii, and with F to Ferio.
6. Within the name, **s** after a vowel means that the proposition is converted simply; **p** means that it is converted by limitation (*per accidens*); **m** means that the premises are transposed; and **c** means that the mood is reduced to the impossible; the other letters, such as the r and the t, mark nothing.
7. The scholastics summed this up in a verse: “S vult simpliciter verti, P vero per accid, M vult mutari, C per impossibile duci”; so Cesare (EAE in the second figure) goes to Celarent by converting its major simply, and Datisi (AII in the third) goes to Darii by converting its minor simply.
8. To reduce Camestres (AEE in the second figure), as in “Every jealous man is bitter; no saint is bitter; so no saint is a jealous man,” the C sends it to Celarent, the m tells us to transpose the premises, and each s tells us to convert simply the proposition it follows, here the minor and the conclusion.
9. Transposing and converting gives “No bitter man is a saint” as the major and “Every jealous man is bitter” as the minor, with the conclusion “No jealous man is a saint,” which is Celarent; converting that conclusion simply gives back the original conclusion.
10. So the same conclusion has been drawn in a perfect mood, and the imperfect one is shown to be valid.
11. Some reductions need conversion by limitation: in Felapton (EAO in the third figure), as in “No animal is incorruptible; every animal is a living being; so some living being is not incorruptible,” the F sends it to Ferio, and the p after the second vowel tells us to convert the minor by limitation.
12. “Every animal is a living being” becomes “Some living being is an animal,” and with the major unchanged we have EIO in the first figure (Ferio); in the same way Darapti (AAI of the third figure) goes to Darii by converting its minor by limitation.
13. Two moods, Baroco (AOO in the second figure) and Bocardo (OAO in the third), cannot be reduced directly, since an O cannot be converted simply, and so they are reduced to the impossible.
14. We suppose an opponent who grants the premises but denies the conclusion; we take the contradictory of the conclusion, put it in place of the premise marked by the c, and draw a conclusion in Barbara that contradicts the other premise, which the opponent had granted.
15. So in Baroco (“Every fool is tiresome; some talker is not tiresome; so some talker is not a fool”), if the conclusion is denied, every talker is a fool, and with “Every fool is tiresome” this gives in Barbara “Every talker is tiresome,” which contradicts the granted minor; any imperfect mood can be reduced this way, but these two can be reduced in no other.
16. The indirect moods of the first figure are reduced by restoring to the first premise its proper role as the major, as Celantes (“No philosopher is an angel; Descartes is a philosopher; so no angel is Descartes”) becomes Celarent by reading the conclusion directly as “Descartes is not an angel,” and Baralipton becomes Barbara.
17. Maritain notes that this works because the indirect conclusion already was the direct one, stated with its terms turned around.
18. In the later account, where these moods form a fourth figure, they are reduced by transposition and conversion, as Bramantip (AAI of the fourth figure) goes to Barbara by transposing the premises and converting the conclusion by limitation.
19. Reduction does not mean that the imperfect moods borrow their validity from the first figure, since Maritain reads Aristotle as saying that their evidence depends on a principle more particular than the principle of the first figure, not that they prove nothing by themselves; a syllogism in the second figure concludes by its own form, and the reduction verifies it by showing the same truth in a perfect form.
20. The figures also keep their own uses, which reduction would hide, since the second figure is the natural form of a refutation and the third the natural form of a counterexample; so reduction is a test and an explanation of validity, not a rule that every argument should be rewritten in the first figure.

## The Modal Syllogism in Depth (`adv-mods`)

1. Aristotle holds that in the first figure a necessary major premise with a plain minor premise gives a necessary conclusion, and the reason is the principle that governs the first figure: if a predicate belongs necessarily to everything under the middle term, it belongs necessarily to whatever falls under the middle term, and the plain minor premise places the minor term under the middle.
2. So “Every metal necessarily conducts electricity; this wire is metal; so this wire necessarily conducts electricity” concludes necessarily, though the wire might have been made of something else, because the necessity in the conclusion is the necessity with which the property follows from being metal, and the minor premise contributes only that the wire falls under “metal.”
3. The reverse does not hold, since a plain major premise with a necessary minor premise gives only a plain conclusion; Aristotle’s example is that every man is necessarily an animal, and if every animal is in fact moving, it follows that every man is moving, but not that every man is necessarily moving.
4. The principle explains the difference, because the major premise states how the predicate belongs to everything under the middle term, and what is said of the middle is said of what falls under it in the same way.
5. A necessary minor only makes the minor term fall under the middle necessarily, and it cannot make the predicate belong to the middle any more firmly than the major says it does.
6. Every valid syllogism has a kind of necessity, since if the premises are true, the conclusion cannot be false, and St. Thomas calls this the **necessity of the consequence**, distinguishing it from the **necessity of the consequent**, the necessity of the conclusion taken by itself.
7. From contingent premises a conclusion follows necessarily, in the first sense, but the conclusion need not be necessary in the second sense; so “If Socrates is sitting, he is necessarily sitting” is true of the consequence and false of the consequent, since his sitting is not necessary.
8. A modal syllogism concerns the second kind of necessity, that is, whether the conclusion is itself necessary or contingent, not whether it follows.
9. The distinction between a modal read as said of what is said and as said of the thing decides some modal syllogisms, as in the old objection “Whatever God knows is necessarily so; God knows that this man will sin; so this man will necessarily sin,” which appeals to Aristotle’s first-figure rule, since its major premise is necessary and its minor plain.
10. St. Thomas answers by distinguishing the major: said of what is said, it is true, since the statement “What God knows is so” cannot be false; said of the thing, it is false, since the things God knows are not each necessary in themselves.
11. Read the first way, the major does not say that the predicate belongs necessarily to everything under the middle term, and so the rule does not apply; St. Thomas answers by distinguishing the premise, not by denying the rule.
12. The reduction of modal syllogisms depends on converting modal premises, and Aristotle sets out how they convert: a necessary universal negative converts simply (if it is necessary that no stone is an animal, it is necessary that no animal is a stone), and a necessary universal affirmative converts by limitation.
13. The contingent universal negative behaves differently, because “It is contingent that no man is white,” in the strict sense, means that any man may be white or not white, and it does not convert into “It is contingent that no white thing is a man.”
14. Aristotle shows this just before he finds that two contingent premises give no conclusion in the second figure, where such a conversion would be needed to reduce the syllogism.
15. Since “possible” has two senses, the conclusion of a modal syllogism can be possible in one sense and not in the other; in the first figure, when the major premise is plain and the minor contingent, Aristotle says that the syllogisms are imperfect and that the negative ones conclude not to contingency in the strict sense, but only that the predicate does not belong necessarily.
16. The difference is between “can be and can also not be” (contingent, or two-sided) and “need not be” (one-sided), and a conclusion of the second kind says less, since it denies necessity without asserting that the opposite can also hold.
17. Aristotle’s mixtures with contingent premises differ in this way from mood to mood, and each has to be examined on its own.
18. When both premises are necessary, the conclusion is necessary, and the general rules apply without difficulty, but the other mixtures led to so many distinctions that the theory of the modal syllogism has been called “the logicians’ cross” (*crux logicorum*), as Maritain reports.
19. The *Summa totius logicae*, once attributed to St. Thomas, follows Aristotle and lists the cases figure by figure.
20. St. Albert the Great, St. Thomas’s teacher, also follows Aristotle, but he adds that the plain minor premise must be true “simply” (*simpliciter*), by the nature of its terms, and not only “as of now” (*ut nunc*), meaning that the predicate belongs to the subject substantially; and historians of logic report that Robert Kilwardby, the thirteenth-century English Dominican, gave the same qualification.
21. Theophrastus, Aristotle’s successor at the Lyceum, held a simpler rule, that the conclusion always follows the weaker premise in mode, but the medieval commentators did not adopt it for the first figure, and this course follows Aristotle.

## Compound Arguments (`adv-carg`)

1. A syllogism is compound when it contains several syllogisms, stated or implied, and the simplest is the **epicheirema**, a syllogism in which one premise, or both, is joined to its proof (“Every martyr is a saint, because every martyr has heroic charity; Peter is a martyr; so Peter is a saint”).
2. The causal premise of an epicheirema is itself a syllogism in brief, and so in judging an epicheirema we judge each syllogism it contains, the stated one and the one hidden in the causal premise.
3. Aristotle used the word “epicheirema” in another sense, for a dialectical argument from probable premises, and the later sense may come from the fact that such premises invite further argument, and a premise that carries its own proof answers the invitation in advance.
4. A **polysyllogism** links several syllogisms so that the conclusion of one is a premise of the next, each stated in full.
5. A **sorites** links several propositions so that the predicate of each is the subject of the next, until a conclusion joins the subject of the first with the predicate of the last (“Peter is a man; every man is an animal; every animal has instincts; whatever has instincts reacts on its own; so Peter reacts on his own”).
6. A sorites contains as many syllogisms as it has premises, less one, but states none of their conclusions until the last, and its validity depends on the validity of every syllogism it contains.
7. Cicero called the sorites the most deceptive of arguments, because a defect in any link is easily hidden among the others; and Aristotle does not use the name, but he describes the reasoning in the *Prior Analytics*.
8. In the Aristotelian sorites the series runs from the minor premise toward the major, and each middle term is wider than the one before.
9. Rudolph Goclenius, a sixteenth-century German logician, gave his name to the reverse form, which begins with the premise that contains the predicate of the conclusion and ends with the one that contains its subject.
10. Each form has its rules, which follow from the rules of the syllogism: in the Aristotelian sorites only the first premise may be particular and only the last may be negative, and in the Goclenian sorites the rules are reversed, so that only the last premise may be particular and only the first may be negative.
11. A particular premise elsewhere leaves a middle term undistributed, and a negative premise elsewhere lets the major term be distributed in the conclusion when it is not distributed in its premise.
12. A **dilemma** sets out a disjunction such that, whichever member is taken, the same conclusion follows, and it is usually built on conditionals, as in Maritain’s example of a conscience formed badly through the man’s own fault: if he does the act his conscience commands, he acts against the eternal law, and if he does not, he acts against his conscience, so either way he does wrong.
13. A dilemma can also be categorical (“Every just man needs grace to persevere; every sinner needs grace to be converted; every man is just or a sinner; so every man needs grace”).
14. The dilemma is chiefly an argument of attack, and it is used more often about what to do than about what is so; a famous example is the dilemma of Tertullian, the Christian writer of Carthage around the year 200, against the emperor Trajan’s decree on the Christians: if they are guilty, why forbid searching for them, and if they are innocent, why punish them?
15. A dilemma holds only if three conditions are met: first, the disjunction must be **complete**, leaving no third possibility; second, each consequence must **follow** from its member; and third, the common conclusion must be the **only** one that can be drawn, or the dilemma can be turned against its author.
16. The rules also show how a dilemma is answered: we may show that the disjunction is not complete, which is called escaping between the horns; we may deny that a consequence follows from its member, which is called taking the dilemma by the horns; or we may draw an opposite conclusion from the same members, which is called retorting it.
17. The dilemma of the caliph Omar, “The books in the library either agree with the Koran or not; if they agree, they are superfluous; if not, they are harmful; so burn them,” fails the second rule, since agreement does not make a book superfluous.
18. To retort an argument is to draw the opposite conclusion from its own premises, as Maritain’s dilemma urged on someone considering public office, that governing honestly will please God and governing dishonestly will please men, so that either way it is to his advantage, is retorted by saying that governing honestly will displease men and governing dishonestly will displease God, so that either way it is not to his advantage.
19. A dilemma that can be retorted fails the third rule, since its members support two opposite conclusions equally, while a sound dilemma cannot be retorted, because its conclusion is the only one its members allow; so before urging a dilemma, its author should try to retort it himself.
20. A dilemma is not the same as a disjunctive syllogism, though speakers often confuse them: in a dilemma the same conclusion follows from each member of the disjunction, so that we need not know which member is true, but in a disjunctive syllogism one member is removed, and the other is concluded (“Either the minister was deceived or he knew; he was not deceived; so he knew”).
21. An orator who says he has caught his opponent “in a dilemma” usually means a disjunctive syllogism, with one member closed off by a further premise.
22. The difference matters for answering, because a disjunctive syllogism is answered by defending the member that was removed, and a dilemma by one of the three answers that suit it.
23. In an **oblique** syllogism one term stands in an oblique case, that is, it is not itself the subject or predicate but qualifies one by some relation, marked in English by a preposition or a possessive, as in “Christ is God; Mary is the mother of Christ; so Mary is the mother of God,” where the middle term “Christ” is direct in the major and oblique in the minor.
24. Maritain shows that some arguments usually taken to need a new “logic of relations” are oblique syllogisms: “A is greater than B; B is greater than C; so A is greater than C” is not a syllogism as it stands, since its middle term is “B” in one premise and “greater than B” in the other.
25. The argument becomes valid when its hidden premise is stated, that whatever is greater than something greater than C is itself greater than C; and an oblique syllogism can always be restated as a direct one, but it has its own way of proceeding.

## Induction and Example (`adv-ind`)

1. Aristotle and St. Thomas teach that we come to know in two ways: by the syllogism, which proceeds from universals, and by induction, which proceeds from singulars.
2. The reason lies in our knowledge itself, because all of it depends formally on first principles known through themselves, and all of it takes its origin materially from the singular things the senses perceive.
3. Maritain puts the difference this way: a syllogism stays on the plane of the intelligible and moves from one universal truth to another, resolving a conclusion into the principles it depends on, while induction rises from the plane of the senses to the intelligible, resolving a conclusion into the experience from which the mind drew it.
4. So induction is not a weaker syllogism; it is the other way of making truth known, and it supplies the principles that syllogisms use.
5. In a syllogism two terms are joined because each is joined to a third, the middle term, but induction has no middle term, and its mean is an **enumeration** of individuals or of kinds, which does the work that the middle term does in a syllogism.
6. The first premise of an induction joins a predicate to a series of individuals taken one by one, its second joins those individuals to the universal they share, and the conclusion joins the predicate to the universal (“This water boils at one hundred degrees, and this, and this; and the nature found in these is water; so water boils at one hundred degrees”).
7. Aristotle says the same in the *Prior Analytics*: in induction one extreme is shown of the middle through the other extreme, the enumerated cases.
8. Maritain insists that induction concludes about a nature, not about a collection: from “this metal conducts electricity, and this, and this,” it concludes that metal, every metal, conducts electricity, and so it claims a connection between the property and the nature, even if that connection is not yet seen, and does not merely sum up the cases observed.
9. Read as a statement about a collection, induction would be either worthless or empty: if it passed from some members to the whole collection taken together (“this soldier has a soul, and that one, so the regiment has a soul”), it would be a fallacy, and if it simply listed every member, it would say nothing new.
10. Induction properly so called passes from some to *every*, from the cases to the nature found in each.
11. An induction is **complete** when every part of the universal has been enumerated (“Sight, hearing, touch, taste, and smell each have a bodily organ; these are all the senses; so every sense has a bodily organ”), and **incomplete** when only some of the parts have been enumerated, as in nearly all inductions from experience, since we cannot review every case.
12. “Incomplete” does not mean “badly done,” because an incomplete enumeration can still be **sufficient**, and when it is sufficient, the induction is valid, since a sufficient enumeration is in effect a complete one; and the hard question of when an enumeration is sufficient belongs to the study of each science.
13. Complete induction is not empty either, because to know that each of the five senses has an organ is one thing, and to know that sense, as such, has an organ is another.
14. Maritain gives two rules for the form of induction: first, the enumeration must be **sufficient**, or otherwise the inference is not formal, so “Peter is French, and Paul, and James, so every man is French” is a defective induction, since three Frenchmen do not represent mankind.
15. Second, we may not make a particular term universal by an inductive ascent, even with a sufficient enumeration, so “Some saint lacked this kind of heroism, and some saint lacked that kind, and so on through every kind, so some saint lacked every kind of heroism” does not follow, because a different saint may lack each kind.
16. Some modern logicians treat induction as a syllogism in the third figure (“This magnet attracts iron; this body is a magnet; so some magnet attracts iron,” with “every magnet” then assumed), but Maritain answers that this destroys induction rather than explains it, since as a syllogism the argument concludes only “some magnet,” and the step to “every magnet” would be invalid in form.
17. St. Albert the Great, St. Thomas’s teacher, had already said that the matter of induction can be put into a syllogism but its form cannot, and that induction is opposed to the syllogism properly so called.
18. The syllogism proves that a subject and predicate agree because both agree with a third term, while induction proves that a property belongs to a nature by producing the cases in which they are found together.
19. Reasoning by resemblance, which Aristotle calls the example, passes from one case to another like it (“This remedy cured Paul’s headaches, so it will cure John’s”), and it is an induction that stops halfway: instead of rising to the universal of which Paul’s case is a part, it rises only to a resemblance between the two cases, and from there it descends to the second case.
20. Because the resemblance may be accidental, the example gives probable knowledge, not certainty, though it plays a great part in discovery.
21. The example is not an inference from particular to particular without any universal, as some modern logicians claim, because the universal in it is the likeness itself.
22. Reasoning by resemblance is often called reasoning by analogy, but it must not be confused with analogical knowledge: in reasoning by analogy the likeness is the means of an inference, often a likeness that is more or less accidental, and the conclusion is only probable.
23. In analogical knowledge the analogy belongs to a concept and to the things it is said of, as “being” is said of creatures and of God, each in its own way, and when one of the things is known to us and the other is not, we can know the second through the first, inadequately but with certainty.

## Demonstration (`adv-dem`)

1. A demonstration is a syllogism that produces science, that is, knowledge of a thing through its cause and as something that cannot be otherwise, and so its premises must be of a certain kind.
2. Aristotle requires that the premises of a demonstration be **true**, **first** and **immediate** (not themselves proved through a middle), **better known** than the conclusion, **prior** to it, and its **causes**.
3. St. Thomas explains each condition: the premises must be true, since what is not cannot be known; they must be first and immediate, or else resolve into premises that are, since proof cannot go back without end; and they must be better known, prior, and causes, since the conclusion is known through them.
4. A valid argument from true premises proves its conclusion, but it is a demonstration only when its premises meet all these conditions.
5. The premises of a demonstration are necessary because their predicates belong to their subjects *per se*, that is, through the subject itself, and St. Thomas distinguishes four ways of being said per se.
6. In the **first** way, the predicate is the definition of the subject or part of it (“A triangle is a figure”); in the **second**, the subject enters the definition of the predicate, as its proper subject (“A number is odd or even,” since odd cannot be defined without number).
7. Whatever is predicated neither way is predicated *per accidens*, as “musical” and “white” are said of an animal.
8. Demonstration uses the first way in its principles, since the definition is the middle term, and it concludes in the second way, since the property it proves is defined through the subject in which it belongs.
9. In the **third** way, “per se” means existing by itself, as an individual substance does (Socrates exists by himself, while “the white” exists only in something else), and this sense concerns things, not predication.
10. In the **fourth** way, a predicate belongs to a subject per se when the subject is the cause of it (“What was killed, died”), while what merely happens along with something belongs per accidens (“While he was walking, there was lightning”).
11. The fourth way joins effect to efficient cause, and so it too can appear in demonstration, when the cause named is the proper and necessary cause.
12. The four ways together show what kind of necessity science looks for: a predicate that belongs to a subject through what the subject is or through what it causes, not through what happens to accompany it.
13. Aristotle distinguishes knowing *that* a thing is so from knowing *why*: “Whatever does not twinkle is near; the planets do not twinkle; so the planets are near” is valid and true, but not twinkling is not the cause of nearness, since the planets do not twinkle because they are near, and so this demonstrates only **that** the planets are near (*quia*).
14. Exchange the terms and the demonstration gives the reason (“Whatever is near does not twinkle; the planets are near; so the planets do not twinkle”), because now the middle term is the cause, and we know **why** (*propter quid*).
15. St. Thomas adds that the demonstration of the fact can proceed through an effect that is better known to us, since effects are often more evident to the senses than their causes.
16. A demonstration can also show only the fact when its middle term is a cause, but not the proper and proximate one, as in Aristotle’s example “Whatever is not an animal does not breathe; a wall is not an animal; so a wall does not breathe,” where not being an animal is not the reason a wall does not breathe, since many animals do not breathe in the way meant.
17. The middle term there is too wide, because it lies outside the proper genus of the conclusion and does not convert with the major term; and the demonstration of the reasoned fact needs the proximate cause, the one that converts with the effect.
18. A demonstration must proceed from the principles proper to its own science, not from principles common to many, and Aristotle cites the attempt of Bryson, a Greek mathematician of Plato’s time, to square the circle by a principle true of quantity in general that says nothing about the forms of the circle and the square.
19. Such an argument may be persuasive, but it does not give the reason proper to geometry, and so it is not a demonstration; for the same reason a proof in geometry does not proceed from arithmetic, nor a proof in medicine from mere statistics, since each science demonstrates from the principles that belong to its own subject.
20. Aristotle holds that the first figure is the most scientific, because mathematics and the sciences that give the reasoned fact demonstrate in it, and only in it can a universal affirmative be concluded, which science seeks through its cause; a demonstration in the second or third figure can be completed only by being reduced to the first.
21. A demonstration of the reasoned fact usually has the definition of the subject, or of the property, as its middle term (“Every rational animal is capable of laughter; every man is a rational animal; so every man is capable of laughter”), because the definition explains why the property belongs, and the conclusion states a property in the second way of being said per se.
22. Sometimes one science knows the fact and another the reason, as the physician knows *that* round wounds heal more slowly, while the geometer knows *why*; in such cases the lower science is **subalternated** to the higher, taking its principles from it.
23. This shows that “science” in the classical sense has degrees, since a science that knows the fact without the reason is still knowledge, but it rests on another science for its explanation.
24. St. Thomas counts theology a science in this way, subalternated to the knowledge God and the blessed have, from which it receives its principles by faith.

## The Fallacies in Depth (`adv-fal`)

1. *De fallaciis*, the treatise attributed to St. Thomas, explains why there are exactly six fallacies in the words: a fallacy in the words deceives because one expression seems to signify one thing while it signifies several, and this can happen in three ways.
2. The multiplicity is **actual** when an expression, unchanged, signifies several things, and in one word this is equivocation, and in a phrase amphiboly.
3. The multiplicity is **potential** when the expression signifies several things only as it is spoken in one way or another, and in one word this is accent, and in a phrase composition and division.
4. The multiplicity is **apparent** when a word signifies one thing but seems to signify something else by its likeness to other words, which is the figure of speech; so the six fallacies in the words are equivocation, amphiboly, accent, composition, division, and figure of speech.
5. *De fallaciis* counts three modes of equivocation: in the first, a word signifies several things equally, as “dog” signifies the animal, the star, and the sea creature; in the second, a word signifies one thing properly and another by a figure, as a meadow is said to “laugh” when it flowers, and analogous words such as “healthy” are counted here.
6. In the third mode of equivocation, a word changes what it signifies with its tense, number, or person, as in “Whoever was rising is standing; the seated man was rising; so the seated man is standing.”
7. Amphiboly, the equivocation of a phrase, also has three modes: a phrase may be built in one way and still bear two relations, as “the book of Aristotle” can mean the book he wrote or the book he owns; it may be built in two ways, as when a word can be taken as subject or as object; or the whole phrase may be taken properly in one premise and figuratively in another.
8. In each case of equivocation and amphiboly the remedy is to distinguish the senses and grant the premise in one and deny it in the other.
9. In composition and division the same words can be joined or separated in different ways, so that one phrase is potentially several, as “Whatever lives always exists” is false if “always” goes with “exists” and true if it goes with “lives.”
10. The fallacy is one of **composition** when the phrase is false taken together, and of **division** when it is false taken apart, as “Two and three are five, so two and three are odd and even” divides what is true only together.
11. Accent is the same fault in one word, since a word pronounced in different ways signifies different things; in Latin the difference lay in the pitch of a syllable, and in English it lies chiefly in stress and in the emphasis of the speaker.
12. In the figure of speech a word has one way of signifying but seems to have another, by its likeness to other words, and the treatise counts three modes: a word of one gender may seem to be of another, which matters chiefly in Latin; a word in one category may seem to be in another; and a word that signifies a kind may seem to signify an individual.
13. The third mode explains the famous sophism “Man is a species; Socrates is a man; so Socrates is a species,” which the treatise places under the figure of speech, since it passes from a term that stands for the nature to one that stands for an individual.
14. The figure of speech is the fallacy closest to grammar, since its cause is the likeness between the forms of words.
15. The fallacies outside the words deceive because things that agree in some way are taken as the same simply, or things that differ in some way are taken as different simply, and *De fallaciis* derives seven of them from the general conditions of being.
16. From what is *per se* and what is *per accidens* comes the fallacy of **accident**; from the perfect and the imperfect, **in a certain respect taken flatly**; and from the opposed and the not opposed, **missing the point**.
17. From the same and the different comes **begging the question**; from the prior and the posterior, the **consequent**; from cause and effect, **treating what is not the cause as the cause**; and from the one and the many, **many questions as one**.
18. So the list of seven is not a collection of observed mistakes but an order taken from the ways in which things can be one or many.
19. Accident and consequent resemble each other: accident arises when a predicate is taken to be wholly the same as its subject (“Socrates is an animal, so Socrates is a man” assumes that whatever is an animal is a man), and the consequent arises when a consequent is taken to be wholly the same as its antecedent, so that the consequence is thought to run both ways.
20. The consequent therefore always involves two consequences, one true and one false, as “If someone runs, he moves” is true, but “If he moves, he runs” is false, and “Socrates moves, so Socrates runs” depends on the false one.
21. So, the treatise says, when an argument proceeds from a categorical proposition, the fallacy is accident, and when it proceeds from a conditional, it is the consequent; Aristotle puts it briefly: accident is in one, the consequent in two.
22. To beg the question is to ask that the very thing to be proved be granted, under another name, and the fallacy deceives because the words are different, since if it were asked under the same name, the asker would only be laughed at.
23. The treatise counts five modes of begging the question: the thing defined is asked to prove the definition, or the reverse; a universal is asked to prove its particular; or all the particulars are asked to prove the universal they make up.
24. The other modes of begging the question are that the parts of a proposition are asked one by one to prove the whole, or one of two correlatives is asked to prove the other (“Plato is the son of Socrates” to prove that Socrates is the father of Plato); and in every mode the premise is no better known than the conclusion.
25. In *De fallaciis*, treating what is not the cause as the cause is a fault of argument, not of causal explanation: it occurs when among the premises one is placed that does nothing toward the conclusion, but seems to because it shares terms with the others.
26. This fallacy deceives chiefly in an argument to the impossible, where an absurd conclusion is blamed on a premise that did not produce it, and so a true premise is rejected; the modern “false cause,” which takes what merely goes with a thing for its cause, is a different fault.
27. Many questions as one arises when two questions are put in one form, so that one answer is given to both (“Are Socrates and Plato running?” asked when only one of them runs); a proposition is one when one thing is said of one, or when the several things said make one by nature, as “rational mortal animal,” and otherwise it is several, and so is the question.
28. A refutation is a syllogism that concludes the contradictory of what the opponent holds, and since every fallacy fails either as a syllogism or as a contradiction, *De fallaciis* says that every fallacy is reduced to ignorance of the refutation as to its general principle, because the other twelve each omit something that a true syllogism or a true contradiction requires.
29. Missing the point is also a fallacy of its own, when what is omitted concerns the contradiction itself: the conclusion seems to contradict the thesis, but not of the same thing, in the same respect, in relation to the same, or at the same time (“Two is double of one and not double of three, so two is double and not double”).
30. So the study of fallacies is the study of what a real refutation needs, approached from the side of its failures.
