# Unit 0 Scripts — Orientation and the Meno (Lessons 1–3)

**Conventions used throughout.** Teacher's spoken words are in **bold** — say them as written. (Stage directions are in plain text and parentheses.) Expected student response follows →. "Signal" means tap the table once. Every choral question is asked, then paused on, then signalled: **ask — pause two beats — tap.** Where the script says **(9)** or **(12)**, take an individual turn with that student; say the child's actual name, not the number.

**Response words.** Unit 0 uses four fixed response words for sorting the acts of the mind — ONE, TWO, THREE, NONE — and, from Lesson 2, ANSWER and SWARM, and from Lesson 3, TOO NARROW, TOO WIDE, GOOD. Students say the word alone. Do not accept a sentence where the word is called for, and do not accept the word where the script asks for a reason.

**Firming.** Canonical wordings are printed in full and must be said exactly as printed. Do not paraphrase them, shorten them, or improve them until a later script marks them FIRM.

# LESSON 1 — What Logic Is                        (~20–30 min, both students)

**MATERIALS:** whiteboard and marker; a knife or a spoon and a shovel or trowel (any two real tools) on the table; no cards, no app today.

---

## 1. REVIEW / ORIENTATION (6–8 min)

(There is no prior material to review. This slot teaches the classroom procedures themselves. Every later lesson depends on them, so teach them the way you would teach doctrine: model, lead, test.)

### 1a. The signal

**We are starting a new subject today. It has rules about how we answer. Here is the first rule. When I ask a question, do not answer right away. Think. When I tap the table, everybody answers together. Watch me do it.**

(Model twice with a question you answer yourself.)

**My turn. What animal gives us milk?** (pause two beats, tap the table) **Cow.**

**My turn again. What do we eat bread with?** (pause, tap) **Butter.**

**Your turn. Do not answer until I tap. What animal gives us milk?** (pause two beats, signal) → *Cow.*

**Good. You waited.**

**What color is snow?** (pause, signal) → *White.*

**How many legs does a dog have?** (pause, signal) → *Four.*

> **CORRECTION — a student answers before the signal.**
> **Stop. You answered before the tap. My turn.** (Ask the same question, pause, tap, answer it yourself.) **Your turn.** (Same question, pause, signal.) → correct response. **That is it. Wait for the tap.**
> Retest this item in step 4.

### 1b. Together, and alone

**Second rule. Sometimes I say "everybody" and you both answer. Sometimes I say one name, and only that person answers. Let's practice.**

**Everybody. How many legs does a bird have?** (pause, signal) → *Two.*

**(9). How many legs does a bird have?** → *Two.*

**(12). How many legs does a spider have?** → *Eight.*

**Everybody. How many legs does a spider have?** (pause, signal) → *Eight.*

> **CORRECTION — both students answer on an individual turn.**
> **Stop. I said one name. When I say one name, the other person thinks the answer but keeps it inside. Again. (9). How many legs does a bird have?** → *Two.* **Good.**
> Retest in step 4.

### 1c. Say it exactly

**Third rule. Some sentences in this subject have to be said in exactly the same words every time, like a prayer. When I say "say it with me," we say the words together, exactly. Try one. Say it with me: The hand is a tool.** → *The hand is a tool.*

**Again.** → *The hand is a tool.*

**Good. That was not the real sentence. It was practice. The real one is coming.**

## 2. NEW MATERIAL (10–12 min)

### Concept A — What logic is, and what it is about

**MODEL.**

**Today we start logic. Listen. Logic is the art of reasoning well.**

**Again. Logic is the art of reasoning well.**

**Here is why we need it.** (Pick up the two tools.) **Look at my hand. My hand can do almost anything. It can grab, it can push, it can touch. If I had to, I could dig a hole with my hand. Could I dig a hole with my hand?** (pause, signal) → *Yes.*

**Would I dig it well?** (pause, signal) → *No.*

**So what did people make?** (Hold up the shovel.) **A shovel. The hand is good at almost everything and great at almost nothing. So the hand makes tools, and then the hand works well.**

**The mind is like the hand. By itself the mind thinks about ordinary things pretty well. You do not need logic to know that stealing is wrong, or to learn to fix a bicycle. But about the hardest questions — questions about the soul, and about God — the mind by itself rarely thinks well. So the mind makes tools for itself. It makes exact words. It puts words together into statements. It puts statements together into arguments. Those are the mind's tools. Logic is the study of how to make those tools well.**

**Is the shovel a tool of the hand?** (pause, signal) → *Yes.*

**Is a statement a tool of the mind?** (pause, signal) → *Yes.*

**LEAD.**

**Say it with me. Logic is the art of reasoning well.** (signal) → *Logic is the art of reasoning well.*

**Again.** (signal) → *Logic is the art of reasoning well.*

**Again.** (signal) → *Logic is the art of reasoning well.*

**TEST.**

**Everybody. What is logic?** (pause, signal) → *Logic is the art of reasoning well.*

**(9). What is logic?** → *Logic is the art of reasoning well.*

**(12). What is logic?** → *Logic is the art of reasoning well.*

> **CORRECTION — student gives a partial or reworded answer ("thinking well", "the art of reasoning").**
> **My turn. Logic is the art of reasoning well.** (Say it once more.) **Your turn. Say it with me. Logic is the art of reasoning well.** → together. **Alone. What is logic?** → *Logic is the art of reasoning well.* **Now every word is right.**
> Retest in step 4.

**MODEL — what logic is about.**

**Now, every art is about something. Music is about sounds. What is music about?** (pause, signal) → *Sounds.*

**Arithmetic is about numbers. What is arithmetic about?** (pause, signal) → *Numbers.*

**Logic is about three things in a row: words, thoughts, and things. Here is how they line up. I say the word "apple." The word stands for my thought of an apple. And my thought is a likeness of the real apple on the table. Word, thought, thing.**

(Write on the whiteboard, left to right: **words → thoughts → things**.)

**Here is the sentence. Logic is about words insofar as they signify things through our thoughts.**

**"Signify" means "stand for." Words stand for things — but not directly. They stand for things through our thoughts.**

**LEAD.**

**Say it with me. Logic is about words insofar as they signify things through our thoughts.** (signal) → *Logic is about words insofar as they signify things through our thoughts.*

**Again.** (signal) → repeated.

**TEST.**

**Everybody. What do words stand for?** (pause, signal) → *Thoughts.*

**Everybody. What are thoughts likenesses of?** (pause, signal) → *Things.*

**(12). What is logic about?** → *Logic is about words insofar as they signify things through our thoughts.*

**(9). Say the three, in order. Words —** (pause, signal) → *Thoughts.* **— thoughts —** (pause, signal) → *Things.*

> **CORRECTION — student reverses the order (says "things, thoughts, words").**
> **Stop. Look at the board. Which one is first?** → *Words.* **My turn: words, thoughts, things. Your turn.** → *Words, thoughts, things.* **Again.** → *Words, thoughts, things.* **Words come first because words are the ones we can hear.**
> Retest in step 4.

### Concept B — The three acts of the mind

**MODEL.**

**Now listen while I read something old and hard. You will not understand all of it today. Listen for the word "three."**

(Read aloud, straight through, at an even pace. Read it exactly as printed and do not stop at the three dots — they mark words we are skipping.)

> **There is a twofold operation of the intellect... one is the understanding of simple objects, that is, the operation by which the intellect apprehends just the essence of a thing alone; the other is the operation of composing and dividing. There is also a third operation, that of reasoning, by which reason proceeds from what is known to the investigation of things that are unknown.**

**How many operations of the mind did that say there are?** (pause, signal) → *Three.*

**We will call them the three acts of the mind. Here they are in short words. Listen.**

**Act one — grasping what a thing is.**
**Act two — putting thoughts together to say true or false.**
**Act three — reasoning from what we know to what we don't yet know.**

**Watch me sort three things.**

**My turn. "Dog." That is just one thing grasped. Nothing said about it yet. Act one.**

**My turn. "Dogs are animals." Two thoughts put together, and it is true. Act two.**

**My turn. "All animals move themselves, so dogs move themselves." We started with what we know and got to something else. Act three.**

**LEAD.**

**Say them with me. Act one — grasping what a thing is.** (signal) → repeated.
**Act two — putting thoughts together to say true or false.** (signal) → repeated.
**Act three — reasoning from what we know to what we don't yet know.** (signal) → repeated.

**Now the short way. When I say an act, you say the number. Grasping what a thing is.** (pause, signal) → *One.*
**Putting thoughts together to say true or false.** (pause, signal) → *Two.*
**Reasoning from what we know to what we don't yet know.** (pause, signal) → *Three.*

**TEST — the sorting sequence.**

**I will say something. You say ONE, TWO, or THREE. Wait for the tap.**

(Ask each item, pause two beats, signal. Ask them in this order — the order matters.)

| # | Item (say it exactly) | Response |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Bread.** | → *One.* |
| 2 | **Bread is food.** | → *Two.* |
| 3 | **All food comes from God, so bread comes from God.** | → *Three.* |
| 4 | **Hammer.** | → *One.* |
| 5 | **The hammer is heavy.** | → *Two.* |
| 6 | **Saint Joseph was a carpenter.** | → *Two.* |
| 7 | **Every carpenter uses tools, so Saint Joseph used tools.** | → *Three.* |
| 8 | **Grandmother.** | → *One.* |
| 9 | **My cousin is not tall.** | → *Two.* |
| 10 | **Every ball in this house is round, so the ball under the couch is round.** | → *Three.* |

(Items 1–10 are the positives: four are act one, four are act two, three are act three, and they range over food, tools, saints, family, and sport, so that no student can sort by topic. Now the negatives — say them with the same voice as the positives.)

**New rule. Some things I say are not any of the three. If it is none of them, say NONE. Listen: "Is the dog outside?" That asks. It does not tell us what is. So — NONE.**

| # | Item (say it exactly) | Response |
|---|---|---|
| 11 | **Are dogs animals?** | → *None.* |
| 12 | **Dogs are animals.** | → *Two.* |
| 13 | **Sit!** | → *None.* |
| 14 | **The dog sits.** | → *Two.* |
| 15 | **Is Grandmother kind?** | → *None.* |
| 16 | **Grandmother is kind.** | → *Two.* |
| 17 | **Pass the bread!** | → *None.* |
| 18 | **Bread.** | → *One.* |

(Each negative is one small change away from the positive that follows it. That is the point: the difference is the form of the words, not the subject matter.)

**Why is "Are dogs animals?" none of the three?** (individual, **(12)** first) → *Because it does not tell us what is. / Because it asks instead of telling.*

**(9). Does "Sit!" tell us what is?** → *No.* **So what do we say?** → *None.*

**We will come back to questions and commands much later and find out what they really are. Today: they are not one of the three.**

> **CORRECTION — student says TWO for a question ("Are dogs animals?").**
> **Stop. Listen to it again. "Are dogs animals?" Does that tell me something is true, or does it ask me?** → *It asks.* **My turn: none. Your turn. Are dogs animals?** → *None.* **Dogs are animals.** → *Two.* **Are dogs animals?** → *None.* **Good. The tell is that it asks.**
> Retest in step 4.

> **CORRECTION — student says ONE for a short statement (e.g. "Bread is food." → *One*).**
> **Stop. Count the thoughts. In "bread," how many things did I grasp?** → *One.* **In "bread is food," did I put two thoughts together?** → *Yes.* **Then it is act two. My turn: bread — one. Bread is food — two. Your turn. Bread.** → *One.* **Bread is food.** → *Two.* **Good. One word alone, act one. Words put together to say something, act two.**
> Retest in step 4.

> **CORRECTION — student says TWO for an argument (item 3, 7, or 10).**
> **Stop. Listen for the word "so." When you hear "so," we started from something we knew and got somewhere new. That is act three. My turn: All food comes from God, so bread comes from God — three. Your turn.** (Repeat item.) → *Three.* **Good. "So" is the sound of act three.**
> Retest in step 4. (Note: "so" is a helpful clue today only. Do not tell them it is a rule; it is not.)

## 9. DRAFTER'S FLAGS

1. **"Reasoning well" heard as "reading well."** A 9-year-old who is still working on reading will hear "the art of reading well." *Fix applied:* the model says the sentence twice before any lead, and the hand-and-shovel teaching sits between the model and the lead so the sentence is heard three separate times in context. If he still says "reading," say **"Not reading. Reasoning. Reasoning is thinking one thing from another,"** then re-lead.

2. **The Aquinas passage sounds like something to memorize.** It is not, in this lesson — only the short forms are drilled. *Fix applied:* the read-aloud is framed with **"You will not understand all of it today. Listen for the word 'three,'"** which gives him one job he can do, and the only question asked of the passage is how many operations it names.

3. **The ellipsis in the canonical passage.** Read straight through "intellect... one is" without pausing or saying "dot dot dot." A pause there invites **(12)** to ask what is missing, mid-passage. *Fix applied:* stage direction added at the read-aloud. If she asks afterward, the missing words are "as Aristotle says in his book On the Soul" — say so and move on.

4. **"NONE" invites guessing by tone.** Children detect a question by the teacher's rising voice before they detect it by grammar. *Fix applied:* the stage direction at item 11 tells you to say the negatives *with the same voice as the positives*, and the correction procedure makes the student state the reason ("it asks"), not just the verdict. Watch this specifically with **(9)**.

5. **"So" as a false rule for act three.** Items 3, 7, and 10 all contain "so," which makes act three trivially detectable and will fail the moment a lesson gives an argument without it. *Fix applied:* the correction box names "so" as a *clue for today only* and explicitly instructs you not to present it as a rule. Spare items keep the same shape deliberately — the pattern is broken on purpose in Unit 1, not here.

6. **Individual turns numbered "9" and "12."** Read aloud as written, these sound like the numbers nine and twelve, which collide with the response words ONE, TWO, THREE. *Fix applied:* the unit conventions note at the top says to substitute the child's real name; the script never prints a bare number as a teacher utterance.

7. **"My cousin is not tall" (item 9).** A negative statement is still act two. A child who has just learned "putting thoughts together" may hear "not" as "no thoughts put together." *Fix applied:* the item is placed after three easy act-two positives, so the pattern is established before the negative form appears. If he says NONE, say **"It still tells us something is true or false. Act two,"** and move on — do not open the topic of negation, which arrives in its own lesson.

8. **The two real tools on the table are a distraction risk.** *Fix applied:* MATERIALS says put them on the table, and the script has you pick them up at one specific moment. Put them out of reach again immediately after Concept A. If **(9)** starts handling them, that is a signal to move on, not to compete.

## 2. NEW MATERIAL (10–12 min)

(This is a retelling with checks, then one drill. Tell the story in the words printed — they are short on purpose. Do not add jokes or extra characters.)

### 2a. The story (narrate, checking as you go)

**Long ago in Greece there was a man named Socrates. He was famous for asking questions. A young rich man named Meno came to him with a question of his own.**

**Meno asked: can virtue be taught? Virtue means being good — being brave, being honest, being just. Meno wanted to know if you can be taught to be good, the way you are taught to read.**

**Everybody. What did Meno want to know?** (pause, signal) → *Whether virtue can be taught.*

**Socrates said something surprising. He said: I cannot tell you whether virtue can be taught, because I do not know what virtue is. And he said this: when I do not know the "what" of anything, how can I know its properties?**

**Listen to what that means. If I do not know what a hammer is, can I tell you whether a hammer is heavy?** (pause, signal) → *No.*

**If I do not know who your grandmother is, can I tell you whether she is kind?** (pause, signal) → *No.*

**So Socrates says: first the "what," then everything else.**

**(12). Why couldn't Socrates say whether virtue can be taught?** → *Because he didn't know what virtue is.*

**So Socrates asked Meno: tell me what virtue is. And Meno answered. Listen carefully to his answer. Meno said: a man's virtue is to run the city well and help his friends. A woman's virtue is to keep her house well. A child has another virtue, and an old man another, and a slave another. There are virtues without number.**

**Everybody. Did Meno give one answer or many?** (pause, signal) → *Many.*

**Socrates said: how lucky I am, Meno — I asked you for one virtue, and you have given me a swarm of them.**

**A swarm is what?** (pause, signal) → *A lot of bees.*

**Then Socrates made his best move of the day. He said: suppose I ask you, what is a bee? And you tell me there are many kinds of bees. I would say — but do bees differ as bees? Some bees are big and some are small. Some are prettier. But is any bee more of a bee than another bee?** (pause, signal) → *No.*

**Meno agreed. He said bees do not differ from one another as bees. And Socrates said: then there is one nature by which all bees are bees. Tell me that. And in the same way, however many virtues there are, they all have one common nature that makes them virtues — and the man who answers "What is virtue?" must fix his eye on that.**

**Everybody. Do bees differ as bees?** (pause, signal) → *No.*

**(9). Is a big bee more of a bee than a small bee?** → *No.*

**(12). What did Socrates want Meno to name — the many virtues, or the one nature in all of them?** → *The one nature.*

**My turn. One: Meno asked Socrates whether virtue can be taught. Two: Socrates said he could not answer, because he did not know what virtue is. Three: Meno gave a swarm of virtues, and Socrates asked for the one nature in all of them.**

**Say it with me. One: Meno asked Socrates whether virtue can be taught.** (signal) → repeated.
**Two: Socrates said he could not answer, because he did not know what virtue is.** (signal) → repeated.
**Three: Meno gave a swarm of virtues, and Socrates asked for the one nature in all of them.** (signal) → repeated.

**(12). All three, alone.** → all three sentences.

**(9). Sentence one, alone.** → *Meno asked Socrates whether virtue can be taught.*

**(9). Sentence three, alone.** → *Meno gave a swarm of virtues, and Socrates asked for the one nature in all of them.*

> **CORRECTION — student retells out of order or adds material.**
> **Stop. Three sentences, no more. My turn.** (Say all three.) **Your turn, sentence one.** → correct. **Sentence two.** → correct. **Sentence three.** → correct. **Now all three.** → all three. **Three sentences is the whole story for us.**
> Retest in step 4.

### 2c. NEW CONCEPT — the One-Over-Many check

**MODEL.**

**Here is the rule we take from this, and we will use it all year. An answer to "What is X?" must be one answer that fits every X.**

**One answer. Fits every one.**

**Watch me. Somebody asks: what is a bird? I say — a bird is an animal with feathers. That is one answer, and it is about every bird. So that is an ANSWER.**

**Watch again. Somebody asks: what is a bird? I say — a bird is a robin, a crow, and a duck. That is not one answer. That is a pile of birds. So that is a SWARM. And here is what we say to it: That is a swarm, not an answer.**

**One more. What is a shoe? A shoe is a covering for the foot. One answer, fits every shoe. ANSWER.**

**LEAD.**

**Say it with me. An answer to "What is X?" must be one answer that fits every X.** (signal) → repeated.

**Again.** (signal) → repeated.

**And say it with me. That is a swarm, not an answer.** (signal) → repeated.

**TEST — the ANSWER / SWARM sequence.**

**I will ask a question and then give the answer somebody gave. You say ANSWER or SWARM. Wait for the tap.**

## 9. DRAFTER'S FLAGS

1. **"Swarm" as a bee-fact rather than a logic-word.** A 9-year-old may spend the lesson thinking about bees. *Fix applied:* the swarm is introduced first as Socrates's *joke about Meno's answer* ("I asked for one, you gave me a pile"), and the bee comparison comes after, as Socrates's *move*, not as the topic. Also, ANSWER/SWARM items 1–8 use no bees at all — the word is being detached from insects deliberately. If he drifts, say **"swarm means a pile of examples,"** and continue.

2. **SWARM over-called as "bad answer."** This is the predictable failure of the lesson: children learn SWARM as a scolding word and apply it to any answer they think is wrong. *Fix applied:* item 9 ("a bird is an animal that flies") is placed inside the test sequence, immediately followed by an explicit **(12)** exchange establishing that a wrong answer is still an answer; a dedicated correction box handles it; and the mastery note names it as the thing most likely to need re-running.

3. **"Virtue" is an unfamiliar word.** *Fix applied:* it is glossed in the narration the first time it appears — **"Virtue means being good — being brave, being honest, being just"** — with three concrete instances, and it is never used as a test item on its own until item 11, by which time it has been heard six times. Do not define virtue further; that is not this lesson's business.

4. **Meno's answer sexes the virtues ("a man's virtue is to run the city, a woman's to keep her house").** With a 12-year-old girl in the room this can pull the lesson into a discussion of whether Meno was right. *Fix applied:* the narration reports it flatly and Socrates immediately rejects it as an answer — the script's own verdict on Meno's list is that it is not an answer at all. If she raises it, say **"Socrates didn't accept it either — but not because it was unfair. Because it was a swarm,"** and move on. If she wants the larger question, take it outside the lesson.

5. **The question "Did Meno give one answer or many?" can be answered by counting.** *Fix applied:* it is asked immediately after the list is read, so "many" is the obvious and intended response; the harder work is loaded onto **(12)**'s question about *the one nature*, which cannot be answered by counting.

6. **"Do bees differ as bees?" is grammatically strange to a child.** The construction "differ *as* bees" is doing real work and is easy to hear as "do bees differ, as bees do?" *Fix applied:* the question is never asked cold — it is preceded by **"Some bees are big and some are small... is any bee more of a bee than another bee?"**, which is the same question in child-grammar, and **(9)**'s individual turn uses that easier form. Only **(12)** gets the "as bees" form as a test.

7. **The teacher's voice will give away the swarms.** Lists have a natural falling cadence on the third item. *Fix applied:* stage direction before the test table tells you to say question and reply in one breath, in a flat voice. Practice items 1 and 2 aloud once before teaching; they should sound identical in tone.

8. **Two canonical sentences arrive in the same minute.** "An answer to 'What is X?' must be one answer that fits every X" and "That is a swarm, not an answer" are both required exactly. *Fix applied:* they are led separately, and only the second is required as a *student utterance* during the test — the first is required only in the lead and in guided practice. Do not test both in the same item.

## 2. NEW MATERIAL (10–12 min)

### 2a. The two Rules

**MODEL.**

**Yesterday we learned that an answer to "What is X?" must be one answer that fits every X. Today we learn how to test an answer — how to find out whether it is a good one.**

**A good answer to "What is X?" has a name. It is called a definition. And a definition has two jobs. Here they are.**

(Write on the board as you say them. Leave them up for the rest of the lesson.)

> **Rule 1: A definition must include every instance of the thing defined.**
>
> **Rule 2: A definition must exclude everything that is not the thing defined.**

**"Instance" means one of them. One dog is an instance of dog. One shoe is an instance of shoe. Rule one says: leave nobody out. Rule two says: let nobody in.**

**Everybody. Rule one says leave nobody —** (pause, signal) → *Out.*

**Everybody. Rule two says let nobody —** (pause, signal) → *In.*

**Watch me test three definitions.**

**My turn. "A dog is an animal." Now I hunt. I look for something that is an animal but is not a dog. A cat. A cat is an animal, and a cat is not a dog. So this definition let somebody in. It broke rule two. It is TOO WIDE.**

**My turn. "A shoe is a leather covering for the foot." Now I hunt the other way. I look for a shoe this leaves out. A rubber boot. A rubber boot is a shoe, and it is not leather. So this definition left somebody out. It broke rule one. It is TOO NARROW.**

**My turn. "A triangle is a shape with three straight sides." I hunt for a triangle it leaves out. I can't find one. I hunt for something with three straight sides that is not a triangle. I can't find one. Nobody left out, nobody let in. It is GOOD.**

**The thing I hunt for has a name. It is called a counterexample. The cat was a counterexample. The rubber boot was a counterexample.**

**LEAD.**

**Say it with me. Rule one: A definition must include every instance of the thing defined.** (signal) → repeated.

**Say it with me. Rule two: A definition must exclude everything that is not the thing defined.** (signal) → repeated.

**Again, both.** (signal) → both repeated.

**Now the short way. Too wide means somebody got —** (pause, signal) → *In.*
**Too narrow means somebody was left —** (pause, signal) → *Out.*

**TEST — how to answer.**

**From now on I say a definition. You say TOO NARROW, TOO WIDE, or GOOD. Then I say "Name it," and you name the counterexample — the one left out, or the one let in. If it is GOOD there is nobody to name, and I will not ask.**

Item 15 then lands as confirmation rather than a surprise.

2. **"Instance" is a new word doing load-bearing work in rule one.** *Fix applied:* it is glossed immediately and concretely — **"'Instance' means one of them. One dog is an instance of dog"** — before the rule is led, and it never appears in a test item where the student has to produce it.

3. **"Include" and "exclude" sound alike to a distractible listener.** Both rules are long sentences differing mainly in one prefix. *Fix applied:* the rules stay on the board for the whole lesson, and every drill and correction uses the short forms **"leave nobody out"** and **"let nobody in,"** which are not confusable. The full canonical sentences are required in the lead and in one individual turn each during guided practice; the verdicts are never driven off the long forms.

4. **A verdict without a counterexample looks like mastery and is not.** Three verdicts means a one-in-three guess rate, and a child who guesses will look fluent for a whole lesson. *Fix applied:* **Name it** is built into the response protocol from the first test, a dedicated correction box handles the empty verdict ("no name, no verdict"), the delayed test flags it explicitly, and the mastery note makes the counterexample — not the verdict — the criterion.

5. **GOOD items get no "Name it," which is itself a giveaway.** If you only ever ask for a name after a wrong verdict, the pattern teaches the student to listen to your follow-up rather than to the definition. *Fix applied:* the protocol announces the rule up front — **"If it is GOOD there is nobody to name, and I will not ask"** — so the follow-up carries no information the student hasn't already committed to. Ask for the verdict on the signal, and never before.

6. **"A cat is a small animal with fur" (guided practice) can be argued.** A child may say a rabbit is not an animal with fur "like a cat." *Fix applied:* accept *rabbit* or *mouse*; if the student disputes it, say **"Does a rabbit have fur? Is a rabbit small? Then the definition let him in,"** and move on. Do not litigate biology. The same applies to *a boat is a thing that floats* → *a duck.*

7. **The slave-boy narration is not drilled, but ends with a canonical line.** A child who has just been told "we are not drilling this" may switch off and miss the one thing that is required. *Fix applied:* the two choral checks come *inside* the narration, and the required sentence is led separately and given its own individual turn for **(9)**, after the "we will come back to this" close. Say the two checks in your teaching voice, not your storytelling voice.

8. **This lesson has three tracks running at once (T1, ANSWER/SWARM, definitions) and can overrun 30 minutes.** *Fix applied:* section 1 is written long enough to be cut — if you are behind, drop the last four review items, not the counterexample work. Section 2c may also be moved to the top of Lesson 4 if needed; it is a preview and nothing in Unit 0 depends on it. The one thing that must not be shortened is the **Name it** protocol.
