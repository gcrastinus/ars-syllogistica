Ars Logica

Learning the Art of Logic
Logic is the art that directs the act of reason itself, so that we may proceed in order, with ease, and without error. Grammar asks whether what is expressed has been formed well, and rhetoric asks whether the audience is persuaded to see the truth that has been expressed. Logic asks about terms and about propositions and about reasoning from conclusion to premises. This course follows the three acts of the mind: to grasp what a thing is, to judge that something is or is not true, and to reason from what we know to what we do not yet know.

Opening the acts. The path unfolds by the acts of the mind. In Logic as a Science, the studies of each act open when that act’s exercises are passed: the First Act’s with all four of its exercises, the Second Act’s with all three, and the Third Act’s with its reasoning (VIII–XVII); the Demonstration exercise and study open with Ad Demonstrationem, and the study of the fallacies with Sophistica. part one

Logic as an Art

Introduction · The Art of Reasoning
Logic is the art of reasoning well. This study sets out what logic is and what its parts are. A study of each of the three acts of the mind (simple apprehension, judgment, and reasoning) opens the section of exercises that perfects it.
Difficulty
The First Act of the Mind · Simple Apprehension
The first act of the mind, which comes before all judging or reasoning, is simply to grasp what a thing is. Here we learn the five predicables (the ways a universal is said of a subject) and the ten categories into which all that can be said of a thing falls; then we learn to divide a whole into its kinds and to define what a thing essentially is. These are the arts by which a concept is made clear and distinct, and everything that follows depends on them.
The Second Act of the Mind · Judgment
The second act of the mind joins or separates concepts in order to affirm or deny, and what it forms is the proposition. We picture propositions on Venn diagrams, relate one proposition to another by opposition, equipollence, and conversion, and qualify them by the modes of necessity and possibility.
The Third Act of the Mind · Reasoning
Ratiocinatio · The Work of Reason
The third act of the mind reasons from things already known to things unknown, and its form is the syllogism. An argument is valid when its conclusion must be true if its premises are, whatever they are about; it is invalid when the premises could be true and the conclusion still false. That single test is applied here to arguments of every kind (categorical, conjunctive and disjunctive, conditional, modal, and stated in ordinary speech), and in categorical and modal arguments the conclusion follows the weaker part in quantity and quality.
Ad Demonstrationem · Approaching Science
When valid form is joined to true matter, the argument is sound; when its premises are also necessary, first, and causal, it becomes science, that is, sure knowledge of why a thing must be so. This exercise weighs an argument in form and in matter at once. It does not yet present complete demonstrations, but it prepares the mind for those later and higher proofs.
Dialectica · Reasoning from What Is Granted
Where we cannot demonstrate, we still need not guess, because dialectic reasons from what is probable (what seems so to everyone, or to most, or to the wise) and reaches an opinion held for good reasons. Here we learn the topics an argument may be drawn from, what standing a premise has before we offer it, and the four ways the schools answered an objection.
Rhetorica · The Art of Persuasion
Here the reasoning of the third act is carried into rhetoric, which is ordered to persuasion and truth, especially since the true and the just are by nature more powerful than their opposites (cf. Aristotle, Rhetoric I.1). The enthymeme is the orator’s syllogism, in which a premise is left unsaid and silently supplied by the hearer. Here we learn to state the hidden assumption and examine it.
Sophistica · The Detection of Fallacy
Reasoning goes wrong, and it goes wrong in ways that can be named. A fallacy is an argument that looks sound and is not, and De fallaciis, a treatise attributed to St. Thomas, insists that each has two causes: what makes it look good, and what makes it fail. Six come from the words and seven from outside them. We study them not to use them but to recognize them when others use them.

The Venn Diagram Workshop

What the circles mean

A general term is a universal: one word standing over a host of individuals. We may picture it as standing over them, with the term above and beneath it every single thing of which it can be truly said:

Every circle in these diagrams is such a universal seen from above, its individuals gathered inside. When two or three circles overlap, their regions sort the individuals beneath, and the whole logic of propositions and syllogisms can be read off the picture. Use the buttons below to see any form drawn for you, or mark the regions yourself and let the readout say what your diagram asserts.

How to read a Venn diagram

Shading declares a region empty; an × declares that something exists there. Each categorical proposition makes exactly one mark in the diagram (what an affirmative universal also says about existence is shown by a ⊗ when it is needed):

“All S are P”: shade the part of S outside P (no S lies outside P).
“No S are P”: shade the overlap (nothing is both).
“Some S are P”: place an × in the overlap.
“Some S are not P”: place an × in S outside P.

With three circles, a universal proposition empties a lens of two cells, one inside the third circle and one outside it. A single × placed in one cell asserts several “some”-propositions at once, as the readout below will show. In syllogism mode the readout also states what your marks force about the two outer terms, that is, the conclusion, if one follows.

Venn Diagrams: Traditional vs. Modern Interpretations

There are two ways to read these diagrams.

Logic textbooks (Venn, Copi, Hurley, Gensler) treat shading as “this region is empty” and an × as “something is here.”

Mathematics textbooks, from the first lessons on sets through probability, shade a region to pick it out. There the diagram is used to name or compute a set, such as a union or an intersection, so shading marks the part meant or occupied.

The traditional (Aristotelian) reading assumes that the terms have members, that is, it gives them existential import; the modern reading does not.

This course follows the logic textbooks: shading means empty. On existential import it keeps the traditional doctrine, in which an affirmative proposition asserts that its subject exists and a negative does not; that existence is shown by a ⊗ where an argument needs it. The fuller history is at the foot of this page.

Click a region: once to shade it (declared empty) · twice for an × (something exists) · a third time to clear
The History of These Diagrams

In the tradition a general term signifies a nature, and it stands for (supposits for) the individuals of which that nature can be truly said. So comprehension is prior to extension: we know what a dog is before we can say which things are dogs, and “All dogs are animals” attributes animality to the dog as such. A circle in these diagrams pictures only the extension of a term, that is, the things for which it stands; it does not picture what the term means, and it is not a set defined by its members.

John Venn, the English logician, published these diagrams in 1880 as an aid to the algebra of George Boole, in which a term names a class, a class may be empty, every class has a complement, and all classes lie within a universe of discourse. Leonhard Euler, the Swiss mathematician, had used circles in the eighteenth century, drawing each proposition as one arrangement of circles; Venn instead drew every possible region and marked it. On the Boolean reading “All S are P” says only that no S lies outside P, and so it is true when there are no S.

This course uses Venn’s drawing but keeps the traditional doctrine. Shading declares a region empty, an × declares that something is there, and a ⊗ shows the existence that an affirmative universal asserts of its subject, drawn where an argument needs it. Where the modern logician speaks of a complement, we speak of an infinite term such as “non-animal,” which the course treats through obversion. A fuller account is in Logic as a Science: Opposition and Conversion in Depth.

Euler’s circles differ from Venn’s in what they draw. An Euler diagram draws only the relation that holds between the terms: for “All A are B,” the circle A lies inside the circle B; for “No A are B,” the two circles stand apart; and for “Some A are B,” the two circles overlap.

A Venn diagram, by contrast, always draws every region, whether or not anything is there, and then marks a region as empty by shading it or as occupied by placing an × in it. So the same Venn figure serves for every proposition, and only the marks change.

The Square of Opposition

The square of opposition, in itself

Every simple categorical proposition has a quantity (universal or particular) and a quality (affirmative or negative). When these two are combined, exactly four forms result, named by the vowels of affirmo (“I affirm”) and nego (“I deny”):

A — universal affirmative, All S are P.   E — universal negative, No S are P.   I — particular affirmative, Some S are P.   O — particular negative, Some S are not P.

The square sets these four at its corners (A and E along the top, I and O beneath), so that the logical relations between them may be seen at a glance. There are four such relations:

Contradictories (the diagonals, A–O and E–I) can never share a truth-value: of each pair, exactly one is true and one false. Contraries (A–E) cannot both be true, though both may be false. Subcontraries (I–O) cannot both be false, though both may be true. Subalterns (A above I, E above O): truth descends from the universal to its particular, and falsity ascends from the particular to its universal.

Why it is used. Given the truth or falsity of any one corner, the square lets you read off at once what must follow for the other three. It is the oldest means of valid inference from a single premise, and the basis of the contradictory, the converse, the obverse, and the contrapositive below, which you can try on any pair of terms in the square at the foot of this page.

The contradictory, the converse, the obverse, and the contrapositive, briefly

Conversion exchanges subject and predicate. E and I convert simply, and the result is equivalent, so truth passes both ways: “No fish are birds” ⇄ “No birds are fish”; “Some singers are poets” ⇄ “Some poets are singers”.

A converts only per accidens, that is, the quantity is reduced: “All foxes are animals” yields “Some animals are foxes”. Why is the quantity reduced? The A proposition places the foxes wholly inside the animals, but it says nothing about the whole of the animals, so “All animals are foxes” would claim far more than was given (the illicit conversion). Yet since an affirmative asserts that its subject exists, there are foxes, and so that part of the animals which contains them must be foxes: hence “some animals are foxes”. Truth is preserved in one direction only: we cannot infer “All foxes are animals” back from “Some animals are foxes”.

O has no converse at all: “Some animals are not foxes” is true, while “Some foxes are not animals” is false.

Obversion changes the quality and replaces the predicate with its complement. It applies to all four forms and is fully truth-preserving, since the obverse is equivalent to the original: “All foxes are animals” ⇄ “No foxes are non-animals”; “Some poets are singers” ⇄ “Some poets are not non-singers”.

Contraposition gives, from an A or an O, an equivalent in which both terms have changed places and been replaced by their complements; but it is really three familiar steps in succession, not a single step: obvert, then convert, then obvert again. Consider “All foxes are animals.” (1) Obvert, changing the quality and negating the predicate: “No foxes are non-animals.” (2) Convert, since an E converts simply: “No non-animals are foxes.” (3) Obvert again: “All non-animals are non-foxes.” That is the contrapositive, equivalent to the original (whatever fails to be an animal certainly fails to be a fox). Converting first is a tempting shortcut, but it gives “All animals are foxes”, and an A does not convert. E contraposes only per accidens, to an O; I has no contrapositive.

The contradictory is not a truth-preserving inference but a truth-reversing pairing: A with O, E with I, each always taking the opposite truth-value of the other.

In sum: simple conversion (E, I), obversion (all), and contraposition (A, O) are equivalences, so truth passes both ways. Conversion of A and contraposition of E hold in one direction only, the universal weakening to a particular by existential import. And O cannot be converted, nor I contraposed, at all: “Some animals are not dogs” is true, yet its converse “Some dogs are not animals” is false, because the O gives no purchase on the predicate beyond the part it names.

The square in traditional and modern logic

The square sets the four forms A, E, I, and O at its corners so that their relations can be seen together. Its doctrine comes from Aristotle’s On Interpretation. In the older logic an affirmative proposition is true only if its subject stands for something that exists, and a negative carries no such existence. So when the subject stands for nothing, A and I are false and E and O are true, and every relation of the square still holds: contradictories, contraries, subcontraries, and subalterns.

Modern logic, from Boole and from Gottlob Frege, the German mathematician and logician, reads “All S are P” as “For anything whatever, if it is S, it is P.” On this reading the universal is true when nothing is S. Then A and E can both be true, I and O can both be false, and A no longer yields I; of the four relations only contradiction remains. With subalternation go the conversion of A per accidens, the contraposition of E, the five weakened moods, and Darapti, Felapton, Bramantip, and Fesapo.

The tradition answers that the modern formula represents the universal without saying what it means, since “All men are mortal” attributes mortality to man as such. The medieval logicians also saw the problem of empty terms, and they placed existence in the affirmatives (as Gyula Klima shows). This course follows the traditional reading of the square. A fuller account is in Logic as a Science: Opposition and Conversion in Depth.

What is recitation?

One of the oldest and best-proven ways to study is to read a section of a book, or study a topic, in one sitting, then close your notes and write down everything you remember, in as much detail as you can. This is called "recitation," and this tablet lets you do it.

After you finish studying a part of the course, close it and open the Recitation Tablet. Choose the part you just finished from the menu, then write down, in order, everything you can remember about it. This is not easy. But the difficulty is what produces results, especially the moment when you feel you remember nothing more and then keep searching.

When you have finished, click "See Full Recitation." You will see a complete outline of the material with every point stated simply. The outline is a checklist: go through it carefully and mark exactly what you included correctly and what you missed. The course will remind you to recite the same material again after a few days, since each new recall fixes it more firmly.
