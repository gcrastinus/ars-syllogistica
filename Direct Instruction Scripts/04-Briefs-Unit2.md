# Unit 2 Briefs — The Second Act: Composing and Dividing (Lessons 16–27)

---

## LESSON 16 — Noun and Verb

**Objective.** Students can sort expressions SIMPLE/COMPLEX and simple expressions NOUN/VERB, including indefinite nouns.

**Canonical wording.** The noun is a simple expression that has no reference to time; the verb signifies with time.

**Doctrinal material.** A simple expression is one whose parts do not have significance by themselves; the parts of a complex expression do ("man" vs "the man runs"; the "m" in man means nothing alone). Words signify thoughts, thoughts are likenesses of things — so this part of logic studies word-combinations because they point to the mind's composing and dividing. The indefinite noun is the noun with 'not' or 'non' added ('non-dog') — plant the seed: L24's obversion will harvest this.

**Example sequences.** (a) SIMPLE/COMPLEX ≥8. (b) NOUN/VERB ≥10 (include "is walking," "hits," "is healthy" as verbs; "runner" as noun; 'non-man' as indefinite noun). Minimal pairs: "run" (verb) vs "a run" (noun).

**Tracks.** T2, T5, T10.

**Deeper.** Why 'tomorrow' isn't a verb though it involves time (signifies time, doesn't co-signify it of a subject — keep light).

**Mastery note.** Both sorts firm.

## LESSON 17 — The Statement

**Objective.** Students can pick out statements from non-statements and say the canonical wording.

**Canonical wording.** Only the statement is either true or false.

**Doctrinal material.** Aristotle: this part of logic is about the statement, or proposition (do NOT teach 'proposition' yet — L20). A prayer/question/command is speech but neither true nor false. Truth: to say of what is, that it is. The second act composes and divides thoughts; the statement is its outward tool.

**Example sequences.** STATEMENT / NOT-A-STATEMENT ≥12, wide variety (exclamations, questions, commands, wishes, single words, statements true, statements false — a FALSE statement IS a statement: drill this hard; it is the classic child error). Minimal pair: "The door is shut." / "Shut the door!"

**Tracks.** T2, T7-seed, review Meno counterexample skill.

**Deeper.** Are promises true or false when made? (discussion, not doctrine).

**Mastery note.** Both accept false statements as statements without wobble.

## LESSON 18 — Quality: Affirmation and Denial

**Objective.** Students can label statements AFFIRMATION/DENIAL and produce the proper denial of any simple statement.

**Canonical wording.** The affirmation joins together the subject and predicate; the denial separates them.

**Doctrinal material.** Every affirmation has a properly opposed denial and vice versa: same noun, same verb, one composes, one divides ("Socrates is tan" / "Socrates is not tan"). Statements differing this way differ in quality. 'Negation' = synonym for denial (introduce once).

**Example sequences.** (a) Label ≥10. (b) Produce-the-denial ≥8 — watch the error of changing the predicate ("Socrates is short") instead of dividing ("Socrates is not tan"): make this the scripted correction. Minimal pair: "Socrates is not tan" (denial) vs "Socrates is untanned" (affirmation with indefinite predicate — Deeper for now).

**Tracks.** T7 begins in earnest; T2, T10.

**Deeper.** The indefinite-noun affirmation vs the denial — feeds L24.

**Mastery note.** Proper denial produced instantly, same subject and predicate kept.

## LESSON 19 — Quantity: the Four Statement Types

**Objective.** Students can classify any simple statement as A, E, I, or O and give quantity + quality for each.

**Canonical wording.** Universal statements take the universal subject in all of its universality; particular statements take it only in part.

**Doctrinal material.** Statements with universal subjects begin with 'every/all', 'no', 'some', 'some...not' (or equivalents); singular statements have individual subjects (handled per app convention in L20); an indefinite statement drops the sign ("Man is tan") — treat as needing a sign supplied. The four: A universal affirmation, E universal denial, I particular affirmation, O particular denial. Vowel names from AffIrmo and nEgO (enrichment, Poinsot/tradition — teach as a memory hook).

**Example sequences.** ≥16 classification items across wildly varied content; minimal pairs across each boundary (Every dog barks / Some dogs bark; Some dogs bark / Some dogs do not bark; No dogs bark / Some dogs do not bark). Include equivalents ('all', 'none', 'not one', 'a few').

**Tracks.** T7 core; T2 retire (absorbed), T5 spot-review.

**Deeper.** Indefinite statements: is "Man is an animal" universal in force? (Andres: seems universal when the predicate belongs essentially).

**Mastery note.** A/E/I/O called with quantity+quality stated; ≥95%.

## LESSON 20 — Standard Form; "Proposition"

**Objective.** Students can rewrite ordinary sentences in standard form and use 'proposition' as a synonym for 'statement'.

**New concepts.** (1) Standard form: quantity-sign + subject + is/are (+ not) + predicate; verbs unfold into 'things that...': "All dogs bark" → "All dogs are things that bark." (2) The mapping, on Andres's own authority: the statement, or proposition — the app says 'proposition'; same thing. (3) App singular convention (T10): singulars stand whole — treat "Socrates is a man" as taking its subject in its entirety, one thing wholly included or wholly shut out.

**Example sequences.** Translation drill ≥12, from easy ("Every cat sleeps") to tricky ("Nobody home was awake," "Dogs bark," "Only the brave deserve the fair" — last one Deeper). Correction watch-point: losing the quantity sign or the 'not' in translation.

**App.** Open the app's Second Act study section; browse **Diagramming Propositions** instructions only (diagrams taught L25).

**Tracks.** T7, T9 begins; T10.

**Deeper.** 'Only'-sentences; exceptives.

**Mastery note.** Standard form automatic; the word 'proposition' provokes no blink.

## LESSON 21 — Opposition I: Contradictories and Contraries

**Objective.** Given a statement, students can produce its contradictory and its contrary (when it has one) and apply the two truth rules.

**Doctrinal material.** Contradictories are two statements opposed in both quantity and quality (A↔O, E↔I): with a pair of contradictories it is always the case that one is true and the other is false, though we may not know which. Contraries are two universal statements opposed in quality (A↔E): contraries cannot both be true, but they might both be false ("Every man is tan" / "No man is tan" — both false). Distinguish from the Categories' use of 'contrary' (one line).

**Example sequences.** (a) Produce-the-contradictory ≥8 (watch the A→E error: giving the contrary when asked for the contradictory — scripted correction with the both-false demonstration). (b) TRUE/FALSE/UNKNOWN inference drill ≥8: "A is true; what about O? about E?" Use letters and English both.

**Tracks.** T7, T9; T8 begins.

**Deeper.** Why the rules for contradictories are the master rules (L22 derives the rest).

**Mastery note.** Both truth rules recited and applied.

## LESSON 22 — Opposition II: The Square

**Objective.** Students can draw and fill the square of opposition and run all four relations in TRUE/FALSE/UNKNOWN drills.

**Doctrinal material.** The medievals added: subcontraries (I↔O) can both be true, cannot both be false; subalternates (A→I, E→O): if the universal is true the particular is true, not vice versa; if the particular is false the universal is false, not vice versa. The last two oppositions follow from the first two — demonstrate once: A true → E false (contraries) → I true (contradictories); the student of logic must know the first two rules and be able to apply them to all statements.

**Example sequences.** Full-square drills ≥12: given one corner's truth-value, fill all four; include UNKNOWN outcomes (A false → I unknown) — the hardest discrimination; give it the most minimal-difference attention.

**App.** **The Square of Opposition** lab (unscored study) after the lesson, both children, 10 minutes.

**Tracks.** T7, T8 core, T9.

**Deeper.** Derive subcontrariety from the master rules aloud.

**Mastery note.** Any corner given, all corners filled, UNKNOWNs included.

## LESSON 23 — Conversion

**Objective.** Students can convert E, I, and A statements correctly and refuse to convert O, with reasons.

**Canonical wording.** In every conversion, the new statement has the same quality as the old statement, but it sometimes differs in quantity.

**Doctrinal material.** The four rules: (1) the universal denial converts universally (No B is A → No A is B); (2) the universal affirmation converts into a particular affirmation (Every B is A → Some A is B; every man is an animal, but only some animals are men); (3) the particular affirmation converts into a particular affirmation (Some B is A → Some A is B); (4) the particular denial does not convert (some human beings are not doctors, but it is false that no doctors are human beings... and 'some animals are not men' does not give 'some men are not animals'). Conversion rules apply to true statements.

**Example sequences.** (a) Convert-or-refuse ≥12 in letters and English. (b) Truth-transmission drill: original true → converse true; converse true → original UNKNOWN (for A). Correction watch-point: converting A simply ("Every man is an animal" → "Every animal is a man") — the program's single most important error to kill before the syllogism.

**Tracks.** T8, T9, T7.

**Deeper.** Names 'simple conversion' and 'conversion per accidens' (the app and tradition use them).

**Mastery note.** O-refusal and A-weakening automatic.

## LESSON 24 — Obversion and Contraposition (App Conventions)

**Objective.** Students can obvert any statement and contrapose A and O, per the app's Immediate Inference set.

**Framing (say to students).** These are extra tools the scholastic tradition polished after Aristotle; our book teaches conversion; the app adds these two. They are built from what you know: the square + the indefinite ('non-') term from L16.

**Doctrinal material (align with the app's study text).** Obversion: change the quality and replace the predicate with its indefinite (negative) term; valid for all four types. Contraposition: valid as an equivalence for A and O; conversion of A and contraposition of E hold in one direction only, the universal weakening to a particular by existential import; O cannot be converted, nor I contraposed. Summary to teach: simple conversion (E, I), obversion (all), contraposition (A, O) are equivalences — truth passes both ways.

**Example sequences.** (a) Obvert ≥8 (watch double-negative reading errors: 'No dogs are non-animals' — script slow unpacking). (b) Contrapose A and O ≥6. (c) Mixed "which tool?" drill ≥8. Letters first, then English.

**App.** Begin **Immediate Inference** set (gates Act III). Easy, letters on.

**Tracks.** T8, T9.

**Deeper.** Why I has no contrapositive — build the counterexample.

**Mastery note.** Tool chosen and applied correctly ≥90%.

## LESSON 25 — Diagramming Propositions

**Objective.** Students can draw and read the app's two-circle diagrams for A, E, I, O.

**Doctrinal material (the app's conventions, teach as pictures of what statements say).** Shading declares a region empty; an × declares an occupant; ⊗ marks the existential import assumed in the traditional reading — our books assume the subject of a true universal is not empty, and the app draws that assumption as ⊗. A: subject-only region shaded, ⊗ in the overlap. E: overlap shaded, ⊗ in subject-only. I: × in overlap. O: × in subject-only.

**Example sequences.** (a) Statement→diagram ≥8. (b) Diagram→statement ≥8. (c) "What does this diagram deny?" ≥4. Minimal pairs: A vs I diagrams (shading+⊗ vs bare ×).

**App.** **Diagramming Propositions** set in earnest (gates Act III). The app's feedback draws the correct Venn for every answer — tell students to study each one.

**Tracks.** T8, T9, T7.

**Deeper.** Read a square-of-opposition fact off two diagrams (A's diagram contains I's ×: subalternation drawn).

**Mastery note.** All eight directions (4 types × 2 directions) firm.

## LESSON 26 — The Modal Statements

**Objective.** Students can classify statements as asserting the necessary, impossible, possible, or contingent, per the app's Modal Propositions set.

**Framing.** Beyond our main book; the tradition (and our app) completes the second act with modes: not just *that* something is, but *how* it is — necessarily, possibly. Enrichment source: Poinsot's treatment of modal propositions in the Outlines; keep to the four modes and their oppositions, at the depth the app's set requires (calibrate against the set before drafting — see alignment notes).

**Doctrinal material.** The four modes: necessary (cannot be otherwise: every triangle has three sides), impossible (cannot be: a triangle with four sides), possible (can be: it will rain tomorrow), contingent (can be and can not-be: the dog is sleeping). Equipollences (necessary = not-possible-not, impossible = not-possible) for the Deeper track.

**Example sequences.** Four-way sort ≥16, heavily concrete; minimal pairs: "Every man is an animal" (necessary) vs "Every man in this room is seated" (contingent though universal) — quantity is not modality: drill that discrimination hard.

**App.** Begin **Modal Propositions** set (gates Act III as shipped — see alignment notes suggesting this be made optional).

**Tracks.** T7, T8, T9.

**Deeper.** Equipollence chains; the modal square.

**Mastery note.** Four-way sort ≥90%; both can say "quantity is not modality."
