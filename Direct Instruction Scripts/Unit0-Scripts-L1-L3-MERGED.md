## CONVENTIONS (read once, applies to all three lessons)

**Teacher's spoken words are in bold** — say them as written. (Stage directions are in plain text and parentheses.) Expected student response follows →. **Signal** means tap the table once. Every choral question is asked, then paused on, then signalled: **ask — pause two beats — tap.**

**Individual turns** are marked **(9)** and **(12)**. Say the child's actual name, never the number. The numerals are on the page only so the drafter can see who carries which item.

**Response words.** Unit 0 uses short fixed labels. Students say the label alone. Do not accept a sentence where the label is called for, and do not accept the label where the script asks for a reason.

| Track | Labels | Introduced |
|---|---|---|
| The three acts | **ONE · TWO · THREE · NONE** | Lesson 1 |
| The One-Over-Many check | **ANSWER · SWARM** | Lesson 2 |
| Definition testing | **GOOD · TOO WIDE · TOO NARROW** | Lesson 3 |

> **Never ask a counting question in a lesson that sorts the acts.** "How many legs does a dog have?" → *Four* collides with ONE / TWO / THREE. Lesson 1's warm-up was rewritten to remove every number answer for exactly this reason. Keep it that way in later units.

**Canonical wordings** are printed in full and must be said exactly as printed. Do not paraphrase them, shorten them, or improve them until a script marks them FIRM. Nothing is FIRM in Lesson 1. By Lesson 3, *Logic is the art of reasoning well* and the three act names may be required whole, without chunking.

**Counterexamples are not scripted answers.** From Lesson 3 on, the sample after each arrow is one correct answer, not the only one. A child who says *brown dogs* where the page says *white dogs* is right. Accept any true instance left out, or any true outsider let in.

**The board.** Everything the script tells you to write stays up for the rest of the lesson, and gets rewritten at the top of the next lesson if it was erased. The children should be able to look up, not just remember.

# LESSON 1 — What Logic Is  (~25–30 min, both students)

**MATERIALS:** whiteboard and marker; a pencil; one real hand-tool (a spoon, a knife, or a trowel); table clear for the signal tap. No cards, no app today.

---

## 1. REVIEW / ORIENTATION (5–6 min)

(There is no prior material. This slot teaches the classroom procedures — but it teaches them on **word vs. thing**, which is the seed of Concept B. Do not spend this time on trivia; the procedure and the content can be learned at once.)

### 1a. The signal

**We are starting a new subject today. It has rules about how we answer. Here is the first rule. When I ask a question, do not answer right away. Think. When I tap the table, everybody answers together. Watch me do it.**

(Model twice with a question you answer yourself.)

**My turn.** (hold up the pencil) **What is this?** (pause two beats, tap) **A pencil.**

**My turn again. Is a pencil a tool?** (pause, tap) **Yes.**

**Your turn. Do not answer until I tap.** (hold up the pencil) **What is this?** (pause two beats, signal) → *A pencil.*

**Good. You waited.**

**Is a pencil a tool?** (pause, signal) → *Yes.*

**Is a pencil a dog?** (pause, signal) → *No.*

> **CORRECTION — a student answers before the signal.**
> **Stop. You answered before the tap. My turn.** (Ask the same question, pause, tap, answer it yourself.) **Your turn.** (Same question, pause, signal.) → correct response. **That is it. Wait for the tap.**
> Retest this item in step 4.

### 1b. Together, and alone

**Second rule. Sometimes I say "everybody" and you both answer. Sometimes I say one name, and only that person answers. Let's practice.**

**(9), is a pencil a tool?** → *Yes.*

**(12), is a pencil a dog?** → *No.*

**Everybody. Is a pencil a tool?** (pause, signal) → *Yes.*

> **CORRECTION — both students answer on an individual turn.**
> **Stop. I said one name. When I say one name, the other person thinks the answer but keeps it inside. Again. (9), is a pencil a tool?** → *Yes.* **Good.**
> Retest in step 4.

### 1c. Word and thing

**Now listen. Sometimes I will say a word. Sometimes I will show you a thing. A word is what we say or write. A thing is what you can hold or meet.**

(Write APPLE on the board. Sketch an apple beside it.)

**I say the word apple. Word or thing?** (pause, signal) → *Word.*

**This apple.** (point to the sketch) **Word or thing?** (pause, signal) → *Thing.*

**I say the word pencil. Word or thing?** (pause, signal) → *Word.*

**This pencil.** (hold it up) **Word or thing?** (pause, signal) → *Thing.*

**(9), I say sister. Word or thing?** → *Word.*

**(12), your own hand. Word or thing?** → *Thing.*

> **CORRECTION — a student calls a spoken word a thing.**
> **A thing is what you can hold or meet. A word is what we say or write. I said apple. That is a word. Word or thing?** (pause, signal) → *Word.*
>
> **CORRECTION — a student calls the held pencil a word.**
> **This is the thing.** (hold it) **The word is "pencil." Word or thing?** (pause, signal) → *Thing.*
> Retest both in step 4.

### 1d. Say it exactly

**Third rule. Some sentences in this subject have to be said in exactly the same words every time, like a prayer. When I say "say it with me," we say the words together, exactly. Try one. Say it with me: The hand is a tool.** → *The hand is a tool.*

**Again.** → *The hand is a tool.*

**Good. That was practice. The real one is coming in one minute.**

## 2. NEW MATERIAL (12 min) — three short cycles

### Concept A — What logic is, and why we need it

**MODEL.**

**An art is a way of doing something well. Cooking is an art. Building is an art.**

**Today we start logic. Listen. Logic is the art of reasoning well.**

**Again. Logic is the art of reasoning well.**

**Here is why we need it.** (Hold up your open hand, then the tool.) **Look at my hand. My hand can do almost anything. It can grab, it can push, it can touch. If I had to, I could dig a hole with my hand. Could I dig a hole with my hand?** (pause, signal) → *Yes.*

**Would I dig it well?** (pause, signal) → *No.*

**So what did people make?** (Hold up the tool.) **A shovel. The hand is good at almost everything and great at almost nothing. So the hand makes tools, and then the hand works well.**

**The mind is like the hand. By itself the mind thinks about ordinary things pretty well. You do not need logic to know that stealing is wrong, or to learn to fix a bicycle. But about the hardest questions — questions about the soul, and about God — the mind by itself rarely thinks well. So the mind makes tools for itself. It makes exact words. It puts words together into statements. It puts statements together into arguments. Those are the mind's tools. Logic is the study of how to make those tools well.**

**Is the shovel a tool of the hand?** (pause, signal) → *Yes.*

**Is a statement a tool of the mind?** (pause, signal) → *Yes.*

(Put the tool out of reach now. It has done its work.)

**LEAD.**

**Say it with me. Logic is the art of reasoning well.** (signal) → *Logic is the art of reasoning well.*

**Again.** (signal) → *Logic is the art of reasoning well.*

**Again.** (signal) → *Logic is the art of reasoning well.*

**TEST.**

**Everybody. What is logic?** (pause, signal) → *Logic is the art of reasoning well.*

**(9). What is logic?** → *Logic is the art of reasoning well.*

**(12). What is logic?** → *Logic is the art of reasoning well.*

**(9). The hand makes a shovel. What does the mind make?** → *Words, statements, and arguments.*

**(12). Why does the mind need tools?** → any of: *because by itself it doesn't reason well about the hardest things / because the mind is like the hand / so it can work well.* (Accept any of these. This is not a canonical wording and is not required back word for word.)

> **CORRECTION — student gives a partial or reworded answer ("thinking well", "the art of reasoning", "being smart").**
> **My turn. Logic is the art of reasoning well.** (Say it once more.) **Your turn. Say it with me. Logic is the art of reasoning well.** → together. **Alone. What is logic?** → *Logic is the art of reasoning well.* **Now every word is right.**
> Retest in step 4.

### Concept B — What logic is about

(Write on the board, well spaced, and leave it up for the rest of the lesson:)

```
WORDS  →  THOUGHTS  →  THINGS
```

**MODEL.**

**Every art is about something. Music is about sounds. What is music about?** (pause, signal) → *Sounds.*

**Arithmetic is about numbers. What is arithmetic about?** (pause, signal) → *Numbers.*

**Logic is about three things in a row.** (Point along the board.) **I say the word "apple."** (point to WORDS) **The word puts a thought of an apple in your mind.** (point to THOUGHTS) **And your thought is a likeness of the real apple.** (point to THINGS)

**Words signify thoughts. Thoughts are likenesses of things. "Signify" means "stand for."**

**Logic is not about ink, and not about noise. Here is the sentence. Logic is about words insofar as they signify things through our thoughts.**

**LEAD — in three parts, then whole.**

**Say it with me in parts. Logic is about words** (signal) → *Logic is about words.*

**insofar as they signify things** (signal) → *insofar as they signify things.*

**through our thoughts.** (signal) → *through our thoughts.*

**Now the whole thing. Say it with me. Logic is about words insofar as they signify things through our thoughts.** (signal) → repeated.

**Again.** (signal) → repeated.

**TEST.**

**Everybody. What do words stand for?** (pause, signal) → *Thoughts.*

**Everybody. What are thoughts likenesses of?** (pause, signal) → *Things.*

**Everybody. What is logic about?** (pause, signal) → *Logic is about words insofar as they signify things through our thoughts.*

**(12). What is logic about?** → *Logic is about words insofar as they signify things through our thoughts.*

**(9). What is logic about?** → *Logic is about words insofar as they signify things through our thoughts.*

**I say "bread." Point to the board. The word goes where?** (pause, signal) → *Words.*
**Then the thought. Where?** (pause, signal) → *Thoughts.*
**Then the thing. Where?** (pause, signal) → *Things.*

> **CORRECTION — student drops words ("Logic is about words and things").**
> **The whole wording. Logic is about words insofar as they signify things through our thoughts.** (Chunk it again in three parts if needed.) **What is logic about?** (pause, signal) → full sentence. Do not accept a shortened form.
>
> **CORRECTION — student reverses the order (says "things, thoughts, words").**
> **Stop. Look at the board. Which one is first?** → *Words.* **My turn: words, thoughts, things. Your turn.** → *Words, thoughts, things.* **Again.** → *Words, thoughts, things.* **Words come first because words are the ones we can hear.**
> Retest in step 4.

### Concept C — The three acts of the mind

**MODEL.**

**Now listen while I read something old and hard. You will not understand all of it today. Listen for the word "three."**

(Read aloud, straight through, at an even pace. Read it exactly as printed and do not stop at the three dots — they mark words we are skipping.)

> **There is a twofold operation of the intellect... one is the understanding of simple objects, that is, the operation by which the intellect apprehends just the essence of a thing alone; the other is the operation of composing and dividing. There is also a third operation, that of reasoning, by which reason proceeds from what is known to the investigation of things that are unknown.**

**How many operations of the mind did that say there are?** (pause, signal) → *Three.*

**We will call them the three acts of the mind. Here they are in short words.**

(Write and leave up, beneath the WORDS → THOUGHTS → THINGS line:)

```
ONE   — grasping what a thing is
TWO   — putting thoughts together to say true or false
THREE — reasoning from what we know to what we don't yet know
```

**Act one — grasping what a thing is.**
**Act two — putting thoughts together to say true or false.**
**Act three — reasoning from what we know to what we don't yet know.**

**Watch me sort three things.**

**My turn. "Dog." That is just one thing grasped. Nothing said about it yet. Act one.**

**My turn. "Dogs are animals." Two thoughts put together, and it can be true or false. Act two.**

**My turn. "All animals move themselves, so dogs move themselves." We started with what we know and got to something else. Act three.**

**LEAD.**

**Say them with me. Act one — grasping what a thing is.** (signal) → repeated.
**Act two — putting thoughts together to say true or false.** (signal) → repeated.
**Act three — reasoning from what we know to what we don't yet know.** (signal) → repeated.

**Now the short way. When I say an act, you say the number. Grasping what a thing is.** (pause, signal) → *One.*
**Putting thoughts together to say true or false.** (pause, signal) → *Two.*
**Reasoning from what we know to what we don't yet know.** (pause, signal) → *Three.*

**TEST — the sorting sequence.**

**I will say something. You say ONE, TWO, or THREE. Wait for the tap.**

(Ask each item, pause two beats, signal. Ask them in this order — the order matters. Board list stays visible.)

## 9. DRAFTER'S FLAGS

1. **"Reasoning well" heard as "reading well."** A 9-year-old still working on reading will hear "the art of reading well." *Fix applied:* the model says the sentence twice before any lead, and the hand-and-shovel teaching sits between the model and the lead so the sentence is heard three separate times in context. If he still says "reading," say **"Not reading. Reasoning. Reasoning is thinking one thing from another,"** then re-lead.

2. **"Art" is used inside the first canonical sentence and is not a child's word.** *Fix applied:* Concept A opens with **"An art is a way of doing something well"** and two concrete instances, before the canonical is said. The gloss is never tested; it only has to be heard.

3. **The review used to be dead weight.** Cow / snow / how-many-legs taught the signal and nothing else, and its number answers collided with ONE / TWO / THREE later in the same lesson. *Fix applied:* the whole review now runs on **word vs. thing**, which trains the procedures and plants Concept B at the same time, and contains no number answers at all.

4. **"Logic is about words insofar as they signify things through our thoughts" is a heavy sentence for a 9-year-old.** Fourteen words, one of them a preposition he has never used. *Fix applied:* it is led in three chunks, then whole, then asked individually. Do not accept a shortened form in test — chunk it again instead.

5. **The Aquinas passage sounds like something to memorize.** It is not, in this lesson — only the short forms are drilled. *Fix applied:* the read-aloud is framed with **"You will not understand all of it today. Listen for the word 'three,'"** which gives him one job he can do, and the only question asked of the passage is how many operations it names.

6. **The ellipsis in the canonical passage.** Read straight through "intellect... one is" without pausing or saying "dot dot dot." A pause there invites **(12)** to ask what is missing, mid-passage. *Fix applied:* stage direction added at the read-aloud. If she asks afterward, the missing words are "as Aristotle says in his book On the Soul" — say so and move on.

7. **"NONE" invites guessing by tone.** Children detect a question by the teacher's rising voice before they detect it by grammar. *Fix applied:* the stage direction at item 11 tells you to say the negatives *with the same voice as the positives*, and the correction procedure makes the student state the reason ("it asks"), not just the verdict. Watch this specifically with **(9)**.

8. **A one-word command will be called ONE.** "Sit!" is one word, and act one is about one thing grasped. *Fix applied:* a dedicated correction box, and every command in the sequence sits next to a minimally different act-one or act-two item.

9. **"So" as a false rule for act three.** Items 3, 7, and 10 all contain "so," which makes act three trivially detectable and will fail the moment a lesson gives an argument without it. *Fix applied:* the correction box names "so" as a *clue for today only* and explicitly instructs you not to present it as a rule. Spare items keep the same shape deliberately — the pattern is broken on purpose in Unit 1, not here.

10. **Individual turns numbered "9" and "12."** Read aloud as written, these sound like the numbers nine and twelve, which collide with ONE, TWO, THREE. *Fix applied:* the conventions note says to substitute the child's real name; the script never prints a bare number as a teacher utterance.

11. **"My cousin is not tall" (item 9).** A negative statement is still act two. A child who has just learned "putting thoughts together" may hear "not" as "no thoughts put together." *Fix applied:* the item is placed after three easy act-two positives, so the pattern is established before the negative form appears. If he says NONE, say **"It still tells us something is true or false. Act two,"** and move on — do not open the topic of negation, which arrives in its own lesson.

12. **The real tool on the table is a distraction risk.** *Fix applied:* the script has you pick it up at one specific moment and a stage direction puts it out of reach immediately after. If **(9)** starts handling it, that is a signal to move on, not to compete.

13. **Three new concepts in one lesson.** Each cycle is short; the long work is the Concept C sort, which is examples, not a fourth idea. If time runs long, cut the last two guided items — never the Concept C test.

## 2. NEW MATERIAL (11–12 min)

### 2a. The story (narrate, checking as you go)

(Tell the story in the words printed — they are short on purpose. Do not add jokes or extra characters.)

**Long ago in Greece there was a man named Socrates. He was famous for asking questions. A young rich man named Meno came to him with a question of his own.**

**Meno asked: can virtue be taught? Virtue means being good — being brave, being honest, being just. Meno wanted to know if you can be taught to be good, the way you are taught to read.**

**Everybody. What did Meno want to know?** (pause, signal) → *Whether virtue can be taught.*

**Socrates said something surprising. He said: I cannot tell you whether virtue can be taught, because I do not know what virtue is. And he said this: when I do not know the "what" of anything, how can I know its properties?**

**Listen to what that means. If I do not know what a hammer is, can I tell you whether a hammer is heavy?** (pause, signal) → *No.*

**If I do not know who your grandmother is, can I tell you whether she is kind?** (pause, signal) → *No.*

**So Socrates says: first the "what," then everything else.**

**(12). Why couldn't Socrates say whether virtue can be taught?** → *Because he didn't know what virtue is.*

**So Socrates asked Meno: tell me what virtue is. And Meno answered. Listen carefully to his answer. Meno said: a man's virtue is to run the city well and help his friends. A woman's virtue is to keep her house well. A child has another virtue, and an old man another, and a slave another. There are virtues without number.**

**Everybody. Did Meno give one answer or many?** (pause, signal) → *Many.*

**Socrates said: how lucky I am, Meno — I asked you for one virtue, and you have given me a swarm of them.**

**A swarm is what?** (pause, signal) → *A lot of bees.*

**Then Socrates made his best move of the day. He said: suppose I ask you, what is a bee? And you tell me there are many kinds of bees. I would say — but do bees differ as bees? Some bees are big and some are small. Some are prettier. But is any bee more of a bee than another bee?** (pause, signal) → *No.*

**Meno agreed. He said bees do not differ from one another as bees. And Socrates said: then there is one nature by which all bees are bees. Tell me that. And in the same way, however many virtues there are, they all have one common nature that makes them virtues — and the man who answers "What is virtue?" must fix his eye on that.**

**Everybody. Do bees differ as bees?** (pause, signal) → *No.*

**(9). Is a big bee more of a bee than a small bee?** → *No.*

**(12). What did Socrates want Meno to name — the many virtues, or the one nature in all of them?** → *The one nature.*

### 2c. NEW CONCEPT — the One-Over-Many check

**MODEL.**

**Here is the rule we take from this, and we will use it all year. An answer to "What is X?" must be one answer that fits every X.**

**One answer. Fits every one.**

(Write and leave up:)

```
ANSWER = one answer that fits every X
SWARM  = a list of X's
```

## 9. DRAFTER'S FLAGS

1. **"Swarm" as a bee-fact rather than a logic-word.** A 9-year-old may spend the lesson thinking about bees. *Fix applied:* the swarm is introduced first as Socrates's *joke about Meno's answer* ("I asked for one, you gave me a pile"), and the bee comparison comes after, as Socrates's *move*, not as the topic. Also, ANSWER/SWARM items 1–8 use no bees at all — the word is being detached from insects deliberately. If he drifts, say **"swarm means a pile of examples,"** and continue.

2. **SWARM over-called as "bad answer."** This is the predictable failure of the lesson: children learn SWARM as a scolding word and apply it to any answer they think is wrong. *Fix applied:* the model states outright that a weak one-liner is still an ANSWER *before* any item is asked; items 11 and 12 are weak-but-single answers placed inside the test sequence; an explicit **(12)** exchange establishes that a wrong answer is still an answer; a dedicated correction box handles it; and the mastery note names it as the thing most likely to need re-running.

3. **A swarm can be sorted on surface form if the swarms are bare lists.** If the answers are sentences ("A bird is an animal with feathers") and the swarms are bare noun-strings ("Robins, crows, and ducks"), the child learns *sentence = answer, list of nouns = swarm* and never attends to the content. *Fix applied:* every reply in the test sequence is a complete sentence of the same shape, swarms included — **"A bird is a robin, a crow, and a duck."** The only difference is what the sentence does.

4. **A list of properties is not a swarm, and is ambiguous.** "A bird has wings, feathers, and a beak" is one subject with several marks — it is closer to an answer than a swarm, and it will muddy the discrimination. *Fix applied:* no property-list appears anywhere in the sequence or the spares. Every swarm is a list of *instances or kinds* of the thing. For the same reason, part-lists were removed: *"A family is a mother, a father, and children"* names the parts of one family, not a pile of families, and is not usable here.

5. **"Virtue" is an unfamiliar word.** *Fix applied:* it is glossed in the narration the first time it appears — **"Virtue means being good — being brave, being honest, being just"** — with three concrete instances, and it is never used as a test item on its own until item 13, by which time it has been heard several times. Do not define virtue further; that is not this lesson's business.

6. **Meno's answer sexes the virtues ("a man's virtue is to run the city, a woman's to keep her house").** With a 12-year-old girl in the room this can pull the lesson into a discussion of whether Meno was right. *Fix applied:* the narration reports it flatly and Socrates immediately rejects it as an answer — the script's own verdict on Meno's list is that it is not an answer at all. If she raises it, say **"Socrates didn't accept it either — but not because it was unfair. Because it was a swarm,"** and move on. If she wants the larger question, take it outside the lesson.

7. **The question "Did Meno give one answer or many?" can be answered by counting.** *Fix applied:* it is asked immediately after the list is read, so "many" is the obvious and intended response; the harder work is loaded onto **(12)**'s question about *the one nature*, which cannot be answered by counting.

8. **"Do bees differ as bees?" is grammatically strange to a child.** The construction "differ *as* bees" is doing real work and is easy to hear as "do bees differ, as bees do?" *Fix applied:* the question is never asked cold — it is preceded by **"Some bees are big and some are small... is any bee more of a bee than another bee?"**, which is the same question in child-grammar, and **(9)**'s individual turn uses that easier form. Only **(12)** gets the "as bees" form as a test.

9. **The teacher's voice will give away the swarms.** Lists have a natural falling cadence on the third item. *Fix applied:* stage direction before the test table tells you to say question and reply in one breath, in a flat voice. Practice items 1 and 2 aloud once before teaching; they should sound identical in tone.

10. **The Meno retell could balloon.** *Fix applied:* three numbered sentences on the board; any extra plot (the slave boy, recollection) is cut with **"Not yet."** The slave boy is a two-minute preview in Lesson 3 and gets its full treatment in Lesson 28.

11. **Two canonical sentences arrive in the same minute.** "An answer to 'What is X?' must be one answer that fits every X" and "That is a swarm, not an answer" are both required exactly. *Fix applied:* they are led separately, and only the second is required as a *student utterance* during the test — the first is required only in the lead, in guided practice, and once in the delayed test. Do not test both in the same item.

12. **The lesson ends pointing at tomorrow.** "Wrong answers are still answers. We test those tomorrow" is not a throwaway — it is the hinge into Lesson 3, and it is what stops SWARM from silently absorbing the job of TOO WIDE. Say it.

## 2. NEW MATERIAL (12 min)

### 2a. The two Rules

**MODEL.**

**Yesterday we asked: is this one answer, or a swarm? Today we test the answer — we find out whether it is a good one.**

**A good answer to "What is X?" has a name. It is called a definition. A definition is one answer that says what a thing is. And a definition has two jobs. Here they are.**

(Write on the board as you say them. Leave them up for the rest of the lesson.)

```
RULE 1 — A definition must include every instance of the thing defined.
RULE 2 — A definition must exclude everything that is not the thing defined.
```

**"Instance" means one of them. One dog is an instance of dog. One shoe is an instance of shoe. Rule one says: leave nobody out. Rule two says: let nobody in.**

**Everybody. Rule one says leave nobody —** (pause, signal) → *Out.*

**Everybody. Rule two says let nobody —** (pause, signal) → *In.*

**Watch me test three definitions.**

**My turn. "A dog is an animal." Now I hunt. I look for something that is an animal but is not a dog. A cat. A cat is an animal, and a cat is not a dog. So this definition let somebody in. It broke rule two. It is TOO WIDE.**

**My turn. "A shoe is a leather covering for the foot." Now I hunt the other way. I look for a shoe this leaves out. A rubber boot. A rubber boot is a shoe, and it is not leather. So this definition left somebody out. It broke rule one. It is TOO NARROW.**

**My turn. "A triangle is a shape with three straight sides." I hunt for a triangle it leaves out. I can't find one. I hunt for something with three straight sides that is not a triangle. I can't find one. Nobody left out, nobody let in. It is GOOD.**

**The thing I hunt for has a name. It is called a counterexample. The cat was a counterexample. The rubber boot was a counterexample.**

**LEAD.**

**Say it with me. Rule one: A definition must include every instance of the thing defined.** (signal) → repeated.

**Say it with me. Rule two: A definition must exclude everything that is not the thing defined.** (signal) → repeated.

**Again, both.** (signal) → both repeated.

**Now the short way. Too wide means somebody got —** (pause, signal) → *In.*
**Too narrow means somebody was left —** (pause, signal) → *Out.*
**Left out is which rule?** (pause, signal) → *Rule one.*
**Let in is which rule?** (pause, signal) → *Rule two.*

**TEST — how to answer.**

**From now on I say a definition. You say TOO NARROW, TOO WIDE, or GOOD. Then I say "Name it," and you name the counterexample — the one left out, or the one let in. If it is GOOD there is nobody to name, and I will not ask.**

### 2c. Preview — the slave boy (about 2 min; narrate, do not drill)

**One more thing happened in the Meno, and I am only going to tell it to you. We are not drilling it today, and we come back to it much later.**

**Meno asked Socrates: how can you even look for something if you don't know what it is? Socrates said: I will show you. He called over a boy who worked in Meno's house — a boy who had never studied geometry. Socrates drew a square in the sand. He asked the boy a question. Then another. Then another. He never told the boy an answer. He only asked, in order. And by the end, the boy had worked out something about squares that he had not known when he sat down.**

(Say the next two checks in your teaching voice, not your storytelling voice.)

**Everybody. Did Socrates tell the boy the answer?** (pause, signal) → *No.*

**Everybody. Did the boy start from something he already knew?** (pause, signal) → *Yes.*

**That is reasoning. That is act three. Reasoning goes from what we know to what we don't yet know.**

**Everybody. Say it with me. Reasoning goes from what we know to what we don't yet know.** (signal) → repeated.

**(9). The slave boy went from what he knew to what he didn't know. Which act is that?** → *Three.*

**We will come back to the boy in the sand much later.**

(If they miss the act, say: **Act three — reasoning from what we know to what we don't yet know,** and stop. Do not turn this into a second new concept.)

## 5. DEEPER (2–3 min, for **(12)**)

**Meno asked Socrates a hard question, and it is famous. It goes like this: how can you look for something if you don't know what it is? Because if you don't know it at all, you won't know where to look — and if you do find it, you won't know that it's the thing you were looking for.**

**Say the problem back to me in your own words.** → any version of *you can't search for what you know nothing about.*

**That is called Meno's paradox. Now — Socrates did not answer it with a proof. He answered it with the boy in the sand. His reply was practical. It was: inquire anyway, in order.**

**(12). Say that line.** → *Inquire anyway, in order.*

**Why "in order"?** → *Because you start from what you do know and go one step at a time.*

**Right. You never search in the dark. You search from the edge of what you already have. That is what "from what we know to what we don't yet know" means, and it is why we started this year with what a word is before we touched an argument.**

**One last one. We hunted counterexamples today. Did we know the counterexample before we started hunting?** → *No.* **But did we know what we were hunting for?** → *Yes — something that fits the definition but isn't the thing. / Something left out.*

**That is Meno's paradox answered in one lesson: you don't know the answer, but you know the shape of it.**

## 9. DRAFTER'S FLAGS

1. **A verdict without a counterexample looks like mastery and is not.** Three verdicts means a one-in-three guess rate, and a child who guesses will look fluent for a whole lesson. *Fix applied:* **Name it** is built into the response protocol from the first test, a dedicated correction box handles the empty verdict ("no name, no verdict"), the delayed test demands verdict + counterexample + rule in one utterance, and the mastery note makes the counterexample — not the verdict — the criterion.

2. **GOOD items get no "Name it," which is itself a giveaway.** If you only ever ask for a name after a wrong verdict, the pattern teaches the student to listen to your follow-up rather than to the definition. *Fix applied:* the protocol announces the rule up front — **"If it is GOOD there is nobody to name, and I will not ask"** — so the follow-up carries no information the student hasn't already committed to. Ask for the verdict on the signal, and never before.

3. **TOO NARROW and TOO WIDE will swap.** *Fix applied:* the short forms **"leave nobody out"** and **"let nobody in"** are drilled as choral completions before any item is asked, they stay on the board, and the reversal correction rebuilds from the board rather than from the long rule sentences.

4. **"Include" and "exclude" sound alike to a distractible listener.** Both rules are long sentences differing mainly in one prefix. *Fix applied:* the full canonical sentences are required in the lead, twice in guided practice, and once each in the delayed test — but no *verdict* is ever driven off the long forms. Every drill and correction runs on the short forms.

5. **"Instance" is a new word doing load-bearing work in rule one.** *Fix applied:* it is glossed immediately and concretely — **"'Instance' means one of them. One dog is an instance of dog"** — before the rule is led, and it never appears in a test item where the student has to produce it.

6. **"A dog is a black animal" breaks both rules, not just rule one.** It is TOO NARROW (leaves out white dogs), and it also lets in black cats and crows, so it breaks rule two as well. A sharp 12-year-old will see this. *Fix applied:* the item is placed at #5, *after* the shoe triple has established the narrow/wide contrast on clean cases — it is not used as the opening model. If **(12)** says "but a black cat is a black animal," say: **"You found a second counterexample. This one breaks both rules. Well hunted."** Do not correct her; she is right. Item 16 then lands as confirmation rather than a surprise.

7. **The narrowing device must vary.** If every TOO NARROW item is a wrong colour, the child learns "colour word means too narrow" and never hunts. *Fix applied:* the sequence narrows by material (leather, wooden), colour (black), and age (older) — and the spares narrow by material, place, purpose, and setting as well.

8. **SWARM and the new test will bleed into each other.** *Fix applied:* guided practice deliberately puts a list-item and a one-line definition of the same subject next to each other (*What is a dog? — A dog is a collie, a beagle, and a lab* against *A dog is an animal that barks*), a correction box names which job is on the table, and the mastery note has a branch for it.

9. **"A dog is an animal that barks" was an ANSWER yesterday and is TOO NARROW today.** That is not a contradiction and it is the single most useful item in the lesson, because it shows the two checks are different jobs. *Fix applied:* a stage direction gives you the exact line to say if a student objects, and differentiated expectations — one fault for **(9)**, both for **(12)**.

10. **The both-faults item is (12) only.** *A bird is a flying animal* sits at the end of the test, labelled "(12) alone," so **(9)** is not asked for two faults at once on day one of T6.

11. **Accepted counterexamples are any true instance or outsider, not one official animal.** A 9-year-old who says "brown dogs" instead of "white dogs" is correct. *Fix applied:* the conventions note states the acceptance rule for the whole unit, and each arrow prints one sample so the teacher is not left guessing.

12. **"A cat is a small animal with fur" (guided practice) can be argued.** A child may say a rabbit is not an animal with fur "like a cat." *Fix applied:* accept *rabbit* or *mouse*; if the student disputes it, say **"Does a rabbit have fur? Is a rabbit small? Then the definition let him in,"** and move on. Do not litigate biology. The same applies to *a boat is a thing that floats* → *a duck*.

13. **The slave-boy narration is not drilled, but ends with a canonical line.** A child who has just been told "we are not drilling this" may switch off and miss the one thing that is required. *Fix applied:* the two choral checks come *inside* the narration, the required sentence is led separately and given its own individual turn for **(9)**, and a stage direction tells you to say the checks in your teaching voice, not your storytelling voice.

14. **This lesson has three tracks running at once and can overrun 30 minutes.** *Fix applied:* section 1 is written long enough to be cut — if you are behind, drop the last four review items, not the counterexample work. Section 2c may be moved to the top of Lesson 4 if needed; it is a preview and nothing in Unit 0 depends on it. The one thing that must not be shortened is the **Name it** protocol.

# APPENDIX — Editor's notes on this revision

**What this file is.** A single merged Unit 0, built from two independent drafts of the same three lessons: the Unit 0 draft (file 03) and the standalone scripts (files 08, 09, 10). The Unit 0 draft is the spine throughout. The standalone scripts contributed structure, board work, and several drafting insights.

**Global decisions.**

| Question | Decision | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Negative response word | **NONE** (03) | The alternative, "NOT A STATEMENT ABOUT WHAT IS," is eight words. Choral unison fails on it and you cannot distinguish a right answer from a mumble. The honesty problem it solved is handled instead by the explicit deferral: *"We will come back to questions and commands much later and find out what they really are."* |
| Act labels | **ONE / TWO / THREE** (03) | Kept short and parallel with ANSWER/SWARM and GOOD/WIDE/NARROW. Safe only because the number-answer warm-up was removed — see next row. |
| Lesson 1 review content | **Word vs. thing** (08) | The 03 review taught the signal on trivia (*cow, snow, how many legs*) that did no other work — and whose number answers collided with ONE/TWO/THREE later in the same lesson. The 08 material trains the same procedures and plants Concept B. |
| "The mind makes tools so it can work well" as a fourth canonical | **Cut** (against 08/09) | Four required wordings on day one is too many, and 03's own model gives the truer reason (the mind fails on questions of the soul and of God). It survives as a comprehension question with accepted paraphrases. |
| Board work | **Kept and extended** (08/09/10) | 03 boarded only *words → thoughts → things*. All three act names, the ANSWER/SWARM box, and the two Rules now go up and stay up. This is the single largest usability gain for the 9-year-old. |
| Chunked lead on the long canonical | **Adopted** (08) | *"Logic is about words insofar as they signify things through our thoughts"* is fourteen words. Led in thirds, then whole. |
| "An art is a way of doing something well" | **Adopted** (08) | 03 demanded *"Logic is the art of reasoning well"* verbatim without ever explaining *art*. |

**Lesson 2 — what came from where.** The narration is 03's: Socrates famous for asking questions, the actual joke (*"how lucky I am, Meno — I asked you for one virtue, and you have given me a swarm"*), and the reconstructed bee exchange, which 09 compressed to four flat sentences. 09 contributed the three numbered story sentences on the board and the stem-scaffolding, demoted here to a correction for **(9)** rather than the target — 03 is right that the child should produce whole sentences.

The test sequence is 03's, with one important repair borrowed from 09's *reasoning* but not its practice: **every swarm is now a full sentence** (*"A bird is a robin, a crow, and a duck"*), matching the form of the answers. 09's swarms were bare noun-strings (*"Robins, crows, and ducks"*), which lets a child sort correctly on surface form — sentence versus list — without ever attending to what the reply does. 09's flag 4 saw the danger in a related case (property-lists) and excluded them; that exclusion is adopted, and extended: **part-lists were also removed**, so 09's *"What is a family? — Mom, dad, and the kids"* is gone. That names the parts of one family, not a pile of families, and is not a swarm.

Two weak-but-single answers (items 11, 12) sit inside the sequence, and 03's **(12)** exchange — *"Is it still an answer?" → "Wrong answers are still answers. We test those tomorrow"* — is kept as the hinge into Lesson 3. 03's deeper section wins outright: *act two has to wait on act one* is the shape of the whole dialogue, and 09 does not have it.

**Lesson 3 — what came from where.** 03 supplied the item architecture (the shoe, chair, and knife triples, where only a word changes), the gloss on *instance*, the short forms *leave nobody out / let nobody in*, the announced **Name it** protocol including the "I will not ask on GOOD items" fix, and three of the six correction boxes — most importantly *"no name, no verdict."*

Two defects in 10 were repaired rather than imported. First, 10 opens its model with *"A dog is a black animal"* — but that definition also lets in black cats, so it breaks both rules, and it is a poor first case. 03 caught this in its own flags and placed the item fifth, after clean cases; that placement is kept, along with 03's response if **(12)** spots it (*"Well hunted"*). Second, five of 10's TOO NARROW items narrowed by colour (*black covering, red seat, red tool, red shape, black animal*), which teaches "colour word means too narrow." The merged sequence varies the narrowing device across material, colour, and age, and the spares vary it further.

From 10: the requirement that delayed tests take the fused form (*"too wide — a cat — rule two"*), the correction for a swarm offered where a test was asked, the acceptance rule for counterexamples (any true instance, not the printed one — promoted to the unit conventions), and the best cross-lesson item in either draft — **"a dog is an animal that barks,"** which was an ANSWER in Lesson 2 and is TOO NARROW in Lesson 3. That one item does more to separate the two checks than a paragraph of explanation, and it now carries a stage direction for the objection it will provoke. 10's spare list had an unfinished entry (the book item, with drafting notes left in the text); it was rewritten.

03's slave-boy narration and its Meno's-paradox deeper section were both kept over 10's much shorter versions, on the grounds that *"you don't know the answer, but you know the shape of it"* is the payoff of the whole unit and ties the counterexample hunt back to the paradox.

**Net changes in load.** Lesson 1 gains a concept gloss and loses a dead review block — about even, and better sequenced. Lesson 2 loses one canonical (the fourth from 08/09) and gains two board anchors. Lesson 3 gains two correction boxes and one item, and loses nothing. All three still run 25–30 minutes.
