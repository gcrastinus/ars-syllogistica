## LESSON 1 — What Logic Is

**Objective.** Students can say what logic is, what it is about, why we need it, and name the three acts of the mind in order, with one example of each.

**New concepts.** (1) Logic is the art of reasoning well. (2) Logic is about words insofar as they signify things through our thoughts. (3) The three acts of the mind.

**Canonical wordings.**
- Logic is the art of reasoning well.
- There is a twofold operation of the intellect... one is the understanding of simple objects, that is, the operation by which the intellect apprehends just the essence of a thing alone; the other is the operation of composing and dividing. There is also a third operation, that of reasoning, by which reason proceeds from what is known to the investigation of things that are unknown. (Simplify in speech to: Act one — grasping what a thing is. Act two — putting thoughts together to say true or false. Act three — reasoning from what we know to what we don't yet know.)
- Logic is about words insofar as they signify things through our thoughts.

**Doctrinal material for the script (use freely, no citation needed).** The mind is like the hand: the hand is a universal tool that can do almost anything but few things well, so it makes tools — knife, shovel — to work well; the mind likewise makes tools for itself — precise words, statements, arguments — and logic studies how to make these tools well. Words signify thoughts, thoughts are likenesses of things: the triple order words–thoughts–things.

**Example sequences.** Sort utterances into the three acts: "dog" (act 1) / "dogs are animals" (act 2) / "all animals move themselves, so dogs move themselves" (act 3). At least 6 sorted items varying wildly (single words, statements, little arguments); negatives: a question ("Is the dog outside?") and a command ("Sit!") — neither is any act's finished product yet; hold these for L17 payoff, today just "not a statement about what is."

**Tracks started.** T1 vocabulary.

**Deeper (12yo).** Greek *logos* = word/reason; why one word covers both.

**Mastery note.** Both can answer: What is logic? What are the three acts, in order? Which act is "dog"? Which is "dogs bark"?

## LESSON 2 — The Meno I: What Is Virtue?

**Objective.** Students can retell the opening of the Meno in three sentences and state the first Meno lesson: an answer to "What is X?" must be one answer that fits every X.

**Format note.** This is a review-and-draw-out lesson, not memorization of the dialogue. Script a short narrated retelling with frequent choral checks, then drill the drawn lesson DI-style.

**Doctrinal material.** Meno asks whether virtue can be taught; Socrates answers that he cannot know whether virtue can be taught because he does not know what virtue is — when I do not know the "what" of anything, how can I know its properties? Meno offers a swarm: a man's virtue, a woman's virtue, a child's — one for every age and condition. Socrates's bee move: bees differ in size and beauty, but do bees differ as bees? No — there is one nature by which all bees are bees; likewise there must be one nature by which all virtues are virtue, and the answer must fix its eye on that.

**New concept drilled.** The One-Over-Many check: when someone answers "What is X?" with a list of X's, say "That is a swarm, not an answer."

**Example sequences.** Teacher gives answers to "What is a bird?"-type questions; students call ANSWER or SWARM. Positives (real answers, even bad ones — "a bird is an animal with feathers"); negatives minimally different (lists: "robins, crows, and ducks"). ≥6/4.

**Tracks reviewed.** T1 (three acts; what logic is).

**Deeper.** Whose "what is X" does act one of the mind seek? Connect: a definition answers act one's question.

**Mastery note.** Both can spot SWARM vs ANSWER instantly and say why Socrates wouldn't accept the swarm.

## LESSON 3 — The Meno II: Testing Answers

**Objective.** Students can apply the two Definition Rules by counterexample and state the preview: reasoning goes from known to unknown.

**New concepts.** The two Definition Rules, drawn from the Meno and used all program long:
- Rule 1: A definition must include every instance of the thing defined.
- Rule 2: A definition must exclude everything that is not the thing defined (show how that kind differs from other, similar things).
And the method: test a proposed definition by hunting for a counterexample — an instance left out (breaks Rule 1) or an outsider let in (breaks Rule 2).

**Doctrinal material.** In the Meno, Plato teaches that a definition must perform two tasks: include every instance of the thing defined, and show how that kind of thing is different from other, similar things. Also narrate briefly (2 minutes, no drill): Socrates and the slave boy — by questions in order, the boy moves from what he knows to what he did not know; label it "reasoning — act three — we will return to this" (it returns in L28).

**Example sequences.** Proposed definitions to judge TOO NARROW / TOO WIDE / GOOD: "A dog is a black animal" (narrow — leaves out white dogs); "A dog is an animal" (wide — lets in cats); "A triangle is a shape with three straight sides" (good). ≥8 items, child-familiar, including one that is both narrow and wide for the 12-year-old. Students must *name the counterexample*, not just the verdict.

**Tracks reviewed.** T1, plus SWARM/ANSWER from L2.

**Deeper.** Meno's paradox in one line (how can you look for what you don't know at all?) and Socrates's practical reply: inquire anyway, in order.

**Mastery note.** Given a bad definition, both students produce a counterexample and say which rule it breaks. This skill (T6) reviews in every remaining lesson of Units 0–1.
