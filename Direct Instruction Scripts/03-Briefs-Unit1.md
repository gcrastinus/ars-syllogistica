# Unit 1 Briefs — The First Act: Simple Apprehension (Lessons 4–15)

---

## LESSON 4 — Subject and Predicate; the Universal

**Objective.** Students can split any simple sentence into subject and predicate and sort terms into SINGULAR vs UNIVERSAL.

**Canonical wordings.**
- The subject is that of which something is affirmed or denied; the predicate is what is affirmed or denied of the subject.
- A universal is a word that can be predicated of more than one subject.

**Doctrinal material.** In "Fido is a dog," 'Fido' is subject, 'dog' predicate; 'dog' is said of Fido. In "Fido is not a dog," 'dog' is denied of Fido. Longer subjects/predicates work the same: "The dog which is running / is white with black spots." 'Dog' is a universal because "Spot is a dog" and "Fido is a dog" can both be true — one word, many subjects. 'White' likewise. 'Fido' and 'Socrates' are not universals: singular.

**Example sequences.** (a) Sentence-splitting: ≥8 sentences from short to long, including denials and verb-predicates ("Fido runs"). (b) SINGULAR/UNIVERSAL sort: dog, Socrates, white, Fido, animal, mama, this apple, apple, saint, St. Peter. Negatives minimally different: "Fido" vs "dog"; "this apple" vs "apple."

**App seed (T10).** One line: in our logic app, capital letters will stand for universal (general) terms and lower-case letters or proper names for singular terms.

**Tracks.** T1, T6 review. Start T2 (parsing).

**Deeper.** "Man is a species" — is 'man' here said of many men? First taste of second intention (do not name it).

**Mastery note.** Both split sentences and sort terms without hesitation.

## LESSON 5 — Genus and Species

**Objective.** Students can identify genus and species in a tree and say the canonical wording.

**Canonical wording.** A genus is a kind of thing that has other kinds underneath it.

**Doctrinal material.** Living things divide into plants and animals; animals into dogs, men, horses. 'Animal' signifies a kind with kinds under it — a genus, the genus of dog, man, horse. 'Living thing' is likewise a genus of plant and animal. A species is a kind under a genus. The same kind can be species of the genus above and genus of the kinds below (animal is species of living thing, genus of dog).

**Example sequences.** Build 3 trees on the board (living things; shapes; furniture). Choral: "Is X a genus of Y? A species of Y?" ≥10 items; negatives: individuals at the bottom (Fido is NOT a species of dog — Fido is not a kind), and siblings (dog is not genus of cat).

**Tracks.** T2, T6; start T3 (predicables sorting) and T4 (tree building).

**Deeper.** Highest and lowest: is there a top of every tree? (Plants the categories question answered in L10–12.)

**Mastery note.** Given a tree, both answer genus/species questions and reject the Fido trap.

## LESSON 6 — Difference, Property, Accident

**Objective.** Students can name all five predicables and sort predicates into the five.

**Doctrinal material.** There are five predicables: genus, species, difference, property, and accident. The difference is what marks a species off within its genus (rational marks man off within animal). A property is not the essence but flows from it and belongs to every member always (able to laugh, for man). An accident may come and go without the thing ceasing to be what it is (tan, sitting). Enrichment for the drafter: Porphyry's ordering; John of St. Thomas, Material Logic, on the predicables, for precise phrasings if wanted.

**Example sequences.** Sort predicate-of-'man' items: animal (genus), rational (difference), able-to-laugh (property), tan (accident), man-of-Socrates (species said of the individual). Then a second subject (dog or triangle) to show the sort generalizes. ≥12 items; minimally different pairs: rational vs able-to-laugh (essence-mark vs flows-from-essence); tan vs able-to-laugh (can be lost vs cannot).

**Tracks.** T2, T3, T4, T6.

**Deeper.** Why "featherless biped" fails as a difference for man (property/accident intruding into definition) — links to Definition Rules.

**Mastery note.** Five predicables named in order; sorting ≥90% accurate. If shaky, L7 re-teaches before the test.

## LESSON 8 — Division

**Objective.** Students can divide a whole into its kinds and judge divisions by the rules.

**New concepts.** Division = cutting a whole into its parts/kinds. Rules of good division (phrase for children; enrichment from Poinsot and John of St. Thomas on conditions of good division): (1) the parts together take in the whole; (2) the parts exclude one another; (3) each cut uses one basis at a time.

**Example sequences.** GOOD/BAD division sort with the rule named and the counterexample shown: "Animals: wild and tame" (good); "Animals: wild, tame, and dogs" (bad — dogs overlaps); "Shoes: leather shoes and red shoes" (bad — two bases); "Numbers: even and odd" (good). ≥8 items. Practice performing divisions on child-familiar wholes.

**App.** Begin **Division** set (this one gates the app's Second Act). Easy level.

**Tracks.** T3, T4, T6.

**Deeper.** Division of a body into organs vs division of a genus into species — two kinds of whole.

**Mastery note.** Both can name which rule a bad division breaks.

## LESSON 9 — Definition

**Objective.** Students can build genus-plus-difference definitions and judge definitions by all rules.

**Canonical wordings.**
- A definition must include every instance of the thing defined, and it must show how that kind of thing is different from other, similar things.
- Definition formula: nearest genus + difference.

**Doctrinal material.** The first act of the human mind is the act of simple apprehension — understanding what something is — and we really know what something is when we can give a good definition of it; as the Meno showed, that is difficult, and logic shows us how. The definition must be universal — apply to every instance. Rules to teach beyond the two Meno rules (enrichment, Poinsot): the definition must be clearer than the thing defined; no defining a thing by itself; prefer saying what it is to what it is not.

**Example sequences.** (a) Judge: TOO WIDE / TOO NARROW / CIRCULAR / DARK (obscure) / GOOD — extend the L3 sort with the two new fault-names. (b) Build: given tree + difference bank, students assemble definitions ("A triangle is a shape with three straight sides"). ≥10 judge items, ≥4 build items.

**App.** Begin **Definition** set (gates Act II). Easy.

**Tracks.** T3, T4, T6 (now folded into this lesson's core), T10.

**Deeper.** Define 'genus' itself using genus and difference — definition applied to logic's own terms.

**Mastery note.** Both build a correct definition from a tree unaided.

## LESSON 10 — Using Names; the Ante-Predicaments

**Objective.** Students can sort uses of names into UNIVOCAL / EQUIVOCAL / DENOMINATIVE and say why the categories list is needed.

**Doctrinal material.** A word names a thing when it points the mind to that thing; the more universal the word, the more things it names. Two things are named equivocally when they have the same name but different accounts (bank of a river / money bank); univocally when same name, same account (Fido and Spot are both 'dog'); denominatively when a thing is named from something it has (the brave man from bravery, the tan man from tan). Why categories: since the genus must often be defined, and its genus in turn, the logician wants the list of summa genera — highest kinds, above which there are no genera. This is what Aristotle does in the Categories.

**Example sequences.** Name-use sort, ≥9 items with child-obvious equivocals (bat, bark, ruler); minimally different: 'dog' said of Fido and Spot (univocal) vs 'bat' said of animal and club (equivocal).

**Tracks.** T2, T3, T9-seed (none yet), review Definition Rules.

**Deeper.** Analogy previewed in one line: some names are between univocal and equivocal ('healthy' food, 'healthy' man) — flag, don't teach.

**Mastery note.** Sort firm; both can say in one sentence why we need the categories.

## LESSON 11 — Substance

**Objective.** Students can pick out substances vs accidents and first vs second substance.

**Doctrinal material.** A substance is what exists in itself, not in another (this man, this horse); an accident exists in another (tan exists in the man). First substance: the individual (Socrates, Fido); second substance: its species and genera (man, animal) — said of the individual. Everything else is either said of a subject or present in a subject.

**Example sequences.** SUBSTANCE/ACCIDENT sort (≥10: horse, white, Socrates, sitting, tree, heat, this rock, speed...); then FIRST/SECOND substance sort. Negative minimally different: 'this white' vs 'this rock'.

**Tracks.** T3, T4, T5 starts (categories sorting), T10.

**Deeper.** Why no first substance can be a predicate — 'Socrates' is never said of another.

**Mastery note.** Both sorts firm; carry-over check from app Predicables set.

## LESSON 12 — The Nine Accidents

**Objective.** Given an accident-word, students can place it in the right category; all ten categories named in order.

**Doctrinal material.** The other nine categories, in Andres's own English names (Lesson 6): quantity (how much: six feet tall), quality (what sort: white, brave), relation (double, taller-than), place where (in the market), time when (yesterday), position (sitting, lying), outfit (shod, armed), action (cuts), and being acted upon (is-cut). Use these names as canonical; give the common scholastic names (passion, habit, etc.) only as Deeper synonyms. Teach by sorting concrete predicates of one subject (Socrates in the marketplace) before any list memorization; the ordered list is assembled at the end as a wrap-up chant.

**Example sequences.** ≥18 predicates of Socrates/of a dog sorted into the nine; minimal pairs: cuts (action) vs is-cut (being acted upon); six-feet (quantity) vs taller-than-Plato (relation); sitting (position) vs in-the-market (place where); shod (outfit) vs sandals-as-substance.

**Tracks.** T5, T3, definition build review.

**Deeper.** One page of Material Logic flavor: why exactly ten? (open question tone; no drilling).

**Mastery note.** Ten categories named in order; sorting ≥90%.

## LESSON 14 — The Post-Predicaments (light)

**Objective.** Oral familiarity only: the four ways of being opposed; prior; simultaneous.

**Doctrinal material.** (Andres Lesson 7.) Four opposites: correlatives (double/half), contraries (hot/cold), possession/privation (sight/blindness), affirmation/negation ("he sits"/"he does not sit") — the last is the doorway to the second act: point at it explicitly ("next unit lives here"). Prior in time vs prior in nature; simultaneous.

**Format.** No mastery drilling; sorting game + teacher narration. This lesson is deliberately light to buffer before the test.

**Deeper.** Contraries with and without a middle (black/white vs odd/even).
